# One or two rounds of ICA in TMS-EEG preprocessing?

## Short answer

Neither one nor two rounds is correct in every case. Two-pass ICA is a well-established design, used in TESA-style, TMSEEG, ARTIST and several published pipelines. It is not better by default. What matters is that **each ICA pass has one stated target**, and that nothing you need for your analysis is removed by component rejection.

A defensible default for most single-pulse TEP studies:

- **One main ICA pass** for ocular artifacts (blinks, eye movements) and non-stimulus-locked artifacts (persistent or background scalp EMG, electrode noise, and ECG if present).
- **Handle the large TMS-locked artifacts with methods other than ordinary ICA rejection.** These are the pulse, amplifier decay/recharge and TMS-evoked scalp muscle. Use pulse-window removal and interpolation, a second interpolation window if decay remains, SSP-SIR or projection for early muscle, SOUND for channel noise, trial rejection, or a change of target or coil.
- **Add a second ICA pass only if needed.** Use it when stereotyped non-sensory artifacts are still visible after the first pass and after filtering. Back it up with explicit before/after QC.

If you are reproducing a classic TESA-style two-pass protocol (for example to match an earlier study), that is reasonable. Do it knowing what the first pass removes and what that costs, as described below.

## What each round is meant to remove in the classic two-pass design

The classic order is roughly: epoch → baseline → remove and interpolate the pulse window → (downsample) → reject bad trials and channels → **ICA round 1** → band-pass/notch filter → **ICA round 2** → interpolate bad channels → average reference → final baseline.

| | Round 1 (before filtering) | Round 2 (after filtering) |
|---|---|---|
| **Target** | The largest TMS-locked artifacts: TMS-evoked scalp muscle (biphasic, peaks around 5–10 ms, can exceed 1 mV) and amplifier decay/recharge (smooth exponential drifts that are largest near the coil) | Everything else that is stereotyped: blinks, horizontal and vertical eye movements, persistent or background EMG, ECG, electrode and line-noise residue, and any residual decay |
| **Why before or after filtering** | These artifacts are huge, sharp transients. Filtering across them causes ringing and smearing that spreads the artifact into the TEP. They also dominate the variance, so they need to be removed before the smaller artifacts can be separated well. | Once the big transients are gone and the data are filtered, the smaller artifacts become separable and the decomposition is more stable |
| **Typical size** | Few components carrying very large variance | More components with smaller variance, judged mainly by topography, time course and spectrum |

The principle behind it, made explicit in the TMSEEG and TESA rationale: deal with the high-amplitude, highly variable artifacts first so they do not drive the decomposition. Then inspect again, because residual bad trials, channels and components only become visible after the first round.

## Why I don't make round 1 the default

The round-1 targets are exactly where ICA is weakest for TMS-EEG:

1. **Independence is violated.** TMS-evoked muscle, decay and the real TEP are all time-locked to the pulse and overlap in time and space. Components can be poorly separated in both directions:
   - **Overcleaning:** rejecting a "muscle" or "decay" component also removes genuine early cortical signal (N15/P30 and beyond).
   - **Undercleaning:** muscle survives the cleaning. After a two-pass FastICA pipeline, TMS-evoked muscle was still visible in the 5–15 ms window in about 62% of participants after prefrontal stimulation, about 35% after premotor and 0% after parietal stimulation.
2. **ICA-heavy pipelines tend to shrink TEP amplitudes overall**, including late peaks (N100/P180), not only the windows next to the artifact. The choice of decay-suppression method alone (FastICA vs. SOUND vs. model-based detrending) changes early TEP amplitude and topography.
3. **Sensory components are not artifacts to delete by default.** Auditory and somatosensory responses to the click and scalp sensation are stimulus-locked and overlap with the N100/P200. Reduce them through masking and foam spacing, and handle them with sham or control contrasts or justified subtraction. Removing them with ICA is not the right tool.

So the safer default uses each tool for what it handles best:

| Artifact | Preferred handling |
|---|---|
| Pulse | Remove and cubic-interpolate a short window (e.g. about −2 to 10–12 ms, set from your own data) |
| Decay/recharge | Inspect visually; use a second interpolation window, or SOUND or detrending if justified; record the excluded window |
| TMS-evoked scalp muscle | Coil and target adjustment at acquisition, SSP-SIR or projection, interpolation if the early window is not interpreted, trial rejection |
| Blinks, eye movements, persistent EMG, ECG | **ICA** (the main pass) |
| Residual non-sensory stereotyped artifacts after SOUND/SSP-SIR or filtering | **Optional second ICA** |
| Auditory/somatosensory responses | Masking plus control conditions or contrasts, not default ICA rejection |

In SOUND/SSP-SIR-style workflows the order is: pulse handling → bad trials/channels → **ICA for ocular and background artifacts** → SOUND → SSP-SIR → optional residual ICA → late filtering. In other words, the main ICA comes first and any second pass is a cleanup step, not a requirement.

## When two rounds make sense

- You use no SOUND/SSP-SIR and rely on ICA for decay or muscle reduction (classic TESA, TMSEEG or ARTIST style), and the early window is central to your question. In that case round 1 is doing real work. Pair it with the sensitivity checks below.
- Strong muscle-prone targets (DLPFC, lateral frontal or temporal sites) where large transients clearly dominate the first decomposition.
- You need to match a published pipeline for comparison across studies.

## When one round (or no ICA-based muscle removal) is preferable

- Medial or posterior targets with little muscle artifact. A single ocular/background pass is usually enough.
- You use SOUND and SSP-SIR for channel noise and early muscle.
- Low channel count or few trials. ICA becomes unstable; two decompositions double the risk.
- Your main measure is early-latency amplitude or topography, or you plan source estimates. Minimize stimulus-locked component rejection.

## Practical settings and safeguards for either option

- **Mark bad channels before ICA** and exclude them from the decomposition. Interpolate or repair them after ICA, because interpolating first makes channels dependent and reduces rank.
- **Remove and interpolate the pulse before any ICA or filtering.**
- **PCA compression:** about 25–30 components for a ~60-channel montage is common. Keep the retained dimensionality well below the number of trials, and report the rank.
- **ICA-training copy:** if you high-pass (for example 1 Hz) only to stabilize ICA, fit on a separate copy and apply the unmixing to the analysis data. Don't let a training filter silently become your TEP waveform.
- **Only concatenate conditions** (active/sham, sites) for a shared ICA when they share the same non-neural noise structure. Otherwise cleaning can differ between conditions.
- **Never reject a component on its label alone.** Check topography, single-trial time course, spectrum and the effect on the average.

## QC to report

1. Butterfly plots, topographies and GMFA at each stage (raw → after round 1 → after round 2), focused on 0–100 ms.
2. Number and type of rejected components per pass and per participant, plus retained rank and trial counts.
3. **Sensitivity check:** vary the number of rejected components by ±2 and confirm TEP peaks and topographies are stable.
4. **Comparison with minimally preprocessed data** in artifact-free windows. Large changes there mean overcleaning.
5. **Muscle subgroup check** for frontal or lateral targets: split participants by visible muscle in the *uncleaned* 5–15 ms window and compare cleaned TEPs and spatial correlations. If they differ, cleaning has not equalized the groups.
6. Active vs. sham/control component decisions, to make sure cleaning is not condition-specific.

## Interpretation caveats

- Whatever you choose, cleaned TEP amplitude is not a pure measure of cortical excitability. It reflects residual artifact, sensory input and the cleaning pipeline.
- Null results after heavy two-pass cleaning may come from suppression by preprocessing. Large early peaks in ICA-light pipelines may be residual artifact.
- Amplitude comparisons across studies (or sites or groups) with different ICA strategies are not straightforward. Across sites or groups, they also need sensory ratings and control conditions.

## Assumptions

Single-pulse TEP analysis, about 60 or more EEG channels, roughly 100 or more trials per condition, epoched data with a reliable pulse marker, and access to TESA/EEGLAB or an equivalent MNE-based implementation. Re-check the advice if any of these do not hold: low channel count, very few trials, repetitive TMS, or a focus on immediate (<10 ms) responses.
