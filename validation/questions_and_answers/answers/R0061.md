# Comparing the occipital and premotor P200: which test to use, and what it can show

## Short answer

Because the same participants were stimulated at both sites, the comparison is **within-subject**. That makes it a **paired test**:

- **One pre-defined measure per participant and site** (for example, mean amplitude in an a-priori ROI and time window): use a **paired t-test**. If the paired differences are clearly non-normal or the sample is small (roughly n < 15), use a **Wilcoxon signed-rank test** or a **paired permutation test**. Report the mean paired difference with its 95% CI and a paired effect size (d_z).
- **Exploring the full channel × time space without a strong prior**: use a **cluster-based permutation test** on the paired data (sign-flipping permutations; Maris & Oostenveld, 2007). Note that a significant cluster does not let you claim that the effect sits at a specific latency or electrode (Sassenhagen & Draschkow, 2019).
- **Trial-level data, unequal trial counts, or extra covariates** (intensity, coil–cortex distance, e-field): use a **linear mixed model**, for example `amplitude ~ site + covariates + (1 + site | participant)`.

**The main problem is not which test to pick.** A paired test will tell you whether the P200 differs between sites. It cannot tell you that the occipital cortex is "more responsive." With the P200 especially, a site difference is most likely to come from non-cortical and methodological factors. The rest of this answer explains why and what to do about it.

---

## 1. Why a larger P200 does not mean a more responsive cortex

### 1.1 The P200 is the component most affected by sensory input
The N100–P200 complex in TMS-EEG overlaps heavily with **auditory evoked potentials** (from the coil click) and **somatosensory evoked potentials** (from scalp and muscle stimulation). Studies using realistic sham and sensory-only control conditions show that much of the late TEP can be reproduced without effective cortical stimulation (e.g., Conde et al., 2019; Biabani et al., 2019; Rocchi et al., 2021; Gordon et al., 2021). These sensory inputs differ between occipital and premotor sites:

- **Somatosensory input**: the scalp over the occiput and the frontal scalp differ in innervation, muscle bulk (neck muscles vs. frontalis/temporalis), and how much twitch and discomfort the pulse causes.
- **Auditory input**: the coil sits at a different distance and angle relative to the ears, and noise masking may work differently at each site. Bone conduction also differs.
- **Visual input**: occipital stimulation can cause **phosphenes**, which add a visual evoked response that has nothing to do with "excitability" in the usual sense. Coil-induced eye blinks or saccades also differ by site.
- **Arousal and startle**: a stronger or more unpleasant sensation at one site raises the late, non-specific vertex potential.

So a P200 that is twice as large at the occipital site may simply mean that occipital stimulation was a bigger sensory event.

### 1.2 Stimulation dose is probably not matched
Equal %MSO, or intensities based on resting motor threshold, do not give equal stimulation at the two cortical targets. Coil–cortex distance, skull and CSF thickness, gyral geometry, and coil orientation relative to the sulci all differ between occipital and premotor cortex. Without matching the **induced electric field** (for example, with SimNIBS e-field modelling) or matching an **early local TEP amplitude** (the rt-TEP approach; Casarotto et al., 2022), any amplitude difference mixes "dose" with "responsiveness."

### 1.3 Scalp amplitude also depends on things other than cortical response
- **Source orientation and depth**: a radial vs. tangential generator, or a closer vs. deeper one, changes scalp amplitude for the same neural response.
- **Reference and ROI choice**: the occipital and premotor P200 are measured at different electrodes, with different distances to the reference and different local noise.
- **Preprocessing does not act equally on both sites**: frontal and premotor stimulation usually causes much larger muscle artefacts (temporalis/frontalis). It therefore tends to get more aggressive ICA or SSP–SIR cleaning, which can also remove or reduce later components. If the two datasets were cleaned differently (different numbers of components removed, different filters or interpolation), the amplitude ratio may partly reflect the pipeline.
- **Session and order effects**: cap re-positioning, impedance changes, fatigue, and habituation of the sensory response (the P200 habituates strongly) all matter if site order was not counterbalanced.

### 1.4 Watch for circularity
If the P200 window and electrodes were chosen *because* that is where the occipital response looked largest, testing that same window is double dipping (Kilner, 2013). The window and ROI must be defined independently: in advance, from the literature, from a collapsed/averaged localiser across both sites, or from an independent dataset.

## 2. A design and analysis that can support a claim about responsiveness

### Step 1: control for sensory contributions
- Ideally, collect a **realistic sham** at each site that reproduces the click and the scalp sensation (e.g., a spacer or sham coil plus electrical scalp stimulation). Then analyse a **Site (occipital/premotor) × Condition (active/sham) repeated-measures design**. The claim you want rests on the **interaction**, i.e., (active − sham) differs between sites. Equivalently, run a paired test on the per-participant difference waves.
- If sham data are not available, report noise masking, foam spacing, and per-site **perceptual ratings** (loudness, scalp sensation, discomfort, phosphenes). Use these ratings as covariates or check that they are matched. Be explicit that the late components cannot be interpreted as cortex-specific.

### Step 2: focus on components that reflect the cortex more directly
For "responsiveness," the more defensible measures are the **early, local** responses (roughly 0–50/80 ms) recorded near the stimulation site, after artefact removal:
- Local mean field power or peak-to-peak amplitude over electrodes near each target, in an early window.
- Global mean field power (GMFP) in early windows.
- Source-level measures (e.g., significant current density), which reduce some of the geometry and orientation confounds of scalp measures.
- If possible, a measure of complexity or spreading, such as PCI, rather than one late peak.

Rosanova et al. (2009) compared occipital, parietal, and premotor stimulation and focused on site-specific *natural frequencies* (occipital ~alpha, premotor ~beta/gamma), not late peak amplitudes. That is a more interpretable way to describe site differences.

### Step 3: match or model the dose
- Calibrate intensity per site with an e-field target or a matched early TEP amplitude.
- Or model it: include per-participant, per-site e-field magnitude at the target (and/or coil–scalp distance) as a covariate in a mixed model.

### Step 4: harmonise preprocessing
Apply the same pipeline and parameters to both sites. Report how many ICA/SSP components were removed per site and participant, how many trials were kept, and the residual noise level. Check that the number of removed components does not predict the amplitude difference.

### Step 5: the statistical model
A sensible primary analysis, with ROI and window fixed in advance:

```
amplitude ~ site * condition + efield + (1 + site + condition | participant)
```

Here `condition` = active vs. sham, fitted on trial-level or condition-average data. If you have only active data at a single pre-specified ROI and window, a **paired t-test (or Wilcoxon signed-rank)** on per-participant averages is appropriate. For whole-scalp spatiotemporal exploration, use a **paired cluster-based permutation test**. Correct for multiple comparisons if several components or windows are tested, and state which one is primary.

## 3. How to phrase the result

- What you can say from the current data with a paired test: *"The P200 was larger after occipital than after premotor stimulation (paired t-test / Wilcoxon, effect size, CI)."*
- What you should not say without the controls above: *"Occipital cortex is more responsive/excitable than premotor cortex."*
- What you could say with sham-controlled, dose-matched, early-latency analyses: *"After controlling for sensory co-stimulation and matching the induced electric field, early local TEPs were larger after occipital stimulation."* That is a claim about cortical response, with the remaining caveats about scalp geometry and source orientation.

## Assumptions
- Both sites were recorded in the same participants with the same EEG setup, and you have per-participant averaged TEPs (and preferably trial-level data).
- "P200" refers to a positive deflection around 180–250 ms, measured at a site-specific ROI.
- No sham or sensory-control condition was collected, and intensity was not matched by e-field. If either was done, the Site × Condition and covariate analyses above apply directly.
