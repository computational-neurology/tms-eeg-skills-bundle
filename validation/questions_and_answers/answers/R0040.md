# Fixed coil pose over an MRI-defined DLPFC target: is it the right approach?

## Short answer

**Not as stated.** Keeping the coil still *during the main recording blocks* is correct and worth doing. What is risky is fixing the pose **before** you have checked it in the EEG, on the basis that the target is "anatomically optimal." For TMS-EEG, three separate things have to be optimal, and neuronavigation only covers the first:

1. **Where** the coil is: the x/y/z target, which your navigation handles.
2. **How** the coil is oriented: handle angle (yaw), tilt and current direction. A fixed 45° does not handle this for DLPFC.
3. **What the EEG shows** at that pose: whether there is an interpretable early local TEP, and how large the muscle, sensory, pulse and decay artifacts are. Only online monitoring can tell you this.

DLPFC is the hardest of the commonly used non-motor sites on artifact and sensory measures. A pose chosen only from anatomy is the setup most likely to give you a large dataset you cannot interpret.

Recommended approach: **optimize first, then lock.** Use the navigated MRI target and an individualized orientation as the starting point. Run a short online TEP check and make small adjustments. Fix the pose only once pre-defined go/no-go criteria are met. Then hold it fixed, monitored by navigation, for all main blocks.

## Why "anatomically optimal + 45°" is not enough at DLPFC

### 1. 45° is a motor-cortex convention, not a DLPFC rule
- The 45° posterolateral-to-anteromedial handle angle comes from M1. Large-scale E-field modelling (Gomez-Tames et al., 2018; about 518,000 simulated coil placements in 18 head models) shows that **a common optimal coil orientation across subjects exists only at M1 and S1**.
- In frontal cortex, gyral folding varies so much between people that the optimal orientation is **subject-specific**. The best induced current runs roughly **perpendicular to the local sulcal wall / gyral crown**, and that direction differs from person to person at DLPFC.
- So a fixed 45° handle may induce a poorly oriented current in the target in a sizeable fraction of your participants, even with the coil centre exactly on the coordinate.
- "45°" is also ambiguous. Specify the reference (e.g., relative to the midsagittal line), the handle direction, and the induced current direction (e.g., posterior-to-anterior in the target). Without these the setup cannot be reproduced.

### 2. An exact scalp projection is not necessarily the optimal coil position
- The coil position that maximizes the E-field at a cortical target is on average about **5.5 mm away from the nearest scalp point**, and further where the cortex is deeper. "Coil exactly over the target" is a good starting position, not a proven optimum.

### 3. The MNI coordinate is a group coordinate
- MNI (-38, 44, 26) is a standard-space point. In each participant it lands on that person's own folding, which may be a gyral crown, a sulcal wall or a sulcal fundus. That changes the effective dose and the best orientation.
- Assumption: the coordinate is inverse-normalized into each participant's native T1 and shown on the individual cortical surface. Check this visually for every participant. If you only use a scalp-based or atlas-warped projection, individual variability is even larger.

### 4. Precise targeting does not guarantee usable TEPs
Navigation improves target precision and reporting, but **it does not remove the need for online artifact inspection**. At prefrontal sites (Biabani et al., 2024; n = 135, prefrontal vs premotor vs parietal):

| Measure | Prefrontal | Premotor | Parietal |
|---|---|---|---|
| Participants with residual muscle artifact after ICA | **62.1%** | 34.5% | 0% |
| Perceived click loudness | highest (despite lowest intensity) | mid | mid |
| Twitch / discomfort ratings | highest | mid | lowest |
| White-noise masking effectiveness | worst | good | best |
| N100/P200 amplitude | largest (partly sensory) | mid | smallest |

Planning consequences:
- You should expect about **two in three participants** to carry muscle contamination into the early window if nothing is done at acquisition. Offline cleaning (ICA, SSP-SIR, interpolation) does not reliably rescue this. Coil angle and position adjustment at acquisition is the main defence.
- At DLPFC, the most visible thing in a low-trial online average is often the **early muscle-artifact peak**. This is a bipolar, MEP-like deflection after the pulse artifact, beginning later than about 4 ms, focused over frontal, temporal, jaw or facial channels. It can be large **even when the participant feels no twitch and is comfortable**. A pose that "feels fine" can still produce an uninterpretable early window.
- If the early local response is weak while N100/P200 is large, suspect sensory contamination, a target/orientation mismatch, or ineffective stimulation. Do not read it as strong cortical engagement.

### 5. Intensity interacts with pose
A fixed percentage of RMT does not guarantee equivalent cortical activation at DLPFC. Changing orientation or position changes the effective E-field at the target. Coil pose and intensity should therefore be tuned together, not fixed independently.

## Recommended protocol: optimize, then lock

### Before the session (per participant)
1. **Bring the target into native space.** Warp MNI (-38, 44, 26) into the participant's T1 and check that it falls on the intended DLPFC cortex. Pre-define a small acceptable target zone, e.g. within about 5–10 mm along the same gyrus. Assumption: you do not have a functional constraint, otherwise use that ROI.
2. **Choose the starting orientation from an individual E-field model**, not a default 45°. For example, in SimNIBS, sweep coil yaw around the target (e.g., 24 orientations at 15° steps, as in Balderston et al., 2020). Pick the yaw that maximizes the induced field in the target ROI, or the field component perpendicular to the local sulcal wall. Sampling the field across a small ROI is more stable than sampling at a single point. If you use a system with real-time E-field navigation (e.g., Nexstim-type), you can do this during the session.
3. **Pre-register the adjustment rule**: which parameters may change (yaw within about ±30–45° of the modelled optimum, position within the target zone, intensity within safety limits), what the stopping criterion is, and the maximum setup time.
4. **Identify a backup target**, e.g. a slightly more medial or posterior DLPFC site, in case the primary site stays dominated by muscle artifact.

### At the start of the session (setup phase, before main blocks)
5. Place the coil at the navigated target with the modelled orientation. Use your intensity rule (RMT only as a starting point). Assumptions: single-pulse TMS-EEG with a figure-of-8 coil, and the endpoint involves early TEPs and/or GMFP.
6. Deliver **10–30 pulses** and inspect **single trials first**, then the online average:
   - pulse, recharge and decay artifacts
   - the early bipolar muscle-artifact peak (about 4–15 ms, lateral frontal and temporal channels)
   - early local TEP near the target channels (first about 50 ms)
   - late N100/P200 relative to the early response
7. **Adjust in small steps**: rotate the coil (e.g., 10–15° at a time), shift position by a few mm within the target zone, and adjust intensity if needed. Keep the change that **shrinks the early muscle peak** and gives a clearer early local response without increasing intensity.
8. **Re-tune masking and noise level after each change** in pose or intensity, because click perception and scalp sensation change with coil geometry.
9. **Go/no-go**: stop optimizing and lock the pose when:
   - pulse and decay artifacts are manageable
   - the early muscle-artifact peak does not dominate the planned analysis window
   - an early local response is visible, if early TEPs are an endpoint or QC criterion
   - masking is acceptable and the participant tolerates stimulation
   - otherwise, move to the backup target.

   Do not overfit to a noisy 10–30 trial preview. Look for clear qualitative improvement, not small amplitude gains.

### During the main recording
10. **Now keep the pose fixed.** This is the point where your plan is correct. Use navigation to keep the coil on the locked pose, not on the original anatomical default.
11. Monitor for drift. Pre-specify tolerances, e.g. about 2–3 mm displacement and about 5° angular deviation (assumed values; adapt to your system's accuracy). Re-align between blocks, or pause when they are exceeded. Do not change the pose during a block except to restore the locked position.
12. Log the coil pose per pulse or per block if your system allows. Also keep monitoring impedance, artifacts and comfort between blocks.
13. **Collect sensory ratings after every block** on four separate 0–10 scales: click loudness, scalp muscle twitch, discomfort and pain. These are essential if you compare conditions, sessions or groups. At DLPFC, N100/P200 differences are expected from sensation alone.

## What to report
- Target definition: MNI coordinate, how it was transformed to native space, and the final native coordinates.
- Planned and final coil pose: position, handle angle with reference, tilt, and induced current direction. Give the distance and angle from the anatomical default to the locked pose.
- E-field modelling method and the criterion used to pick the orientation.
- Intensity rule (RMT-based, E-field-based, TEP-titrated) and final intensity.
- The online optimization procedure, number of setup pulses, go/no-go criteria, and how many participants needed adjustment or a backup target.
- Navigation tolerances, deviations during recording, and any re-alignments.
- Masking settings, foam/spacer, and sensory ratings.

---

## Session checklist (condensed)

- [ ] Target warped to native space and visually checked on the cortical surface
- [ ] Starting orientation from individual E-field model (not default 45°); current direction defined
- [ ] Adjustment window and stopping rule pre-registered; backup target defined
- [ ] 10–30 pulse online check: single trials, then average
- [ ] Early muscle-artifact peak (>~4 ms, lateral frontal/temporal) minimized by rotation and position
- [ ] Early local TEP visible; N100/P200 not dominating relative to early response
- [ ] Masking re-tuned after final pose and intensity
- [ ] Go/no-go met, then pose locked and saved in navigation
- [ ] Drift tolerances enforced during blocks; pose logged
- [ ] Four sensory ratings collected after each block
- [ ] All deviations from the anatomical default documented

**Bottom line:** keep the coil fixed during data collection, but only after the pose has been individualized and checked against the online EEG. At DLPFC, an anatomically exact target with a default 45° handle is a reasonable starting point, but it should not be locked without that check.
