# Auditory masking for TMS-EEG: setup and verification

## Assumptions

You did not give the stimulator, coil, target or population, so I assume:

- a single-pulse TEP study in healthy, cooperative adults;
- one stimulator and one coil per session, with participants who can report what they hear;
- noise delivered through in-ear earphones;
- an EEG system that can show an online average during setup (an rt-TEP-style view).

If any of these do not hold, the changes are noted below. This matters most for prefrontal or lateral targets, and for participants who cannot report.

---

## Why bother, and why not just fix it offline

The TMS click produces an auditory evoked potential (AEP), mainly an N100/P200 complex at frontocentral sites. It overlaps the late TEP. Removing it offline with ICA or similar methods assumes two things. First, that the cortical response to the pulse and the response to the click are independent. Second, that they add linearly with no interaction. Neither assumption has been shown to hold. Masking avoids the problem at acquisition, so build it into the design rather than relying on preprocessing.

---

## Part 1: Setting it up

### Step 1: Record your own click

Generic white noise does not match your click. The click's spectrum differs across stimulators and coils, and on some devices across intensities.

- **Microphone:** omnidirectional, with no filtering, compression or distortion anywhere in the recording chain.
- **Position:** about 25 cm lateral to the coil.
- **Recording:** 96 kHz, 24-bit. Deliver a few pulses in a quiet room with little reverberation.
- **Editing:** cut the recording from the sharp transient onset until the signal returns to baseline.
- **Safety:** keep the microphone, stand and every other metal object out of the coil's magnetic field. Metal inside the field can be thrown violently.
- **When to re-record:** after any change of stimulator or coil, and after a large intensity change if your device's click changes with intensity.

### Step 2: Build a customised masker

Mix two components:

| Component | What it is |
|---|---|
| Click-based noise | Your recorded click repeated in a sequence. Each inter-click interval is a fixed latency plus a uniform random jitter, so the sequence has no regular rhythm. |
| White-based noise | Random white noise, resampled so you can shift energy toward higher or lower frequencies. |

Set the white-to-click ratio and the noise volume separately from the computer's system volume.

Recommended starting option: **broadband shuffling.** The click is mixed with about eleven resampled copies of itself (factors of roughly ×0.3 to ×2.0), which spreads energy around the click's real spectrum. This is the configuration that was validated. It also turns off separate frequency tuning, which makes per-participant tuning faster.

Optional extras:
- **Intensity shuffling:** each click is randomly attenuated.
- **Spatial shuffling:** a random inter-aural delay moves the apparent source. This option has **not been validated**, so treat it as experimental.

Save the parameter set, or export the noise as a .wav file, so you can reproduce the exact masker across sessions and report it.

**Tooling:**
- **TAAC** (MATLAB GUI; needs the Signal Processing and Communications toolboxes). This is the published and validated option. It records the click, builds the noise and lets you tune it live.
- **CAMpy-TMS** (Python). A MATLAB-free alternative that is still in development and has not been validated against TAAC. It shapes noise to match the click's spectrum but does not contain real click transients, so the two maskers are not interchangeable. Its volume is digital and not calibrated to dB SPL. Avoid its rhythmic click-train mode in single-pulse studies. A periodic train, especially at gamma rates, can drive an auditory steady-state response at frontocentral sites, which is the confound you are trying to remove. If you use it for a study, validate it against TAAC and verify with the EEG check below.

**Why customise:** in the validation study (n = 20), customised noise masked the click at a lower sound level than white noise in every participant. Under real TMS the median was about 83–84 dB versus 87 dB for white noise, roughly 4–6 dB lower. That corresponds to 61–83% less sound intensity. It also extended the permissible exposure time under NIOSH from about 5 h with white noise to about 10–13 h. These numbers apply only to that stimulator, coil and earphone combination. Do not use them as targets for your own setup.

### Step 3: Reduce bone conduction

Part of the click reaches the inner ear through the skull, from the coil vibrating against the head. Air-conducted noise cannot fully mask that. Place a thin foam layer or spacer between the coil and the cap, and record whether you used one. Bone conduction is also why real TMS needs more masking than sham (next step).

### Step 4: Titrate per participant, under real TMS

This is the step people most often get wrong.

- **Use real TMS, never sham.** Real stimulation needed about **6 dB more** masking than sham for the same noise (84.1 vs 78.3 dB for one customised noise). The extra comes from bone conduction through the vibrating coil. A masker tuned with a tilted coil or a sham coil will under-mask your real condition.
- Titrate at the **real intensity, target and coil orientation** you will use in the experiment.
- Deliver pulses at a jittered interval (for example 2000–2300 ms).
- Raise the volume step by step until the participant **cannot detect a click on 10 consecutive pulses**.
- Stay within a comfortable level. If the participant still hears clicks at the highest acceptable volume, stop raising it. Record that masking was incomplete and collect click-loudness ratings (see Part 2).
- **Re-tune whenever** intensity, coil, target, coil orientation or foam/spacer changes, and **separately for each stimulation site**. Masking performance depends on the site. In a large three-site study, white-noise masking failed to stop click perception in nearly all participants and worked worst at prefrontal sites. Prefrontal stimulation produced the loudest perceived click even at the lowest intensity, so a lower intensity does not mean less auditory input.

### Step 5: Hearing safety

- Measure the sound level at the ear if you can (A-weighted SPL with your actual earphones, ideally on an ear simulator or head-and-torso simulator). Then check the session length against NIOSH/OSHA exposure limits.
- If you cannot measure it, say so in your methods. Cap the volume conservatively, and do not treat a software volume setting as a dB value.
- Customised noise is partly a hearing-safety measure, because it masks at lower levels. This matters most in long or repeated sessions.

## Part 2: Checking that it worked

Use several checks in order of strength. Asking the participant is the weakest.

### Check 1 (primary): no N100/P200 in the EEG

The most reliable sign that masking worked is that the **N100/P200 AEP complex is absent** at frontocentral electrodes. In the reference demonstration, sham TMS with the noise off produced a clear N100 at about 96 ms and P200 at about 196 ms. With customised noise on, both disappeared and the participant reported hearing nothing. Note that this demonstration used one participant in the sham condition. It shows the method can work, not that it always does.

How to apply it:

1. **Click-only block (masking on).** Run a short block with the coil set up so the click is preserved but the cortex is not stimulated (for example tilted or sham). Look at the online average at Cz/FCz around 80–220 ms. There should be no N100/P200. This isolates the air-conducted auditory input. Do not titrate the masker in this block (see Step 4).
2. **Optional reference: the same block with masking off.** This shows what the AEP looks like in that participant on your system, so in step 1 you know what you are looking for.
3. **Real-TMS online average** (10–30 trials, before the main acquisition). On real TMS, late components are not purely auditory, so you cannot expect N100/P200 to vanish completely. They also contain somatosensory and possibly cortical activity. Instead, look at the overall pattern:
   - **Acceptable:** a clear, early, local response near the target, with no large, broadly frontocentral N100/P200 that dominates the trace.
   - **Warning sign:** a weak early local response together with a large frontocentral N100/P200. Suspect auditory or somatosensory contamination, or poor target engagement. Go back and check masking, foam, coil position and intensity.

Run this check in the same online QC loop you use to tune coil position and intensity, so masking is re-checked whenever you change those.

### Check 2: perceptual report under real TMS

- At the end of titration: no click detected on 10 consecutive real pulses.
- **After every block:** collect four separate 0–10 ratings for **click loudness, scalp muscle twitch, discomfort and pain.** Click loudness is the rating most strongly linked to late TEP amplitude. Twitch has a weaker effect, mainly late (>250 ms). Pain showed no effect, so do not use pain or a general discomfort score as a stand-in for contamination. Use the click-loudness ratings as a covariate. They are essential if you compare sites, conditions or groups, where N100/P200 differences are expected from loudness and twitch differences alone.

### Check 3: participants who cannot report

For patients with disorders of consciousness, infants and non-compliant participants, the EEG check is the **only** one available. Build the click-only and online-average checks into the protocol from the start.

### Limits of both checks

- Even perfect perceptual masking does not guarantee identical neural input. Real TMS and a perceptually matched realistic sham still produced different N45, N100 and P180 components despite active masking.
- Masking reduces auditory input but has not been shown to remove it in every setup. Pair it with a somatosensory control (foam, plus a sham or sensory-control condition if your interpretation depends on cortical specificity), and do not treat sham subtraction as controlling every confound.
- Do not read a missing masking effect at a site as low auditory contamination. At prefrontal sites, removing white-noise masking did not change late TEPs, because the masking was already failing.

## Go/no-go before the main acquisition

Proceed when all of these hold:

- [ ] The click was recorded from **this** stimulator and coil (re-recorded since any hardware change).
- [ ] The masker was titrated under **real TMS** at the final intensity, target and orientation, with no click detected on 10 consecutive pulses.
- [ ] The volume is within your safety cap, and the SPL is measured or the lack of a measurement is documented.
- [ ] The click-only block with masking on shows no frontocentral N100/P200.
- [ ] The real-TMS online average shows an interpretable early local response that a late frontocentral complex does not dominate.
- [ ] Foam/spacer is in place and documented.
- [ ] A rating sheet is ready for click, twitch, discomfort and pain after each block.

Pause and adjust if the participant still hears the click at the maximum acceptable volume, or if late N100/P200 dominates while the early response is weak. Do not plan to fix either problem in preprocessing.

---

## What to report

- Click recording: stimulator, coil, microphone, distance, sampling rate.
- Noise type and parameters: tool, click/white ratio, broadband or other shuffling, saved parameter file.
- Earphone model and foam/spacer.
- Titration criterion (for example 10 consecutive undetected pulses) and the condition used (real TMS, at which intensity and site).
- Final volume and SPL if measured, or a plain statement that SPL was not measured.
- Verification method (EEG N100/P200 check and perceptual ratings) and the results.
- What triggered re-tuning and how often it happened.
