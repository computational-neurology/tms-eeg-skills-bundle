# F3 TMS-EEG: fix the acquisition before choosing ICA settings

## Short answer

No ICA setting will rescue this dataset if you record it as it is. I'd strongly advise against collecting all 30 participants with the current setup. The pilot shows two problems that offline cleaning cannot reliably reverse:

1. **Saturated channels have lost information.** When an amplifier clips, the true signal is gone for as long as the channel stays at its rail. ICA is a linear unmixing method. It cannot rebuild samples that were never recorded. Clipped segments also break its linear-mixing assumption, so they tend to spread distortion into other components.
2. **Decay that dominates the first ~20 ms is mainly an acquisition problem.** It depends on electrode contact, lead and cable layout, and stimulation intensity. You can partly suppress it offline with ICA, fitting or detrending, SOUND-like methods or interpolation. Each of these is a modelling choice that can also remove or distort real early responses. Different decay-suppression methods give different early TEP amplitudes and *topographies* from the same data.

F3 is also a left DLPFC/frontal site, which is the worst common site for artifacts. In a large three-site comparison (n = 135), prefrontal stimulation left visible TMS-evoked muscle activity in the 5–15 ms window in about **62% of participants even after two-pass FastICA**. The comparable figures were about 35% for premotor and 0% for parietal sites. Prefrontal stimulation also produced the loudest perceived click and the highest twitch and discomfort ratings, and white-noise masking worked least well there. So a large decay, saturation and frontal muscle activity all fall in the same window. Planning to "remove it later with ICA" is the scenario most likely to produce an uninterpretable early TEP.

I'm assuming a 60-ish-channel TMS-compatible EEG system, a single-pulse protocol over F3/left DLPFC, and no fixed primary endpoint yet. If your endpoint is only a late component (N100/P200), some of this matters less, but the sensory-confound issues below still apply.

## What to do instead: a short fixing session (half a day to one day)

This costs far less lab time than collecting 30 datasets whose first 20–30 ms you would have to discard. Use one or two volunteers and work through these steps, watching single trials and 10–30-trial online averages after each change.

### 1. Eliminate saturation first (non-negotiable)
- Find out *which* channels saturate. Usually they are the ones under or right next to the coil, or ones with poor contact or a cable loop.
- Check the amplifier's input range, gain and DC/AC mode. Confirm you are using the TMS-compatible recording settings, such as sample-and-hold or a wide dynamic range, if your system has them.
- Re-prepare those electrodes: lower and stabilise impedance, check gel amount and look for gel bridges. Make sure the electrode is not being pressed or moved by the coil.
- Go/no-go: **zero channels saturating beyond the pulse artifact itself**, over a block of single trials.

### 2. Shorten the decay
- Route every lead roughly **perpendicular (about 90°) to the coil handle / induced-current direction**. Avoid wire loops and slack near the coil, keep cables free of tension, and photograph or document the routing.
- Check whether the decay sits in specific electrodes or along a particular cable path. If it does, it is a contact or lead problem you can fix.
- Test small changes one at a time: re-gel a channel, re-route one cable bundle, shift the coil slightly. Watch whether the decay shortens.
- Check its dependence on intensity. Decay duration and amplitude grow with stimulation intensity, and decay amplitude tracks pulse amplitude closely. If your intensity was set as a fixed %RMT, consider calibrating it from the online TEP and artifact profile instead. RMT does not guarantee equivalent cortical activation at a frontal site.
- Raising the sampling rate mainly shortens the *pulse* artifact. It does not by itself remove decay.
- Note that some decay can persist even with low impedance and a careful setup. The goal is "short and stable", not zero.

### 3. Tune coil orientation and position at F3 against the early muscle peak
- Don't default to 45°. Optimal orientation is subject-specific in frontal cortex. Start from anatomy or an E-field model if you have one, then rotate the coil and adjust its position in small steps.
- Judge each setting by whether the **early bipolar, MEP-like muscle-artifact peak (from about 4 ms, over frontal, temporal and jaw channels)** shrinks. Don't rely only on participant comfort. "No felt twitch" does not mean the early window is clean.
- If the anterior/lateral F3 position cannot be made usable, consider a slightly more medial or posterior backup target.

### 4. Sensory controls (these also can't be fixed with ICA)
- Tune auditory masking (noise matched to the click) and add a foam spacer. Re-tune both after any change to intensity, orientation or coil position.
- Collect click-loudness, scalp-sensation, twitch and discomfort ratings from every participant. A sham or sensory control condition is advisable if you will interpret N100/P200.

### 5. Define a written go/no-go rule before the first real participant
For example:
- no saturated channels;
- signal back to baseline within a pre-defined short window (decide it from the improved pilot, e.g. ≤ 10 ms);
- a manageable early muscle peak;
- masking confirmed.

Apply the same rule at the start of every session. Save screenshots and notes of the online average, coil coordinates and orientation, intensity, masking settings and cable layout for each participant.

### 6. During collection
Recheck the artifact profile whenever you change intensity or orientation. Pause if pulse, decay or muscle artifact suddenly changes. Record a short blink/eye-movement block for later ocular ICA.

## How ICA should actually be used (once acquisition is fixed)

With a clean acquisition, ICA has a narrow, appropriate role. A conservative plan:

| Step | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| Event handling | Epoch around the pulse. Inspect channel-wise traces from pre-pulse to at least 100 ms. |
| Pulse window | Remove and cubic-interpolate the pulse window, e.g. about −2 to 10–12 ms. Set it from your measured artifact duration, not a default. |
| Bad channels | Mark saturated or unstable channels as bad **before** ICA so they are not part of the decomposition. Repair them afterward with interpolation or SOUND. |
| Downsampling | Only after the pulse window is handled. Check for ringing at the interpolation edges. |
| ICA training copy | Fit on a separate copy high-passed at about 1 Hz. Apply the unmixing to the less-filtered analysis data, so the early high-pass doesn't silently become your TEP filter. |
| Dimensionality | Reduce rank with PCA to roughly 25–30 components for about 60 channels. Account for any rank lost through interpolation or average reference. |
| Algorithm | FastICA (or Infomax) with a fixed random seed. |
| What to remove | Blinks, eye movements, persistent electrode noise and non-time-locked background muscle. **Do not** reject components with clear TMS-locked activity or the early TMS-evoked muscle shape through routine review. Handle residual early muscle with SSP-SIR or projection, explicit window handling, or trial rejection. Do not remove auditory/sensory components by default; control them with masking and contrasts. |
| Residual decay | If a short residual decay remains, handle it with an explicit, reported method: a second interpolation window, fitting, or a dedicated first ICA pass on the decay. Run a sensitivity check against an alternative method, because the choice changes early amplitude and topography. |
| Sensitivity | Vary the number of rejected components by ±2 and confirm the TEP is stable. Compare cleaned results with minimally processed data in artifact-free windows. |
| Muscle QC | Split participants by visible muscle activity in the *uncleaned* 5–15 ms epochs, then compare cleaned TEPs and topographies between subgroups. If they diverge, the cleaning is not working. Pre-register this check. |
| Reporting | Report the removed and interpolated windows, the decay method, retained components and rank, retained trials, and which latency range you consider uninterpretable. |

## If you go ahead with the current setup anyway

Be explicit about what you give up:
- Treat at least the **first ~20–30 ms as lost**, and longer on any channel that saturated. Remove or interpolate that window and exclude saturated segments and channels; ICA cannot recover them. Early components such as N15/P30 and anything i-TEP-like are not interpretable, and early topographies at F3 are probably shaped by hardware or muscle.
- Expect residual muscle artifact in a majority of participants even after two-pass ICA. Expect ICA-based cleaning to reduce TEP amplitude overall, not only near the artifact window.
- Restrict the endpoints to later components, and interpret N100/P200 only alongside sensory ratings and a control condition. They are strongly driven by click loudness and scalp sensation at frontal sites.
- Budget for higher trial and participant exclusion.

**Bottom line:** spend the lab time on one targeted fixing session: eliminate saturation, shorten decay through electrode, lead and intensity work, tune coil orientation against the early muscle peak, and set masking. Then collect the 30 participants with a written go/no-go check. ICA is then a cleanup tool for ocular and residual artifacts, not a rescue plan.
