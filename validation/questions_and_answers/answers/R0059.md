# How long a pulse window to remove and interpolate, and how to choose it

## Short answer

No single window length is correct. It depends on your sampling rate, stimulation intensity, amplifier, lead routing, target (how much muscle you get) and which TEP latencies you plan to interpret. Practical guidance:

- **Typical published starting points:** remove from about **−2 ms to +10 ms**, or **−2 ms to +12 ms**, around the pulse, then interpolate (cubic in TESA-style pipelines). Some pipelines add a **second, shorter window later** (for example about −3 to +10 ms) to catch residual artifact after ICA, SOUND or SSP-SIR.
- **With a good setup it can be shorter.** With sample-and-hold amplifiers, recovery from the pulse usually takes about 2–5 ms. DC-coupled amplifiers with high sampling rates (≥5 kHz) and fine resolution are designed to resolve very early signals. At very high sampling rates (tens of kHz), the pulse artifact itself can be only about 2 ms wide.
- **It should rarely be much longer than about 10–15 ms.** If contamination lasts beyond that, it is usually decay or TMS-evoked muscle artifact, not the pulse itself. Interpolating over it erases real early signal and makes interpolation less reliable. Handle it with methods aimed at those artifacts instead (see step 4 below).
- **Pick the window from your own data, not from a paper.** Sampling frequency is the strongest predictor of pulse-artifact amplitude and duration. Stimulation intensity comes second. For example, recording at 20 kHz instead of 5 kHz gave a much larger but roughly 57% shorter artifact, and higher intensity lengthened it by about 30% (Jamil et al., 2024). A window copied from a study with different acquisition settings may be too short or needlessly long for yours.

Rule of thumb: **use the smallest window that reliably covers the pulse artifact in every channel, trial, condition and participant, plus a small safety margin, and end it well before the earliest latency you want to interpret.**

## How to decide the length: a step-by-step procedure

### 0. Before choosing, collect these settings
Sampling rate, amplifier type (sample-and-hold or DC-coupled), how stimulator and EEG are synchronized and how precise the trigger is, stimulation intensity for each condition, stimulator and pulse shape, target site, and how electrode leads were routed. These settings are what drive artifact length.

### 1. Look at the raw data first
- Use **unfiltered, non-downsampled** epochs aligned to the pulse, from about −20 to +100 ms.
- Plot **single trials** overlaid for every channel, a butterfly plot of all channels, and the trial average. Averages hide trials with long artifacts.
- A log-scale plot of absolute amplitude, or of the sample-to-sample difference, makes it easy to see where the signal comes back to a normal EEG range.
- Check the channels nearest the coil, and any channels with poor impedance or long lead loops. These usually set the worst case.

### 2. Set the start of the window (pre-pulse edge)
- Start early enough to cover trigger-to-pulse jitter and any artifact that begins slightly before the event marker. In most systems this means **1–2 ms before the marker**. If triggers are not hardware-synchronized or show jitter at the level of single samples, start earlier.
- Check that every pulse marker sits on the actual artifact onset. If they are off, fix the markers first. Widening the window does not fix bad markers.

### 3. Set the end of the window (post-pulse edge) with an objective rule
- For each channel and trial, find the **first time after the pulse when the signal stays within normal limits**. Normal can mean amplitude within k × the pre-stimulus SD (k ≈ 5–10 is a common heuristic, but set it yourself). It can also mean the derivative is back at pre-stimulus levels.
- Summarize **conservatively**: use a high percentile (such as the 95th–99th) across trials and channels, then the maximum across participants and conditions.
- **Add a margin of about 1–2 ms**, then round to a clean value.
- Use **the same window for all conditions and participants**. If intensities differ between conditions, the highest-intensity condition will usually set the window. Different windows between conditions can create spurious condition differences at early latencies.
- If possible, set the rule and window on pilot data or a held-out subset, and pre-register them, so the choice cannot be tuned to the results.

### 4. Tell apart the three things that last longer than the pulse
Don't simply extend the interpolation window to cover them.

| Contamination | How it looks | Usual duration | Better handling than a longer interpolation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pulse artifact (core) | Huge, very sharp transient, possibly clipping | Under 1 ms of real width, longer after amplifier filtering or recovery (a few ms) | Remove and interpolate. This is what the window is for. |
| Decay or recharge artifact | Smooth, exponential-looking drift. Same polarity across trials. Polarity depends on lead routing. Scales with pulse amplitude. | A few ms to tens of ms | Optimize lead routing during acquisition. Offline: ICA or decay fitting, or a second interpolation window after cleaning. Otherwise leave the contaminated interval out of the analysis. |
| TMS-evoked scalp muscle artifact | Large, high-frequency, biphasic or MEP-like burst over frontal, temporal or jaw/neck channels. Strongest for lateral targets such as anterolateral DLPFC. | Roughly 5–20+ ms | SSP-SIR or projection-based cleaning with careful QC, trial rejection, or changing coil position or orientation. Don't use ordinary ICA rejection for TMS-locked components. |

Also check for a **recharge artifact** from the stimulator's capacitor recharge, which can appear later than the main pulse. If present, it needs its own short window at its own latency.

### 5. Match the window to the latencies you will interpret
- **Only later TEPs (N45, N100, P180):** a generous window such as −2 to +12–15 ms costs little.
- **Early TEPs (N15, P30):** the window and its interpolation edges must end clearly before the analysis window starts. Many DLPFC studies remove −2 to 10 or −2 to 12 ms and then analyze early-local windows such as 20–60 or 30–60 ms.
- **Immediate TEPs (i-TEPs, under about 10 ms):** the window is part of your scientific claim. You need a very short, well-characterized artifact, which in practice means high sampling rates, synchronized acquisition and optimized lead routing. You also need evidence that residual pulse or decay artifact is not producing the response (step 7).

### 6. Choose the interpolation method and check its edges
- **Linear interpolation** is simple and robust for short windows. **Cubic interpolation** (as used in TESA-style pipelines) uses 1 ms or more of data on each side to fit a smooth curve. That makes it sensitive to whether the boundary samples are themselves contaminated. If the post-window edge still contains decay, the cubic fit will carry that artifact into the gap.
- The longer the window, the less the interpolated segment looks like real EEG, and the bigger the gap a later filter must bridge.
- Some pipelines keep the window blanked during ICA and interpolate afterward, so the interpolated segment does not distort the decomposition. Whichever order you use, **remove or interpolate before any filtering or downsampling.** Filtering across an unhandled pulse, or across a sharp edge at the window boundary, causes ringing that spreads through the whole epoch and cannot be undone.

### 7. Run a sensitivity analysis
- Re-run the pipeline with window ends shifted by about ±2–5 ms, for example ending at 8, 10, 12 and 15 ms.
- Check whether peak latencies, amplitudes and topographies of the components you care about stay stable. If an effect appears or disappears depending on the window end, it is at least partly caused by artifact or preprocessing.
- For early latencies, also check whether early peaks **reverse polarity when you reverse current direction or lead routing**. That pattern points to artifact, not brain response. Also compare with sham or sensory control conditions.

## Quick data-driven estimate (Python template)

This template assumes you have already loaded data as `raw`, detected pulse events as `events`, and are working on unfiltered data at the native sampling rate. All thresholds are placeholders to set and justify for your own dataset.

```python
import numpy as np
import mne

# Unfiltered, native-rate epochs around the pulse (no baseline correction yet)
epochs = mne.Epochs(raw, events, tmin=-0.050, tmax=0.100,
                    baseline=None, preload=True, picks="eeg")
X = epochs.get_data()                      # (n_trials, n_channels, n_times)
t = epochs.times * 1000.0                  # ms

pre = (t >= -40) & (t <= -5)               # clean pre-pulse reference (adjust)
mu = X[:, :, pre].mean(axis=2, keepdims=True)
sd = X[:, :, pre].std(axis=2, keepdims=True)
k = 8                                      # PLACEHOLDER threshold in SDs
hold_ms = 2.0                              # must stay "normal" for this long

z = np.abs(X - mu) / sd
post_idx = np.where(t > 0)[0]
hold = max(1, int(round(hold_ms / (t[1] - t[0]))))

def recovery_time(zz):
    ok = zz[post_idx] < k
    for i in range(len(post_idx) - hold):
        if ok[i:i + hold].all():
            return t[post_idx[i]]
    return np.nan

rec = np.array([[recovery_time(z[tr, ch]) for ch in range(X.shape[1])]
                for tr in range(X.shape[0])])          # ms, per trial x channel

print("median recovery (ms):", np.nanmedian(rec))
print("95th / 99th percentile (ms):", np.nanpercentile(rec, [95, 99]))
# Worst channels, to check lead routing and contact quality
worst = np.argsort(np.nanpercentile(rec, 95, axis=0))[::-1][:5]
print("slowest channels:", [epochs.ch_names[i] for i in worst])
```

Run this for each condition and participant. Use the largest 95th–99th percentile plus a 1–2 ms margin as the candidate window end. Then **check it visually**, because a threshold rule can be fooled by muscle bursts or decay that never quite returns to baseline. If recovery times run to tens of ms in particular channels, that is decay or muscle, so handle it as described in step 4 rather than by extending the window.

## QC and reporting checklist

- Overlay pre- and post-interpolation single-trial traces for all channels around the pulse. Look for jumps or kinks at the window edges.
- After filtering, check the interpolation edges again for ringing.
- Compare pulse residuals between active and sham/control conditions and between intensities.
- Report: sampling rate, amplifier and its recovery behaviour, synchronization method, stimulation intensity, lead routing, the exact window(s) in ms relative to the pulse, the interpolation method and how many edge samples it used, any second window, the rule used to choose the window, and the sensitivity analysis results.

## Assumptions and caveats

- This assumes a standard TEP study, with events recorded at the actual pulse onset and data at or above a few kHz. At lower sampling rates, artifact timing becomes coarse, so be more conservative.
- Offline interpolation cannot recover signal lost to amplifier saturation or long decay. If the artifact routinely lasts well beyond about 10 ms, the most effective fixes are at acquisition: lead routing, electrode impedance, coil orientation and amplifier settings.
- Whatever window you choose, TEP amplitudes near it reflect cortical response together with residual artifact, muscle activity, sensory input and preprocessing choices. Treat early-latency results as provisional until the checks above show they hold across windows and are absent in control conditions.
