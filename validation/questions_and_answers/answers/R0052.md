# Stimulation intensity for TMS-EEG over left DLPFC

## Short answer

I can't fully confirm it. Using 110% of resting motor threshold (RMT) for every participant is safe, defensible and easy to report. It is still not the best choice for a TMS-EEG study over DLPFC, and reviewers who know TMS-EEG are likely to question it. The rTMS convention does not carry over well, for three reasons:

1. **Motor threshold is a poor proxy for DLPFC response.** RMT shows how excitable the corticospinal system in M1 is, and how far the coil is from M1. It does not tell you whether a given intensity produces a clear, artifact-free cortical response at DLPFC. Scalp-to-cortex distance, gyral folding relative to the coil, and local excitability all differ between M1 and DLPFC, and they differ across people. The same %RMT can therefore give very different induced electric fields and very different TEP amplitudes at DLPFC.
2. **The TEP is the outcome, so it should guide the dose.** Current TMS-EEG recommendations (e.g., Hernandez-Pavon et al., 2023, *Brain Stimulation*) suggest choosing or checking intensity from the EEG response itself. In practice that means confirming a clear early TEP (roughly the first 50 ms, near the stimulation site) before recording, for example with real-time TEP monitoring (Casarotto et al., 2022, *J Neurosci Methods*). A common working criterion is an early local peak-to-peak amplitude of at least about 6–10 µV in the online average. If you use an RMT-based dose without such a check, some participants will produce low-SNR TEPs and others will produce large artifacts.
3. **Lateral prefrontal sites amplify the main TMS-EEG confounds.** Left DLPFC sits close to the temporalis and frontalis muscles. Higher intensities there produce large cranial-muscle artifacts in the first ~20–40 ms, and these can saturate or swamp the early TEP components (Mutanen et al., 2013). Higher intensities also raise the coil click and the scalp sensation, which drive auditory and somatosensory evoked potentials that can be mistaken for transcranial responses (Conde et al., 2019). A fixed 110% RMT ignores how strong these confounds are in each participant.

Two smaller points:

- **Clinical rTMS dosing has moved on.** The common DLPFC rTMS/iTBS protocols now use 120% RMT, and TBS research protocols often use 80% AMT. So "matching our rTMS protocol" may not match the clinical standard. More importantly, matching a dose is only useful if TMS-EEG is meant to probe the same induced field as the rTMS.
- **Safety is not the concern.** Single pulses at 110% RMT are well within published safety guidance (Rossi et al., 2021). The concern is data quality and how you interpret the results.

## What I would recommend

Pick the option that fits your scientific question.

**Option A (preferred for a stand-alone TMS-EEG study): TEP-guided intensity, anchored to RMT**
- Measure RMT as you do now, for reporting and as a starting point.
- At the neuronavigated DLPFC target, start near 100–110% RMT. Fine-tune coil angle and position to reduce muscle artifact. Then adjust intensity until the online early TEP meets your preregistered amplitude criterion, without saturation and with tolerable discomfort.
- Use noise masking and a foam spacer or thin layer, and record a sensory control (e.g., sham or a realistic sham condition).
- Report the final intensity both as %MSO and %RMT. If you have MRI-based models, also report the estimated E-field at the target in V/m.

**Option B (when TMS-EEG is a readout of your rTMS target engagement): keep 110% RMT, but justify it and control it**
- Keep the fixed %RMT dose so the probe matches the rTMS dose. State this rationale explicitly.
- Add an online TEP quality check, with predefined exclusion or adjustment rules for participants who show no clear early TEP or who show excessive muscle artifact.
- Consider correcting for scalp-to-cortex distance (Stokes et al., 2005) or using E-field-matched dosing (e.g., SimNIBS). This equalises the dose actually delivered to DLPFC rather than at M1.
- Treat intensity (%MSO, and E-field if available) as a covariate in your analyses.

Whichever you choose, define RMT precisely. One standard definition is the lowest intensity that evokes MEPs of at least 50 µV in at least 5 of 10 trials in relaxed FDI or APB (Rossini et al., 2015). An adaptive threshold-hunting procedure is an acceptable alternative.
