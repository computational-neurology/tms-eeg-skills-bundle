# Auditory masking in TMS-EEG: setup and verification

**Assumptions:** single-pulse (or paired-pulse) TMS with a figure-of-eight coil over a cortical target, TEPs as the main outcome, a standard EEG cap and a quiet lab. Adjust the details for your hardware.

## Why masking matters

The coil discharge makes a loud, broadband click. It is often above 100 dB SPL close to the coil, and its loudness grows with intensity. The sound reaches the cochlea in two ways: through the air and through bone, because the coil vibrates against the skull. The click evokes an auditory evoked potential (AEP), mainly an N100–P180/P200 complex that peaks at the vertex. This overlaps in time and space with the "late" TEP components, and it is not specific to the target site. Masking only deals with the auditory part of the peripheral response. Somatosensory input from the scalp needs separate handling (see the end).

## Setup

### 1. Characterise the click
- Record the click with a microphone placed roughly where the participant's ear will be. Use your actual coil, at the actual stimulator intensity, with the coil in its real position or orientation.
- Get the click's spectrum. Clicks differ between coils and stimulators, and they change with intensity, so do this for each coil and intensity range you plan to use.

### 2. Generate a masking noise that matches the click
- Plain white noise works poorly. It puts a lot of energy where the click has little, so you need higher levels to mask the click.
- Use **click-tailored noise** instead. Shape it to the click's spectrum, for example by randomly scrambling or overlapping short segments of the recorded click waveform. The time-varying, click-like texture makes the real click hard to pick out. Massimini et al. (2005) used this idea. The TAAC toolbox (Russo et al., 2022) automates it, including mixing in components for each intensity.
- Play the noise continuously through each block. Start it a few seconds before the first pulse, and use exactly the same noise in active, sham and control conditions.

### 3. Deliver it properly
- Use **insert earphones**, preferably the air-tube type (e.g., Etymotic-style). They keep transducer electronics away from the coil and electrodes, give good isolation and are MR- and TMS-friendly. Check they don't push on electrodes such as T7/T8 or TP9/TP10.
- If the setup allows, add passive protection on top: foam inserts, or ear defenders that don't get in the way of the coil. This lowers the level of noise you need.
- To reduce **bone conduction**, place a thin layer of foam or plastic (a few mm) between the coil and the cap. This adds distance to the scalp. Either determine the stimulation intensity with the spacer already in place, or adjust for it.

### 4. Titrate the noise level for each participant
- Seat the participant with the cap on and the coil at the real target, at the real intensity. Fire single pulses while you raise the noise in small steps (about 2–3 dB).
- At each step, ask whether they can still hear the click. Stop when they can't, or at their comfort limit, whichever comes first. A stricter option is a short forced-choice test: deliver trains of pulses and have the participant say which interval contained a click.
- Stay within safe exposure limits. A common target is to keep the noise at or below about 90 dB(A), with total exposure kept short. Write down the final level (dial setting and the calibrated dB SPL at the earpiece).
- Repeat the titration if you change the target, intensity, coil or coil orientation. Clicks at different sites can also sound different because of how the coil couples to the skull.
- If you can't mask the click at a tolerable level, lower the intensity if the design allows, improve the passive isolation or spacer, and record the incomplete masking as a limitation.

## Checking that it worked

### A. Behavioural checks (during the session)
- After each block, ask for ratings of **click perception** (e.g., 0 = not heard, 1 = uncertain, 2 = clearly heard) and, separately, **scalp sensation**. Analyse these ratings and report them.
- If clicks become audible again over the session (for example, because an earphone shifted or the participant adapted), stop and reseat the earphones or raise the level.

### B. Online EEG checks (real-time TEP monitoring)
- Average about 20–40 trials online, without re-referencing artefacts away. Look at the vertex/central channels (Cz, FCz) at about 80–250 ms.
- A large, symmetric N100–P200 at the vertex that looks the same whatever the target is a warning sign of residual auditory (and/or somatosensory) input.
- An adequately masked recording should show a clear early response near the target (<50–80 ms) that shows up from the first few averaged trials, and no prominent stereotyped vertex complex.
- Keep in mind that some targets, M1 in particular, give a genuine cortical N100. So judge the vertex complex mainly by comparing against controls (below), not just by whether it is present.

### C. Control conditions (the strongest test)
- **Auditory-only / sham-click control:** keep the masking on and fire the coil at the same intensity while stopping the field from reaching the brain. Options are a sham coil, or the real coil lifted or tilted away on a spacer so the click is preserved. Under good masking, this condition should produce essentially no N100–P200.
- **Optional positive control:** run a short block of the sham-click condition **without** masking. This shows the size of the AEP you are suppressing and confirms your pipeline can detect it. Keep the block short to limit noise exposure.
- **Multiple targets:** cortical TEPs vary by site, while sensory-evoked potentials mostly don't. Similar late waveforms and topographies across targets point to a peripheral contribution.

### D. Offline quantitative checks
- Measure N100–P200 peak-to-peak amplitude, or mean amplitude in pre-defined windows, at Cz/FCz. Compare active vs sham-click, and masked vs unmasked if you collected it.
- Use cluster-based permutation tests across space and time to compare active vs sham. To claim the sham response is "absent", use equivalence tests or Bayes factors. A non-significant result alone doesn't show absence.
- Correlate the active-TMS topography or waveform with the sham-click response (spatial or temporal correlation). High correlation in the late window suggests residual sensory contamination.
- Optionally check the time–frequency response for evoked theta/low-alpha at central sites in the sham condition.
- Check that the earphones and noise added no artefacts of their own, such as line-noise peaks, transducer pickup, or electrodes displaced near the ears.

## Caveats

- **Masking alone is often incomplete,** especially at high intensities and over lateral or frontal sites where bone conduction is strong. Plan for a sham/control condition from the start instead of trying to fix contamination during preprocessing.
- **Somatosensory input** (scalp muscle twitch, nerve stimulation, coil vibration) also produces a vertex N100–P200, and it is often the bigger confound. Address it with coil orientation and target choice, the foam spacer, and ideally a realistic sham that includes scalp electrical stimulation matched for sensation (cf. Conde et al., 2019; Rocchi et al., 2021).
- **Preprocessing** (ICA, SSP-SIR, regressing out sham responses) can reduce residual sensory potentials. It can also remove genuine cortical signal, so use it as a supplement to good masking, not a replacement.
- **Report everything:** noise type and how it was generated, delivery hardware, final level (dB SPL), passive protection, spacer, titration procedure, perception ratings, and control conditions. This follows current TMS-EEG reporting recommendations (e.g., Hernandez-Pavon et al., 2023).
