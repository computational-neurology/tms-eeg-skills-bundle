# Auditory masking for TMS-EEG: setup and verification

## Assumptions

You didn't give the target, stimulator, coil, population or protocol, so this answer assumes:

- single-pulse TMS-EEG in healthy adults who can report what they hear;
- one stimulator and coil combination per session;
- in-ear earphones are available;
- the main concern is keeping the auditory evoked potential (AEP, mostly the N100/P200 complex) out of the TEP.

If your target is prefrontal or lateral frontal, or your participants cannot report perception, see the site and population notes below. Those cases change what you can expect and how you verify.

## Why mask at all, instead of cleaning it later

The click produces an AEP that overlaps the late TEP. Removing it offline (for example with ICA) assumes two things:

- the cortical response to the pulse and the response to the click are independent;
- the two simply add together with no interaction.

Neither assumption is guaranteed. Masking stops the click from reaching perception in the first place, so you don't depend on those assumptions. Don't plan a session that relies on preprocessing to remove a clearly audible click.

## Part 1: Setting it up

### Step 1: Record your own click

The click spectrum differs between stimulators and coils, and sometimes between intensities. Generic white noise is therefore not a good masker for your particular click.

- **Microphone:** use an omnidirectional microphone with no filtering or distortion anywhere in the recording chain.
- **Placement:** about 25 cm lateral to the coil.
- **Recording settings:** 96 kHz, 24-bit.
- **Room:** quiet and low-reverberation. Deliver a few pulses at the intensity you plan to use.
- **Trimming:** cut the recording from the sharp transient until the signal returns to baseline.
- **Safety:** keep the microphone and every metal object outside the coil's magnetic field. Metal parts inside the field can be thrown violently.
- **When to re-record:** after any change of stimulator or coil, and after a large change in intensity.

### Step 2: Build a customised masking noise

Build a noise that mixes two components:

- **Click-based noise:** your recorded click, repeated at pseudo-random intervals (a fixed latency plus a random jitter).
- **White-based noise:** white noise that can be resampled to shift its balance between high and low frequencies.

Mix the two with an explicit white-to-click ratio, and set the noise volume separately from the system volume.

**Broadband shuffling** is the recommended default. It mixes the click with eleven resampled copies of itself (about ×0.3 to ×2.0), which spreads energy across the spectrum around the real click. It also reduces the number of parameters, so tuning each participant is quicker. This is the configuration that was validated.

Optional extras:

- **Intensity shuffling:** random attenuation of each click.
- **Spatial shuffling:** random inter-aural delay. This has not been tested, so treat it as unvalidated.

Save the parameter set, or export the noise as a .wav file, so the same masker can be reproduced across sessions and participants.

**Tools:**

- **TAAC (MATLAB GUI):** published and validated. It handles recording, building, live tuning and playback.
- **CAMpy-TMS (Python):** usable if you have no MATLAB licence, but it is uncalibrated and not yet validated against TAAC. It uses click-shaped noise (spectral matching), not real click transients, so it is not equivalent to TAAC.
  - Do not use its rhythmic click-train mode for single-pulse studies. A periodic train in the gamma range can drive an auditory steady-state response at frontocentral sites, which is exactly where you measure N100/P200.

**Why customise:** in the validation study (n = 20, one stimulator, coil and intensity), customised noise masked the click at a lower sound level than white noise in every participant. Under real TMS:

| Noise | Level needed to mask the click |
|---|---|
| Customised noise | about 83–84 dB SPL |
| White noise | about 87 dB SPL |
| Click-adapted noise | about 89 dB SPL |

That difference is a 61–83% reduction in sound intensity. These numbers are specific to that setup. Do not use them as targets for your stimulator, coil and earphones.

### Step 3: Delivery hardware

- Use in-ear earphones. They also block ambient sound.
- Check the full playback chain (sound card, amplifier, earphones) before the first participant. Start at low hardware volume.
- The software volume setting is not a calibrated dB SPL value. If you can, measure the actual SPL at the ear with your earphones, for example with an ear simulator or a head-and-torso simulator. If you cannot, say so in your report.

### Step 4: Titrate per participant under real TMS

This is the most common mistake: **titrate under real stimulation, never under sham.**

- In the validation study, real TMS needed about 6 dB more masking than sham with the same noise (e.g. 84.1 vs 78.3 dB).
- The reason is bone conduction. During real stimulation the coil vibrates against the scalp over a larger contact area.
- A level titrated with a tilted sham coil will therefore under-mask the real condition.

Procedure:

1. Seat the participant, place the coil at the real target, orientation and intensity, and fit the earphones.
2. Start the noise at a low, comfortable level.
3. Deliver single pulses at a jittered interval (about 2000–2300 ms was used in validation).
4. Raise the volume step by step until the participant **cannot detect the click on 10 consecutive pulses**.
5. If you are comparing noise types or conditions, randomise their order.
6. Lock the parameters and keep the noise playing continuously through the block.

**Re-tune whenever any of these change:**

- intensity
- coil
- target or site
- coil orientation
- foam or spacer under the coil

Tune per site, not once per participant.

**Bone conduction:** a thin foam layer between coil and scalp is commonly used to reduce coil vibration. If you use one:

- keep it the same across conditions;
- re-tune after adding or changing it;
- report it.

### Step 5: Hearing safety

The main safety benefit of customised noise is that it masks at lower levels. At the validation levels, the maximum permissible exposure was:

| Noise | NIOSH | OSHA |
|---|---|---|
| Customised | 10–13 h | 13–21 h |
| White noise | 5 h | 12 h |
| Click-adapted noise | 3 h | 9 h |

For long or repeated sessions:

- add up the total time spent under noise;
- set a comfort ceiling before the session;
- do not keep raising volume beyond it.

If the click is still audible at the ceiling, don't push louder. Record that masking was incomplete and plan for it in the analysis and interpretation.

## Part 2: Checking that it worked

Use three checks, from weakest to strongest.

### 1. Self-report (necessary but weak)

- The 10-consecutive-pulses criterion at titration.
- After **every block**, ask for four separate 0–10 ratings:
  - click loudness
  - scalp muscle twitch
  - discomfort
  - pain

Keep these as four separate scales, not one combined score. In the largest sensory-confound dataset, click loudness was the rating that best predicted larger late TEPs (N100–P200 window). Twitch had a weaker effect, mainly after 250 ms. Pain had no effect.

These ratings are essential if you will compare sites, conditions or groups. Differences in perceived click loudness alone can produce N100/P200 differences.

### 2. EEG criterion online (the most reliable check)

The best criterion is **the absence of the N100/P200 complex** in the EEG.

- In the original demonstration, with the noise off, a clear N100 (about 96 ms) and P200 (about 196 ms) appeared.
- With customised noise on, both disappeared and the participant reported hearing nothing.
- That demonstration was one participant under sham, so treat it as a proof of principle, not a guarantee.

How to use it during the session:

- **Use online TEP visualisation during setup.** Check masking in the same loop as coil position, orientation and intensity tuning, using small averages (about 10–30 trials). Inspect single trials before trusting the average.
- **Read the pattern:**
  - If the early local response (under 60 ms) is weak and a large frontocentral/vertex N100/P200 dominates, suspect sensory contamination, including under-masking.
  - Adjust the masking, re-check, and only then start the main acquisition.
- **Optional click-only or sham block:** run a short block with the coil tilted or spaced away so the click is preserved but the brain is not stimulated, with masking on. If an N100/P200 still appears, air-conducted masking is not enough.
  - Passing this block does **not** prove the real condition is masked, because sham lacks the extra bone-conducted sound.
- **Interpret a residual N100/P200 under real TMS carefully.** It can also come from somatosensory input (scalp sensation, muscle twitch) or from genuine cortical activity. Under real TMS it is not a pure auditory readout. Use it together with the click-loudness rating to decide whether masking is the problem.
- **Save evidence:** keep a screenshot or note of the online average together with the masking parameters for each setup.

### 3. For participants who cannot report

For example: disorders of consciousness, infants, or non-compliant patients. Here the EEG criterion is the **only** available check, so build the online check and a click-only control block into the protocol from the start.

## Site dependence: what to expect

Masking efficacy is not the same at every target.

- With white-noise masking, click perception was not prevented in nearly all participants across prefrontal, premotor and parietal targets.
- It was worst at prefrontal sites. Prefrontal stimulation gave the loudest perceived click even at the lowest stimulation intensity, so intensity does not tell you how loud the click will be.
- Late N100/P200 amplitude followed the same order as the click ratings: prefrontal > premotor > parietal.
- At prefrontal sites, removing masking did not change late responses. That reflects masking that was already failing, not low auditory contamination.

For frontal or lateral targets:

- use customised noise;
- titrate under real TMS;
- verify per site;
- expect to collect higher click-loudness ratings anyway.

---

## Limits to keep in mind

- **Perceptual masking is not neural equivalence.** A realistic sham that participants could not tell apart from real TMS, with active masking, still differed from real TMS in N45, N100 and P180. Masking reduces auditory input but has not been shown to abolish it in every setup.
- **Masking does not control somatosensory input.** Interpretation-sensitive designs also need a somatosensory control strategy, for example:
  - site-matched electrical scalp stimulation with a tilted coil;
  - a sensory-matched sham;

  plus a planned online check of that control.
- **Do not treat N100/P200 as purely auditory, or early TEPs as automatically clean.** Neither window is guaranteed to be free of sensory or muscle contamination.

## Session checklist

**Before the study**
- [ ] Record the click with your own stimulator and coil (96 kHz/24-bit, mic about 25 cm lateral, no metal in the field).
- [ ] Build a customised noise (click-based plus white-based, broadband shuffling) and save the parameters or .wav file.
- [ ] Check the earphone chain. Measure SPL at the ear if possible.
- [ ] Set a hearing-safety comfort ceiling and an exposure budget.
- [ ] Define a go/no-go rule. Example: no detectable click on 10 consecutive real pulses, plus no dominant frontocentral N100/P200 in a 10–30 trial online average where the early response is weak.

**Each participant and site**
- [ ] Coil at the real target, orientation and intensity, with the foam/spacer as it will be used.
- [ ] Titrate under real TMS with jittered pulses until 10 consecutive pulses go undetected.
- [ ] Inspect the online TEP for N100/P200 relative to the early response. Optionally run a short click-only block.
- [ ] Re-tune after any change in intensity, coil, target, orientation or foam.

**After every block**
- [ ] Collect the four ratings: click loudness, twitch, discomfort, pain.

**Report**
- [ ] Stimulator and coil used for the click recording.
- [ ] Noise type and parameters.
- [ ] Titration criterion, and that it was done under real TMS.
- [ ] Final volume or SPL. If SPL was not measured, say so.
- [ ] Earphones and foam.
- [ ] Verification method and its outcome.
- [ ] Re-tuning events.
- [ ] Per-block sensory ratings.
