# Occipital vs premotor P200: which test, and what it can and cannot show

## Short answer

Because the same participants were stimulated at both sites, the comparison is **within-subject**. A paired t-test on P200 amplitude (or a Wilcoxon signed-rank test if the paired differences are clearly non-normal) will tell you whether the P200 differs between sites. Better still, use a cluster-based permutation test across channels and time, which avoids picking the electrode or window after seeing the data.

**No statistical test will let you conclude that "occipital cortex is more responsive" from a P200 difference.** The test answers "is the scalp P200 larger after occipital than after premotor stimulation?" It cannot tell you *why*. For P200 in particular, the most likely explanation of a between-site difference is a difference in sensory input, not in cortical responsiveness. Choosing a test is the easy part. The inference is the problem.

## Why the P200 is a poor index of site-specific cortical responsiveness

1. **The late TEP (N100/P200, about 60–300 ms) is dominated by sensory-evoked potentials.** In the largest sensory-confound study to date (n = 135; prefrontal, premotor and parietal sites), how loud participants found the click was the strongest predictor of late TEP size. Removing masking increased late global field power at premotor and parietal sites. Scalp muscle twitch had a weaker effect, mostly beyond about 250 ms once sound was controlled. Discomfort had only a marginal effect, and pain had none. Between-site differences in N100/P200 followed the sensory-rating ordering.
2. **The late response looks much the same wherever you stimulate.** In that study, 88% or more of late-window variance was a single frontocentral component in *every* condition, including stimulation of the shoulder, which involves no cortex. P200 peaked at about 190–200 ms at every site. A component with the same topography regardless of site cannot be used to say that one site's cortex responds more.
3. **The auditory and somatosensory input differs between your sites.** Occipital and premotor coil positions differ in:
   - **Click loudness and bone conduction**, and how well masking works. Masking efficacy is known to vary by site.
   - **Scalp and neck muscle activation and twitch.** Occipital positions are near the neck and occipital muscles.
   - **Visual input.** Occipital stimulation can evoke phosphenes, which adds a visually evoked response that premotor stimulation does not. Premotor stimulation has no comparable effect.
4. **The dose is not equal across sites.** Different coil-to-cortex distances, gyral geometry and coil orientations mean the same stimulator output gives different cortical E-fields. Even standard correction of intensity for coil-to-cortex distance left modelled E-fields significantly different between sites. "Twice as large" may partly reflect "stimulated harder".
5. **TEP amplitude is not a pure excitability measure** at any latency. It also depends on reference choice, channel/ROI selection, trial count, and preprocessing (e.g., how aggressively ICA removed components at each site).

## What you would need for a defensible site comparison

| Requirement | Why |
|---|---|
| Separate per-site, per-block ratings of **click loudness, scalp twitch, discomfort and pain** (0–10), and for occipital, **phosphene presence/intensity** | Lets you test whether the P200 difference survives once sensation is accounted for |
| **Site-matched sensory sham or control** (e.g., a click-preserving sham plus electrical scalp stimulation at each site, or at least a non-cortical control such as shoulder stimulation) | Lets you estimate and subtract the peripherally evoked potential at each site |
| **Matched or modelled dose** (E-field modelling, or reporting intensity per site with justification) | Without it, a size difference may simply reflect dose |
| Masking that was verified separately at each site | Masking efficacy is site-dependent |

## Recommended analysis plan

Assumptions: two active conditions (occipital, premotor) in the same participants; data epoched and cleaned with the same pipeline for both sites; common average reference; P200 measured in a predefined window (e.g., about 160–240 ms).

1. **Descriptive within-subject contrast (what you asked for).**
   - Paired t-test or Wilcoxon signed-rank test on P200 amplitude from a channel/ROI and window **fixed in advance**, not chosen at the peak of the grand-average difference. Report the effect size (dz) with a confidence interval.
   - Preferably, run a **cluster-based permutation test** (paired, channels × time, 0–300 ms or a pre-registered window) on the site difference. Report the clusters as "a site difference in this spatiotemporal region", not as localisation of an effect.
   - If you use GMFA as the outcome, remember that it measures global scalp dispersion, not site-specific responsiveness.
2. **Test whether sensation explains the difference.** Fit a linear mixed model, for example:
   `P200 ~ site + click_loudness + twitch + discomfort (+ phosphene for occipital) + (1 | participant)`
   - Ratings would ideally be at block level, or at least at site level within participants.
   - If the site effect shrinks to zero once click loudness and twitch are included, the difference is consistent with sensory input, not cortical responsiveness.
   - With only two sites per person, keep the random-effects structure simple and treat this as an adjustment analysis, not a causal decomposition.
3. **If you have site-matched shams:** subtract each site's sham response from its active TEP (or compare active-minus-sham between sites with a paired test or cluster test). This is the most direct way to estimate site-specific cortical contributions. Note that evidence that the sensory and cortical responses simply add, without interacting, comes from a limited set of targets and windows (M1 and SMA, up to about 110 ms). Treat late-window subtraction at occipital cortex as an assumption, not an established fact.
4. **If the question is really about local cortical responsiveness, move the analysis earlier and closer to the target.** Compare early, local components (<60 ms) using local mean field power over a predefined ROI near each target, computed after full-channel re-referencing. Early components are much more site-specific and much less affected by auditory manipulation, but they are not clean:
   - Residual TMS-evoked muscle activity survived ICA in about a third of individuals after premotor stimulation. Check occipital similarly, because neck and occipital muscles are nearby.
   - A somatosensory contribution cannot be fully ruled out.
   - The ROIs are different for the two sites, so an amplitude comparison still mixes cortical response with geometry, dose and electrode coverage.
   - A useful QC check: split participants by visible muscle activity at 5–15 ms in the *uncleaned* epochs, and confirm that the site difference holds in the no-muscle subgroup.

## Quality checks before running any test

- Use an identical preprocessing pipeline for both sites, and report per site how many trials and channels were rejected and how many ICA components were removed. Unequal cleaning can create or remove amplitude differences on its own.
- Overlay butterfly plots and topographies per site. If the occipital P200 is frontocentral with the same topography as the premotor one, that points to a shared sensory generator, not an occipital cortical response.
- Check that the reference was the same for both sites and that conclusions do not change with a different reference.
- Report stimulation intensity per site, and how it was set (e.g., %rMT, phosphene threshold, E-field).

## How to report it if sensory data are not available

Frame the result descriptively. For example: "P200 amplitude was larger following occipital than premotor stimulation (paired test, effect size, CI). Because late TEP components are strongly influenced by auditory and somatosensory input, which were not measured/matched across sites, this difference cannot be attributed to differences in cortical responsiveness." Avoid "occipital cortex is more excitable/responsive" unless the difference survives sensory adjustment or sham subtraction, and ideally also appears in early local components.

## Bottom line

Use a **paired test** (paired t-test or Wilcoxon), or better a **paired cluster-based permutation test**, to show that the P200 differs between sites. To support any claim about cortical responsiveness, add **per-site sensory ratings as covariates in a mixed model** and, ideally, **site-matched sham subtraction**. A late, frontocentral component like the P200 is the wrong outcome for that claim. Early, local responses analysed with muscle-artifact checks are a more defensible outcome, though still imperfect.
