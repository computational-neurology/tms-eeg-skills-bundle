# How long a window to remove and interpolate around the TMS pulse

## Short answer

There is no single correct length. The window should cover **only the period your recording is saturated or physically corrupted by the pulse**, and that period depends on your amplifier, sampling rate, electrodes, coil and intensity. In practice:

- **Typical range:** start 1–2 ms **before** the pulse and end **+5 to +10 ms** after it. A window of **−2 to +10 ms** is a sensible default to start from, which you then adjust to your data.
- **Shorter (about −1 to +4–6 ms)** is achievable with good TMS-compatible DC amplifiers at high sampling rates (≥5 kHz), or with sample-and-hold systems where the amplifier is blanked for ~2–3 ms.
- **Longer (+12 to +20 ms or more)** is sometimes needed when there is amplifier ringing or slow recovery. Lengthening the window **to remove cranial muscle or decay artifacts** is a poor trade, because it also removes the earliest TEP components. Handle those with ICA, SSP–SIR, SOUND or decay fitting instead.

Decide the length from your own data, with a criterion you state in advance. Use one fixed window for all participants and conditions, and report it exactly, for example "data from −2 to +8 ms were removed and replaced by cubic interpolation".

## What the window is (and is not) for

Several artifacts follow the pulse, and only the first belongs in the removal window:

| Artifact | Typical duration | Best handled by |
|---|---|---|
| Pulse artifact (induced voltage, amplifier saturation) | ~1–5 ms; longer if the amplifier recovers slowly or rings | **Removal + interpolation** |
| Amplifier ringing / recovery | up to ~10–15 ms on some systems | Removal, if it is clearly non-physiological and brief |
| Cranial muscle (temporalis, frontalis) | ~5–30 ms, very large at lateral sites | ICA (often a dedicated first round), SSP–SIR, or better coil placement and orientation during acquisition |
| Electrode polarisation / decay | tens to hundreds of ms | ICA, exponential or decay fitting, careful skin preparation |
| Capacitor recharge artifact | brief spike at a device-specific delay | A separate short removal window, or shifting the recharge delay outside the analysis window |

If you size the main window to "everything looks clean", you will usually end up at 20–30 ms and lose components such as the motor-cortex N15/P30 or early prefrontal responses.

## How to decide the length: a practical procedure

1. **Record in a way that makes a short window possible.** Sample at ≥5 kHz (ideally higher during acquisition, then downsample later). Use a TMS-compatible amplifier with a wide dynamic range. Keep electrode impedances low and route leads away from the coil. Without hardware filters that ring, the artifact stays short. Most of the window length is decided before preprocessing starts.

2. **Inspect unfiltered data at high temporal resolution.** Epoch around the pulse (for example −50 to +100 ms). Do not filter or downsample yet, because filtering smears the artifact in time. Plot:
   - a butterfly plot of all channels, average and single trials, zoomed to −5 to +30 ms;
   - the same plotted as absolute amplitude on a log scale, or as GMFP. This makes the point where the signal "lands" much easier to see;
   - the channels closest to the coil and the worst trials and participants, not just the grand average.

3. **Define the end point with an explicit criterion.** Examples:
   - the first sample after which the signal stays within ±k baseline SDs (for example k = 5–10 of the −100 to −5 ms baseline) or within a plausible physiological range (for example ±100–200 µV in single trials). Apply this per channel, then take a high percentile (95th–99th) across channels and trials;
   - the point where the first derivative returns to baseline level. The idea is that the pulse artifact shows extreme slopes, while neural signals do not. Automated tools such as ARTIST use a similar derivative-based logic;
   - the end of any non-physiological ringing, meaning regular high-frequency oscillation that is identical across channels.

   Take the **maximum across participants**, or a high percentile if one outlier participant is excluded, and add a margin of **1–2 ms**.

4. **Set the start point.** Begin **1–2 ms before** the trigger. This covers trigger and sample jitter and any pre-pulse leakage, for example on systems where blanking starts early.

5. **Check the separate artifacts.** Look for a recharge spike, or a second pulse in paired-pulse or repetitive protocols. Remove each with its own short window rather than extending the main one.

6. **Run a sensitivity check.** Re-run the pipeline with the window shortened and lengthened by a few ms (for example end at +6, +8, +10, +12 ms). The components you plan to interpret should not change materially. If an early peak appears or disappears depending on the window, it is not interpretable.

7. **Pre-specify and keep the window fixed.** Choose it on pilot data, or on all data pooled across conditions before looking at condition effects. Then apply it to everyone. If the window is tuned per condition or per participant, artifact handling becomes confounded with your effects.

## Interpolation and where it goes in the pipeline

A commonly used order, as in TESA-style pipelines:

1. **Epoch and remove** the pulse window. Initially, replace it with zeros or a constant rather than interpolating. Interpolated segments would otherwise feed artificial structure into ICA.
2. **Downsample** (for example to 1–2 kHz) only after the pulse has been removed. Do not let the anti-aliasing filter run across the raw pulse.
3. Run a **first ICA round** (or SSP–SIR) for large muscle and decay artifacts. Some pipelines interpolate before this step. Either way, avoid filtering across uninterpolated gaps or steps.
4. **Interpolate** the removed window. The usual choice is **cubic interpolation** fitted on a few ms (for example 1–5 ms) of data either side. Linear interpolation is acceptable for very short gaps. Autoregressive or other model-based filling is an option for longer gaps.
5. **Filter**, then run a second ICA round, re-reference, and so on. Interpolate before any band-pass or notch filtering, so the filter does not ring on a step or a zero-filled gap.

Keep in mind:

- **Interpolated data contain no information.** Do not interpret anything inside the window. Also treat the few ms just after it with caution, because filter impulse responses spread the interpolated segment into neighbouring samples, and the cubic fit depends on the first post-window samples.
- Longer gaps produce smoother, more arbitrary fills. That is another reason to keep the window short and handle other artifacts by other means.
- For frequency or phase analyses, the interpolated segment and its filter spread contaminate low-latency time–frequency estimates. Exclude the affected time–frequency region accordingly. Low frequencies are affected for longer, because wavelets are longer at low frequencies.

## Assumptions

- Single-pulse TMS-EEG with a conventional TMS-compatible amplifier and a high sampling rate. For paired-pulse or rTMS, apply the same logic around every pulse.
- You care about early TEP components, so a short window matters. If you only analyse late components (>50 ms), a somewhat longer window costs little, but the same rules still apply: a fixed window, decided in advance, and reported.

## Bottom line

Start with about **−2 to +10 ms**. Then verify on unfiltered, high-resolution data where the signal actually returns to the physiological range across channels, trials and participants, and add a 1–2 ms margin. Use the shortest window that fully covers the pulse and amplifier recovery, and do not stretch it to absorb muscle or decay artifacts. Fix the window before analysing condition effects, check that your conclusions survive a few ms of variation, and report it exactly.
