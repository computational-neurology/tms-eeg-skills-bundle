# Auditory masking for TMS-EEG: setup and verification

## Why masking is needed

Each TMS pulse makes a loud, broadband click. At the coil it can exceed 100 dB(A), and it reaches the cochlea by two routes:

1. **Air conduction** through the ear canal.
2. **Bone conduction**, because the coil vibrates against the skull.

If the click is audible, it produces an auditory evoked potential (AEP). The main parts of the AEP are a vertex-maximal N100 (about 90–120 ms) and P180–P200 (about 180–220 ms). These overlap in time and space with the late TMS-evoked potentials (TEPs). Scalp sensation from the pulse adds its own somatosensory potential in the same window. If you don't control for these, the late TEP mixes cortical and sensory responses.

**Assumptions for this answer:**
- A conventional figure-of-eight coil
- Single-pulse or paired-pulse TEPs
- Healthy adults
- Equipment you can adapt (insert earphones, a sound level meter, the ability to play custom noise)

## Part 1: Setting up the masking

### 1. Hardware

- **Insert earphones.** Use tube-type inserts with foam tips (e.g., ER-3A/ER-2 style). The transducer sits away from the head, which:
  - keeps its electromagnetic field and cable away from the EEG electrodes, and
  - gives some passive attenuation.

  Push the foam tips deep enough to seal the ear canal.
- **Passive protection on top (optional but useful).** Wear earmuffs or ear defenders over the inserts if they fit around the EEG cap and coil. Where they don't fit, foam earplugs alone plus noise through the inserts is a common compromise.
- **Reduce bone conduction.** Place a thin foam or plastic spacer (about 0.5 cm) between the coil and the EEG cap. Noise cannot mask bone-conducted sound well, so this matters most at high intensities.
  - The spacer also increases coil-to-cortex distance. Determine the motor threshold or TEP-based intensity **with the spacer in place**, and keep it the same in every condition.
  - Thin spacers also help keep the coil from pressing on electrodes.
- **Cable routing.** Keep the earphone tubes and cables away from the electrode leads and the coil. Check for any noise-correlated artifact in the EEG at rest before you begin.

### 2. Choosing the noise

| Option | Notes |
|---|---|
| White noise | Historically common. It covers too much high-frequency energy and too little of the click's dominant band, so the level needed to mask is high and masking is often incomplete. |
| Pink or brown noise | Better matched to low-frequency click energy than white noise, but still generic. |
| **Click-derived noise (recommended)** | The noise's spectrum is matched to your own stimulator's click. Examples: noise made by scrambling recorded click waveforms (the approach of Massimini et al.), or adaptive tools such as TAAC (TMS Adaptable Auditory Control), which mixes click-like transients into a click-shaped noise background. This gives the most efficient masking at the lowest level. |

To build click-matched noise:

1. Record the click with a microphone at the participant's ear position. Use your actual coil, stimulator, pulse waveform, and an intensity close to the experimental one. The click spectrum changes with all of these.
2. Calculate the spectrum and generate noise with the same spectral profile. Scrambling segments of the recorded click works, or use a dedicated tool.
3. Make a new noise file if you change the coil, the waveform, or intensity by a large amount.

### 3. Delivery

- Start the noise **before** the block, so the noise onset response is long over, and run it **continuously** through the whole block.
- Use the **same noise at the same level** in every condition, including sham and control conditions. The noise itself can affect arousal and cortical state, so it must not differ between conditions.
- Jitter the inter-stimulus interval (e.g., 2–3 s, random) so the participant cannot predict pulse timing. Expectation and anticipatory potentials add to the late-window contamination.

### 4. Titrating the level, for each participant and each target

Do this with the coil at the real target, at the real intensity, with the cap and spacer on.

1. Start the noise at a comfortable low level.
2. Give single pulses at irregular intervals with the participant's eyes closed. Ask whether they can hear the click (yes/no, or a 0–10 loudness rating).
3. Raise the noise in small steps (about 2–3 dB) until the participant reports the click is inaudible.
4. **Safety ceiling:** do not exceed about **90 dB SPL** at the eardrum. Calibrate the earphone output with a sound level meter and an ear coupler or simulator so you know the real SPL for each software volume setting. Limit total exposure time and give breaks.
5. If the click is still audible at the maximum tolerable or safe level:
   - Record this.
   - Lower bone conduction (check the spacer and coil contact).
   - Consider whether the intensity is unnecessarily high.
   - Rely more heavily on the sham control (Part 2).

   Do not just raise the noise further.
6. Write down for each participant and target: the final noise level (dB SPL), the noise type, whether masking was complete, and any ratings.

Repeat the titration whenever the target or intensity changes, because click loudness and bone conduction differ by site. Check again during long sessions, since adaptation or the inserts loosening can change perception.

## Part 2: Checking that masking worked

Use behavioural checks, electrophysiological checks, and control conditions together. Each one alone is not enough.

### A. Behavioural checks

- **Detection check after titration.** With noise on, give a short series of pulses mixed with "catch" intervals where no pulse is given (or the coil is lifted away). Ask the participant to report whether a click happened. Detection near chance supports masking.
  - The participant can still feel the pulse on the scalp. Ask specifically about **hearing** the click, and expect that tactile cues may make a perfect test impossible.
- **Ratings after each block.** Ask about click loudness (0–10) and scalp sensation (0–10). This catches any drop in masking during the session and gives a covariate you can report.
- **Report** the share of participants and sessions with complete masking.

### B. Electrophysiological checks

1. **Online monitoring (during acquisition).** If you have real-time averaging, watch the running average at the stimulation site and at the vertex (Cz/FCz).
   - A growing, **symmetric, vertex-centred N100–P200 that looks the same regardless of target** is the signature of auditory or somatosensory contamination.
   - If it appears, adjust the noise level, the inserts, or the spacer before continuing.
2. **Offline topography and time course.** Real cortical TEPs, especially early ones (<80 ms), should be lateralized and depend on the target. Warning signs of sensory contamination:
   - a large central N100/P200 that is similar across different stimulation sites, and
   - a global mean field power peak at about 100 and 200 ms that has the same topography in every condition.
3. **Masking vs no masking (pilot).** In a pilot or a few participants, record a short block without noise. Compare the N100–P200 amplitude at the vertex with the masked block. A large reduction shows the masking is doing something. The masked condition's absolute level still needs checking against a sham condition.

### C. Control conditions (strongest evidence)

- **Sham TMS with the same click and the same masking.** Options:
  - A sham coil, or the active coil tilted or spaced away from the head, so it gives a similar click but negligible cortical stimulation.
  - Ideally, add electrical scalp stimulation to match the somatosensory input as well ("realistic sham").

  If masking worked, the sham condition should show no significant N100–P200 compared with the pre-stimulus baseline.
- **Statistical test:**
  - Compare the sham-evoked response at central electrodes in about 80–250 ms against baseline. Cluster-based permutation tests across channels and time work for this.
  - Bayesian statistics can quantify evidence **for** absence of a response.
  - Compare active vs sham responses directly: active minus sham, and spatial or temporal correlations between them. High active–sham correlation in the late window means the "TEP" there is largely sensory.
- **Click-only control.** Hold the coil a few centimetres above the head, or fire it next to the participant, so only the air-conducted click reaches them. This tests air-conducted masking only. Bone conduction is absent here, so it can overestimate how well masking works during real stimulation.

### D. Interpreting the results

- Masking that suppresses the AEP does **not** remove the somatosensory evoked potential, which has a similar N100–P200 shape. A residual vertex response in sham may come from the scalp sensation rather than hearing. Reducing it needs other measures: a realistic sham, a thin foam layer, and adjusting coil position to avoid scalp muscles and nerves.
- Complete masking is often impossible at high intensities (bone conduction). In that case, a matched sham condition plus transparent reporting are the main safeguards. Treat late components (>80–100 ms) cautiously and base the main conclusions on early components or on active-minus-sham contrasts.

## Reporting checklist

- Earphone type, earplugs or earmuffs, spacer material and thickness
- Noise type, how it was generated (click-derived?), and its spectrum
- Noise level in dB SPL, calibrated, for each participant and target, with the titration procedure
- Number of participants or sessions where the click remained audible
- Click loudness and scalp-sensation ratings
- Sham or control condition used, and the statistical result for residual N100–P200
- Inter-stimulus interval and jitter
