# Recording now and fixing it later with ICA: recommendation

## Short answer

I'd advise against recording all 30 participants as they are. ICA can't recover the data you describe, and the problem is much cheaper to fix now than later. Spend one or two pilot sessions on the acquisition first, then record. Settings for the ICA you will still need come in section 4.

**Assumptions:** 60–64 channels, a TMS-compatible amplifier, about 100–150 pulses per condition, and interest in the TEP at and around F3, including early components (roughly 15–60 ms).

---

## 1. Why ICA can't rescue these recordings

| Problem | Why ICA fails |
|---|---|
| **Saturated channels** | Clipping is non-linear and destroys information. ICA assumes the channels are a *linear* mix of sources, so a clipped channel breaks that model. Nothing can recover a clipped stretch of signal. The channels must be dropped and interpolated, and the saturated channels are usually the ones right under the coil. Those are the channels you most care about at F3. |
| **Large decay artifacts for ~20 ms** | These artifacts are strictly time-locked to the pulse, just like the TEP. ICA separates sources by statistical independence, and time-locked artifacts and neural responses are not independent across trials. Decay components therefore usually also contain brain signal. Removing them distorts or removes the early TEP (the components around 15–40 ms at F3). |
| **Artifact size** | The artifact is several orders of magnitude larger than the TEP. It dominates the decomposition, components get "split", and the results become unstable from run to run. |
| **Different contamination per participant** | Each participant would end up with a different set and number of removed components. That adds between-subject variance and possible bias that are hard to defend in review. |

Studies comparing cleaning pipelines on the *same* data have found that different pipelines yield noticeably different TEPs (e.g., Bertazzoli et al., 2021). The variability is largest exactly when the raw artifacts are big. A cleaning step should handle residual artifacts. It can't replace sound acquisition.

A long decay plus saturation usually points to fixable causes:

- electrode impedance that is too high or unequal
- leads forming loops or running parallel to the coil
- the amplifier's input range or gain being unsuitable
- electrodes moving or being pressed by the coil
- for F3 specifically, TMS-evoked cranial muscle activity, which is easily confused with decay

---

## 2. What to fix first (usually 1–2 pilot sessions)

1. **Impedance:** keep it below 5 kΩ on all channels, and as uniform as possible, especially under and next to the coil. Prepare the skin thoroughly (abrasion, enough gel). Recheck impedance during the session.
2. **Electrode leads:**
   - Route the leads perpendicular to the coil's windings or handle, away from the coil.
   - Avoid loops, and bundle or twist the leads together.
   - Make sure the coil doesn't press on the electrodes.
   - Use TMS-compatible electrodes (e.g., notched or C-ring Ag/AgCl, low profile).
3. **Amplifier:**
   - Use the largest input range or DC mode the amplifier offers so channels don't saturate.
   - Sample at ≥ 5 kHz (ideally 20–25 kHz if available) so the pulse and decay are captured accurately.
   - Avoid hardware high-pass filters that ring after the pulse.
   - Check that ground and reference are far from the coil.
4. **Coil position and orientation at F3:**
   - Lateral frontal sites easily recruit the frontalis and temporalis muscles. Small adjustments help: a slightly more medial target (e.g., toward F1/AF3) or rotating the coil.
   - Pick the orientation that minimizes the early large-amplitude response while still evoking a clear TEP.
   - If you use neuronavigation, document the final target and orientation.
5. **Real-time check:** average about 20 trials online (rt-TEP approach, Casarotto et al., 2022). Aim for:
   - no saturated channels
   - artifacts that are mostly gone within about 5–10 ms
   - a visible early TEP of reasonable amplitude (several µV) at the target
6. **Also check sensory confounds:** use noise masking (with ear defenders) and a foam layer under the coil. Consider a sham or sensory control condition.

These checks take roughly an hour of piloting. Without them, you may end up with 30 data sets that can't answer the research question.

---

## 3. If you have to record anyway

Before you record, write down these rules:

- **Exclude the early window.** If the decay still lasts about 20 ms after the fixes above, pre-specify that early components (< ~25 ms) will not be interpreted. Focus on later TEP components (e.g., N45, P60, N100, P180), which are more robust to these artifacts.
- **Handle channels under the coil.** Saturated channels near F3 will need interpolation. Treat analyses at the stimulation site as exploratory if many neighboring channels are affected.
- **Set exclusion criteria in advance.** Example: exclude a participant if more than 10–15% of channels are rejected, or if channels around the target saturate on more than X% of trials.

## 4. Recommended ICA approach for the remaining artifacts

The settings below follow the widely used two-round FastICA approach in TESA (Rogasch et al., 2017), with steps adapted to your problem.

### Preprocessing before ICA

1. **Epoch** from −1 to +1 s around the pulse (long epochs give ICA more data). Subtract the baseline using −500 to −10 ms.
2. **Remove the pulse window** from about −2 to +10 ms (adjust after inspecting the data). For the ICA rounds, replace it with a constant or zeros rather than interpolated data. Interpolate (cubic) only at the end, before filtering, and again before the final output.
3. **Drop bad and saturated channels completely before ICA.** Never feed clipped data into ICA. Interpolate them spherically *after* ICA.
4. **Reject trials with gross artifacts** (e.g., movement or saturation). Use amplitude, kurtosis or probability criteria, and inspect visually.
5. **Downsample** to 1–5 kHz after removing the pulse, if needed for speed. Apply anti-aliasing correctly.

### ICA round 1: large TMS-evoked artifacts (decay and muscle)

- **When:** before any temporal filtering. Filters smear large artifacts in time.
- **Algorithm:** FastICA, symmetric approach, `tanh` nonlinearity.
- **Dimensionality:** reduce with PCA to the true data rank. Count rejected or interpolated channels, plus one if you use an average reference. A rank-deficient ICA produces spurious, unstable components. PCA to 30–40 components is often a pragmatic choice with 60+ channels.
- **Which components to remove:** only clear artifact components:
  - very high amplitude in the 0–50 ms window, at least 8 times the mean in other windows (TESA default)
  - a focal topography around the coil or muscle sites
  - an exponential or very fast time course
  - consistency across trials
- **Remove sparingly.** Typically only a few components. Inspect each one manually.

### Optional alternative or complement to round 1

- **Fit and subtract exponential or polynomial decay** per channel and trial (TESA `tesa_fitdecay`), or
- **SSP-SIR** to suppress muscle artifacts (Mutanen et al., 2016), and/or
- **SOUND** to clean noise and bad channels (Mutanen et al., 2018).

These model the artifact explicitly instead of relying on independence. They often hold up better for time-locked decay.

### Filtering between rounds

- Band-pass 1–100 Hz (or 0.1/0.5–100 Hz if you need slow components), using a zero-phase filter.
- Notch at 50/60 Hz, or use a band-stop.
- Filter only after interpolating the pulse window, so the filter doesn't ring on the gap.

### ICA round 2: other artifacts

- Same algorithm and rank handling as round 1.
- Remove:
  - blinks and eye movements
  - persistent muscle activity
  - electrode noise
  - the decay that remains after round 1
- Use TESA `tesa_compselect` with its default thresholds as a starting point, but **check each suggested component visually**. ICLabel is not trained on TMS artifacts, so its classifications are unreliable for TMS-specific components.

### Robustness and reporting

- **Check stability:** rerun ICA with different seeds (e.g., ICASSO or RELICA) at least in a subset of participants. Unstable components should not be removed blindly.
- **Data requirement:** a rule of thumb is ≥ 20–30 × (number of components)² data points. With 30 components that is about 18,000–27,000 points, which is easily met with 1 s epochs × 100+ trials.
- **Document per participant:**
  - the rejected channels, trials and components (count and type)
  - the variance explained by the removed components
- **Sensitivity analysis:** run a second, independent pipeline (e.g., SOUND + SSP-SIR, or ARTIST by Wu et al., 2018) and report whether the conclusions hold. This is now considered best practice (Hernandez-Pavon et al., 2023).
- **Report** following the TMS-EEG reporting recommendations (e.g., the TMS-EEG checklist and TESA/methods guidelines).

---

## Bottom line

**ICA settings to use:** two-round FastICA (symmetric, tanh) at the correct rank. Remove the pulse window first, exclude saturated channels before ICA, and classify components conservatively with manual checks. Back this up with a second pipeline as a sensitivity analysis.

**More important than the settings:**
- Don't rely on ICA to fix saturation and a 20 ms decay.
- Fix the acquisition before recording all 30 participants: impedance, lead routing, amplifier range and sampling rate, coil orientation, and online TEP monitoring.
- One or two extra pilot sessions are far cheaper than 30 data sets that can't show a valid early TEP at F3.
