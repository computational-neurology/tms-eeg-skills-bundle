# One or two rounds of ICA in TMS-EEG preprocessing?

## Short answer

**Use two rounds when your data contain large TMS-evoked muscle artifacts. This is typical for lateral, frontal and temporal targets such as the DLPFC, inferior parietal cortex or lateral M1 at higher intensities.** In that situation the two rounds do different jobs, and you should not merge them:

| Round | When in the pipeline | Main target | Why it is separate |
|---|---|---|---|
| **ICA 1** | Early: after epoching and removing or zeroing the pulse window, **before** band-pass filtering | The **TMS-evoked muscle artifact**: large (often hundreds of µV to mV), early (~0–20 ms), time-locked to the pulse. Optionally also large decay artifacts | The muscle artifact is so large that it dominates the decomposition and hides everything else. Filtering it first would smear it in time (ringing, spread into baseline and later latencies) |
| **ICA 2** | Late: after interpolating the pulse window, filtering (e.g. 1–100 Hz plus line-noise notch) and re-removing the pulse window | **Everything else**: eye blinks and saccades, residual or persistent muscle (tonic EMG), electrode/channel noise, residual decay, cardiac activity, line noise remnants | With the big muscle artifact gone, ICA can separate the smaller artifacts cleanly. These artifacts are mostly not time-locked to the pulse and resemble "standard" EEG artifacts |

**One round is often enough when the large early muscle artifact is small or absent.** Examples are vertex, midline or medial targets, or low intensities, with good coil placement. In that case a single post-filtering ICA for blinks, eye movements, persistent EMG and noisy channels is usually adequate. If you have only a mild decay artifact, handle it with a dedicated decay-removal step rather than adding an extra ICA. Running a first round that finds no clear muscle components gains you nothing and only adds variance.

This is the logic behind the two-round approach of Rogasch et al. (2014, NeuroImage) and the default TESA pipeline (Rogasch et al., 2017). It also follows the earlier work on removing large muscle artifacts with ICA (Korhonen et al., 2011).

## Typical two-round sequence (TESA-style)

1. Epoch around the pulse (e.g. −1000 to +1000 ms). Remove obviously bad channels and trials.
2. Remove the pulse and early ringing window (e.g. −2 to +10–15 ms; adjust to your amplifier and data). Leave it out of the decomposition by zeroing or excluding it.
3. Downsample if needed.
4. **ICA round 1** (e.g. FastICA). Remove only components clearly representing the TMS-evoked muscle artifact: very large amplitude, peaking within ~0–20 ms, a focal topography near the coil, and a high-frequency spectrum. Optionally also remove clear large decay components.
5. Interpolate the removed pulse window (e.g. cubic). Then band-pass filter and notch filter.
6. Remove the pulse window again (the interpolated segment should not enter round 2).
7. **ICA round 2.** Remove blinks, eye movements, persistent EMG, electrode noise and other residual artifacts.
8. Interpolate the pulse window and missing channels, then re-reference (e.g. average reference) and baseline-correct.

## Practical cautions

- **Be conservative in round 1.** TMS-evoked muscle components are not truly independent of early neural responses. They are time-locked to the same event and overlap in time and space. Removing them inevitably removes some genuine early TEP signal. Treat TEP features before ~15–20 ms at muscle-heavy sites as unreliable whatever cleaning you do.
- **ICA assumptions are strained.** Both the artifact and the brain response are time-locked to the pulse, so the components are not statistically independent and stationary as ICA assumes. Different pipelines (ICA variants, SOUND/SSP-SIR, PCA-based approaches) can give noticeably different TEPs from the same data (e.g. Rogasch et al., 2017; Bertazzoli et al., 2021). Inspect the TEP before and after each round.
- **Rank.** Interpolated channels, average referencing and the removed window reduce data rank. Reduce dimensionality (e.g. PCA to the true rank) before ICA to avoid unstable or duplicated components.
- **Data quantity.** ICA needs enough samples relative to the number of channels squared. With few trials, reduce dimensionality or combine conditions or sessions from the same subject in one decomposition. A shared decomposition also keeps cleaning consistent across conditions you want to compare.
- **Component selection.** Semi-automated classifiers (e.g. TESA's component selection thresholds) are useful, but check classifications visually: time course, topography, spectrum and trial-by-trial images. Generic EEG classifiers such as ICLabel were not trained on TMS-evoked artifacts and can misclassify them.
- **Don't let ICA do jobs other tools do better.** Large decay artifacts can be removed by fitting exponentials or by dedicated decay methods. Very large early muscle artifacts can also be suppressed with SSP-SIR (Mutanen et al., 2018), and channel noise with SOUND (Mutanen et al., 2018). Some groups replace round 1 with SSP-SIR and keep a single ICA for ocular and other artifacts. Fully automated alternatives such as ARTIST (Wu et al., 2018) and the TMSEEG toolbox (Atluri et al., 2016) use their own staged approaches.
- **Reduce artifacts at acquisition.** Coil orientation, target placement, intensity and noise masking (with foam or padding to cut bone-conducted sound and scalp sensation) reduce how much ICA must remove. Recent consensus recommendations (Hernandez-Pavon et al., 2023, Brain Stimulation) stress this, along with careful reporting.
- **Report transparently.** For each subject and round, report the number and type of components removed, the windows removed or interpolated, and the filter settings. Ideally also run a sensitivity check, such as the TEP with and without muscle-component removal.

## Assumptions

I've assumed standard single-pulse TMS-EEG with ≥60 channels, a TMS-compatible amplifier and an EEGLAB/TESA-like workflow. With a different toolbox the order of steps may vary, but the core logic is the same. Isolate and remove the huge, early, pulse-locked muscle artifact first, before filtering. Then clean the remaining "ordinary" EEG artifacts in a second, separate decomposition.
