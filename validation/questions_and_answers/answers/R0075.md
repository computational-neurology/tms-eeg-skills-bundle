# How long should the TMS pulse-artifact window be, and how do you choose it?

## Short answer

No single window length is correct for every dataset. The window depends on your amplifier, sampling rate, stimulation intensity, electrode lead setup and how early the responses you analyse are. The rule is simple: **remove the smallest window that fully covers the pulse and the fast amplifier recovery, measured from your own unfiltered data. Use the same window for every condition, and do not let it overlap the earliest latency you want to interpret.**

As an example, a TESA-style workflow often starts by removing about **−2 to +10–12 ms** around the pulse and filling the gap with cubic interpolation. A second, shorter window (for example −3 to +10 ms) is sometimes used later, after decay or muscle cleaning. Treat these numbers only as a starting point to check against your data, not as defaults to copy.

**Assumptions** (you did not give these details):
- Your data are conventional TMS-EEG (about 60 channels, sampled at roughly 1–25 kHz) with reliable trigger timing.
- You analyse TEPs, GMFA or LMFP rather than immediate TEPs (i-TEPs, under about 10 ms).
- Interpolation happens before any filtering, ICA or downsampling.

If you analyse i-TEPs, see the last section, because there the window becomes part of your scientific claim.

---

## What sets the window length

| Factor | Effect on the window |
|---|---|
| **The pulse itself** | The true transient lasts only about 0.2 ms, but the amplifier's hardware filters stretch it to about 0.5 ms or more. It is 4–5 orders of magnitude larger than the EEG, so any leftover sample contaminates everything that follows. |
| **Amplifier type** | Sample-and-hold amplifiers usually recover within about 2–5 ms. DC-coupled amplifiers with high sampling rates can recover faster but record a sharper transient. |
| **Sampling rate** | This is the strongest predictor of pulse-artifact amplitude and duration. At 20 kHz the artifact is about 86% larger but about 57% shorter than at 5 kHz. A window measured at one sampling rate does not carry over to another. |
| **Stimulation intensity** | The second strongest predictor. Higher intensity lengthens the artifact by about 30%. If you use several intensities, measure the window on the highest one. |
| **Trigger synchronisation / jitter** | A minor effect on amplitude, but jitter between trigger and EEG sample clock shifts where the artifact falls. The window start has to cover this, which is why it usually begins at about −1 to −2 ms rather than at 0. |
| **Decay artifact** | A slow, exponential, capacitive tail that can last **tens of milliseconds**. Its amplitude tracks the pulse amplitude (ρ ≈ 0.86), and its polarity depends on how the electrode leads are routed. Extending the interpolation window is usually the wrong fix for it. |
| **TMS-evoked muscle artifact** | Biphasic peaks around 4–5 and 7–10 ms. It can exceed 1 mV and decays over more than 50 ms, mainly at lateral or frontal sites. It is a separate artifact from the pulse, but it determines whether a short window is enough. |

The key distinction: **interpolation is for the pulse and the fast recovery.** Decay and muscle artifacts are usually handled with separate methods (a second short window after cleaning, decay suppression, SOUND/SSP-SIR, or a better coil position or lead routing at acquisition). Stretching one interpolation window to 20–30 ms to hide them deletes real early TEP signal and replaces it with a smooth curve.

---

## Step-by-step procedure for choosing the window

1. **Check pulse timing first.** Confirm that each event marker sits on the pulse peak in the raw data, and estimate the sample-level jitter. A window placed relative to misaligned events is meaningless.

2. **Plot unfiltered data at high temporal resolution.** For each condition, plot from about −10 to +50 ms, and also out to about 100 ms:
   - single trials overlaid on the trial average, for **all channels**, especially those under and near the coil;
   - a summary trace such as mean absolute amplitude or GMFA across channels, on a **log y-axis**, so the return to baseline is visible across several orders of magnitude;
   - optionally, the first derivative, which shows clearly where the sharp transient stops.

   Use no filters, no re-referencing and no downsampling for this step. Each of these smears the artifact and hides where it really ends.

3. **Set the start.** Find where the signal first leaves the baseline before the pulse, then add a margin for trigger jitter and any pre-ringing from the hardware filters. This is typically −1 to −2 ms.

4. **Set the end.** Find the latency where the **sharp** pulse and recovery transient has ended in the worst channel, the worst condition and the highest intensity. One practical criterion is that the trial-averaged absolute amplitude and slope have dropped back into the range of the pre-stimulus baseline, or onto a smooth decay curve. Add a small safety margin of about 1–2 ms. What remains after this point should be either:
   - EEG-like activity, or
   - a smooth decay with the same polarity on every trial, or a muscle burst. Each of these is handled by its own cleaning step, not by a longer window.

5. **Use one window for all conditions** (active, sham, control) and, ideally, for all participants. Base it on the worst case. Different windows per condition create differences between conditions that come from processing, not physiology.

6. **Choose the interpolation method.**
   - Cubic interpolation, fitted on a few milliseconds of data either side of the gap (as in TESA's remove-data and interpolate-data steps), joins the edges more smoothly than linear interpolation when large transients sit next to the gap.
   - Linear interpolation is simple but can leave sharp corners at the window edges, which ring after filtering.
   - Fit the interpolation on clean data next to the window. If the edges are still contaminated, the window is too short.

7. **Handle the decay and muscle artifacts next,** if they remain. Typical options are a second short interpolation window after ICA, SOUND or SSP-SIR (as in two-pass TESA-style workflows), or targeted decay or muscle suppression. Do not use ordinary ICA rejection to remove clearly TMS-locked muscle components.

8. **Only then filter, downsample and run ICA.** Filtering across an unremoved pulse, or across a sharp interpolation edge, causes ringing that can spread across the whole epoch. Once that happens it cannot be undone.

---

## Trade-offs: too short versus too long

**Window too short:**
- Leftover pulse or recovery artifact rings after filtering and can look like oscillatory TEP components.
- ICA is fitted to artifact-dominated samples and becomes less reliable.
- Fake early peaks appear on all channels at once, often with a polarity set by the lead configuration.
- Sharp jumps appear at the edges of the interpolated segment.

**Window too long:**
- Real early TEP components (for example the M1 P15/N15–P30 complex) are deleted.
- The interpolated stretch is smooth, artificial signal that lowers GMFA and LMFP early on and distorts early topographies.
- Muscle or decay is hidden instead of handled, and the real latency of early peaks becomes impossible to judge.
- Your earliest interpretable latency moves later. **Never analyse or report a peak that falls inside, or right at the edge of, the interpolated window.**

---

## QC and reporting checklist

- Overlay the traces before and after interpolation, for single trials and averages, across all channels, with the window boundaries marked.
- Check for discontinuities or slope breaks at both edges, and for ringing once the analysis filters are applied.
- Compare leftover artifact at the edges between active and sham/control, and between intensities. Leftovers that differ by condition are a red flag.
- **Sensitivity analysis:** rerun the pipeline with the window end shifted by about ±2–5 ms. Conclusions about early components should not change. If they do, treat those components as provisional.
- Compare the cleaned TEP with a minimally processed version in time ranges without artifact, to make sure processing has not changed the signal there.
- Report the exact window (start and end in ms relative to the pulse), the interpolation method and the samples it was fitted on, the sampling rate, the synchronisation method, the amplifier, the stimulation intensity, and any second window.

## Minimal template for measuring the artifact (Python, NumPy on epoched data)

```python
import numpy as np

# X: epochs data, shape (n_trials, n_channels, n_times), UNFILTERED, high sampling rate
# times: seconds relative to pulse; fill these from your own data
X_abs_mean = np.abs(X).mean(axis=0)          # trial-averaged |amplitude| per channel
worst = X_abs_mean.max(axis=0)               # worst-channel envelope over time

base = (times >= -0.100) & (times <= -0.010) # pre-pulse baseline (adjust)
thr = worst[base].mean() + 5 * worst[base].std()   # heuristic threshold, tune and justify

post = times >= 0
above = np.where(post & (worst > thr))[0]
# The first post-pulse return below thr marks the end of the sharp transient;
# inspect visually: a slow decay may stay above thr and needs a separate step.
print("Candidate artifact end (ms):", times[above].min()*1e3 if above.size else None)
```

Use this only to guide your visual inspection. The threshold is a heuristic. Confirm the result on the plots from step 2, repeat it per condition and intensity, and fix one final window before running the main analysis.

## If you analyse very early responses (i-TEPs, under about 10–15 ms)

- The interpolation window is **part of the scientific claim** and should be fixed (ideally preregistered) before you look at the effects.
- Very high sampling rates (tens of kHz) with synchronised acquisition can shrink the pulse artifact to about 2 ms. That is the only realistic way to analyse the first few milliseconds.
- You need to rule out leftover pulse and decay artifact (the decay tail can extend tens of ms past a short −2 to 10 ms window), lead-routing effects, sampling and synchronisation artifacts, and early muscle. Evidence from sham/control conditions is required. Reversing the current direction can also test whether early peaks simply flip with the polarity of the artifact.
- Without this evidence, describe early findings as exploratory.

**Interpretation caveat:** whatever window you choose, the TEP, GMFA and LMFP that remain are scalp responses shaped by cortical, sensory, muscle and processing factors. Do not treat them as pure measures of cortical excitability. Early-window results in particular should always be reported together with the exact artifact window.
