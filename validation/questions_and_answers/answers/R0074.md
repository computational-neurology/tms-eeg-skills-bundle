# How long should the TMS pulse removal/interpolation window be?

## Short answer

No single window length is correct for every dataset. Choose the **shortest window that fully covers the pulse artifact in your own data**, measured on unfiltered single trials at the original sampling rate. Then fix that window for the whole study and test how sensitive your results are to it.

As a starting point to check against your data (not a default to copy):

- **Typical conventional TEP data (about 5 kHz sampling, standard TMS-compatible amplifier):** windows of roughly **−2 to +10…15 ms** around the pulse are common. A widely used TESA-style example is **[−2, 12] ms with cubic interpolation**. Some pipelines add a second, shorter removal step later (e.g., around [−3, 10] ms) to deal with residual artifact after decay/muscle cleaning.
- **Very high sampling rates (e.g., 20–50 kHz):** the pulse transient is much shorter. At 50 kHz it has been reduced to about 2 ms, which allows much shorter windows. This matters most for immediate/early TEP (i-TEP) work.

What actually sets the length is the artifact you measure in your recording, not the number that appears in the literature.

**Assumptions** (you did not specify these):
- You are analysing conventional TEPs, GMFA or LMFP (roughly from 15 ms onward), not immediate responses in the first few milliseconds.
- Data come from a TMS-compatible amplifier at about 5 kHz, with reliable pulse triggers.
- You use a 60-channel-class montage.

If any of these differ, the procedure below still applies, but the resulting window will differ.

---

## Why the window length matters (the trade-off)

| Window too short | Window too long |
|---|---|
| Residual pulse or recovery artifact stays in the data. | Removes real early TEP activity (e.g., early components starting around 15–30 ms). |
| Later filtering spreads it as **ringing** across the whole epoch, and this cannot be undone afterwards. | Replaces real signal with made-up interpolated values. Anything inside the window is not data. |
| It can dominate ICA, distort the decomposition and pull cortical signal into "artifact" components. | Long linear or cubic bridges can create edge discontinuities and odd shapes at the boundaries. |
| Residual decay can look like an early "response". | Early-latency results can no longer be estimated. |

The goal is to remove the part that **cannot be recovered**: the pulse transient itself, and any samples that are saturated or clipped. Slower artifacts should be handled with methods built for them, not by stretching the interpolation window.

---

## What determines how long the pulse artifact lasts in your data

1. **Sampling frequency.** This is the strongest predictor of both artifact amplitude and duration. Going from 5 kHz to 20 kHz gave about 86% higher pulse amplitude but about 57% shorter duration. So a window taken from a study with a different sampling rate does not transfer.
2. **Stimulation intensity.** This is the second strongest predictor: higher intensity lengthened the artifact by about 30%. If your study uses several intensities, set the window from the **highest** one.
3. **Synchronisation of the amplifier and stimulator clocks.** This has a smaller effect, but synchronised acquisition makes the artifact more consistent within a subject. Trigger jitter is the main reason the window needs a pre-pulse margin (the −1 to −2 ms part).
4. **Amplifier design.**
   - Sample-and-hold systems hold the signal during the pulse and recover in about 2–5 ms.
   - DC-coupled, high-sampling-rate, fine-resolution systems record through the pulse but need their own check of how long recovery takes.
5. **Electrode lead layout.** The pulse and the slower decay artifact both come from induction in the lead loops, and their amplitudes are strongly correlated (ρ ≈ 0.86). Lead routing can change their size and even flip the polarity of the decay.
6. **Recharge timing.** If the stimulator's capacitor recharge happens inside your epoch, it produces a separate artifact. Move the recharge delay outside the analysis window if you can, or mark it as its own window.

## A practical procedure for choosing the window

### Step 1: Inspect the raw artifact before any processing
- Use data that are **unfiltered and not downsampled**, at the original sampling rate.
- Epoch around each pulse, e.g., −20 to +100 ms.
- Plot **single trials and the average for all channels** at high time resolution. Do this for each condition and each intensity.
- Confirm that the pulse onset lines up with the trigger across trials (check for jitter).

### Step 2: Find where the artifact starts and ends
- **Start of the window:** the first sample where the pulse departs from the pre-pulse signal, minus a margin for trigger jitter. In practice this is usually −1 to −2 ms.
- **End of the window:** the first sample after which **all channels** have left saturation and returned to a plausible EEG range. One way to define this objectively:
  - Compute, per channel, the absolute amplitude (or the absolute sample-to-sample difference) of the average or median across trials.
  - Find the last sample that exceeds a threshold based on the pre-pulse baseline (e.g., baseline mean + k × SD, or a fixed value in µV).
  - Take the **worst case** across channels, participants and conditions, and add a small safety margin of 1–2 ms.
- Set the threshold and margin **before** you look at condition differences.

### Step 3: Separate the pulse from what follows it
Some post-pulse artifacts should be handled with other tools rather than by lengthening the window:

| Artifact | What it looks like | How to handle it |
|---|---|---|
| Pulse transient / saturation | Very large, sharp, lasts a few ms. | **Remove and interpolate.** |
| Decay / recovery artifact | Smooth, roughly exponential, same polarity on every trial, can last tens of ms. | Inspect it. Use ICA, SOUND/SSP-SIR-type cleaning, or a second short interpolation after cleaning. Otherwise exclude the affected latencies from analysis. Don't interpolate across tens of ms by default. |
| TMS-evoked scalp muscle artifact | Large, oscillatory, high-frequency, varies from trial to trial, roughly 5–30 ms. | Fix it at acquisition (move or rotate the coil) where possible. Offline, use muscle-specific methods such as SSP-SIR or careful component removal. Stretching the interpolation window to cover it erases the early TEP. |

Offline cleaning cannot recover every decay, recharge or discharge artifact. If one is large, the more reliable fix is at acquisition: lead routing, amplifier settings, coil position.

### Step 4: Fix one window for the whole study
- Use the **same window for all conditions**, including active, sham and control, and ideally for all participants. Different windows per condition create artificial condition differences.
- Interpolate **before** any filtering, ICA or downsampling. Then check the interpolation edges after each of those steps, because filtering or anti-alias downsampling across a sharp boundary can cause ringing.
- Interpolation method:
  - **Linear** is simple and doesn't overshoot.
  - **Cubic** (TESA-style, fitted to a short segment on each side, e.g., 1 ms) gives smoother boundaries. It can overshoot if residual decay is present at the edges.
  - Check the boundaries visually whichever you use.
- Start your analysis window **after** the interpolation window, with some margin. Never measure peaks or amplitudes inside interpolated samples.

### Step 5: Run a sensitivity analysis
- Repeat the key steps with the window end moved by a few ms (e.g., ±2–5 ms).
- If your TEP peaks, GMFA/LMFP or condition effects change substantially, the result depends on the artifact window. Report it with that caveat or treat it as exploratory.

### Step 6: Report it fully
Report:
- the exact window (e.g., "−2 to +12 ms relative to pulse onset") and how it was chosen;
- the interpolation method;
- sampling rate, amplifier type, stimulation intensity, synchronisation and recharge delay;
- whether a second removal/interpolation step was used.

## Minimal code template (Python/NumPy)

The two functions below are a sketch. Placeholders are marked `TODO`. Use them on data that are not filtered and not downsampled.

```python
import numpy as np

# epochs_data: (n_trials, n_channels, n_times), unfiltered, native sampling rate
# times: (n_times,) in seconds, 0 = pulse onset
# Build these from your own reader; confirm event timing first.

def estimate_artifact_end(epochs_data, times, base=(-0.020, -0.003), k=5.0,
                          search_max=0.050, margin=0.001):
    """Worst-case (across channels) time at which the trial-median signal
    returns within baseline mean +/- k*SD. Returns seconds."""
    med = np.median(epochs_data, axis=0)                  # channels x times
    bmask = (times >= base[0]) & (times <= base[1])
    mu = med[:, bmask].mean(axis=1, keepdims=True)
    sd = med[:, bmask].std(axis=1, keepdims=True)
    outside = np.abs(med - mu) > k * sd                   # channels x times
    post = (times > 0) & (times <= search_max)
    ends = []
    for ch in range(med.shape[0]):
        idx = np.where(outside[ch] & post)[0]
        ends.append(times[idx[-1]] if idx.size else 0.0)
    return max(ends) + margin                              # TODO: inspect per channel/condition

def interpolate_window(epochs_data, times, tmin_art, tmax_art):
    """Linear interpolation across [tmin_art, tmax_art] (seconds), per trial/channel."""
    out = epochs_data.copy()
    start = np.searchsorted(times, tmin_art)
    stop = np.searchsorted(times, tmax_art, side="right")
    left, right = start - 1, stop
    for tr in range(out.shape[0]):
        for ch in range(out.shape[1]):
            out[tr, ch, start:stop] = np.interp(
                times[start:stop], [times[left], times[right]],
                [out[tr, ch, left], out[tr, ch, right]])
    return out
```

Notes:
- The threshold approach finds the pulse transient well. A slow decay tail can keep the signal "outside baseline" for tens of ms. If the estimated end jumps far beyond a few ms, that is decay, not pulse: handle it separately (Step 3) rather than accepting a very long window.
- If you use a toolbox's built-in pulse-removal routine (e.g., TESA's removal and cubic interpolation in MATLAB), check its exact function signature in the current documentation. Apply the window you justified, not the routine's example values.

## Quality-control checklist

- [ ] Overlay raw and interpolated single trials and averages for all channels, from −20 to +100 ms.
- [ ] Check for discontinuities or overshoot at the interpolation edges, both before and after filtering/downsampling.
- [ ] Compare residual artifact between active and sham/control conditions and between intensities. It should not differ systematically.
- [ ] Look for smooth, same-polarity decay tails after the window. If present, handle them explicitly.
- [ ] Check filtered "blank" segments or pre-pulse data for ringing that looks like oscillatory TEP components.
- [ ] Run the sensitivity analysis on the window end and report it.
- [ ] Keep a minimally processed copy of the data for comparison.

## Caveats

- If you are interested in **immediate or very early responses (< ~10–15 ms)**, the window choice is itself part of your scientific claim. You will need:
  - high-sampling-rate acquisition;
  - documented lead configuration, synchronisation and recharge settings;
  - online QC and inspection of single trials for muscle activity;
  - sham/control or current-direction checks, to show that early peaks are not just leftover pulse or decay artifact.

  Without these, treat early findings as exploratory.
- Even with a well-chosen window, TEP amplitudes are scalp-level signals shaped by cortical, sensory, muscle and preprocessing factors. They are not a pure measure of cortical excitability.
