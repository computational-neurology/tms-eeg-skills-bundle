# One or two rounds of ICA in TMS-EEG preprocessing?

## Short answer

If your data contain a **large TMS-evoked muscle artefact** (common with lateral, frontal, prefrontal or temporal targets, or with higher intensities), use **two rounds of ICA**:

1. **Round 1 (early, before filtering):** removes **only the large TMS-evoked muscle artefact**. This is the high-amplitude, short-latency activity, roughly 5–30 ms after the pulse (sometimes up to about 50 ms). It can also remove part of any decay artefact.
2. **Round 2 (late, after filtering and cleaning):** removes **all other stereotyped artefacts**: blinks, eye movements, persistent (tonic) scalp-muscle EMG, single-electrode noise, residual decay or line noise, and sometimes cardiac activity.

If the TMS-evoked muscle artefact is small, **one round** placed after filtering (equivalent to round 2) is usually enough. Getting that small artefact is easier with careful targeting, coil orientation and real-time TEP monitoring during acquisition, and with midline or motor targets. One round keeps the pipeline simpler and reduces the risk of removing brain signal.

There is no field-wide consensus. Two rounds is the approach introduced by Rogasch et al. (2014) and built into the TESA toolbox (Rogasch et al., 2017), and it is one of the most common choices. It is not the only valid one.

## Why two rounds? The reasoning

- **Amplitude dominance.** The TMS-evoked muscle artefact can be tens to hundreds of times larger than the TEP. ICA maximises independence across the whole dataset, so a huge artefact takes over the decomposition. If you run ICA once on everything, small artefacts (blinks, electrode noise) end up poorly separated and spread across components that also carry the big muscle signal. Taking the big artefact out first gives a cleaner second decomposition for the remaining artefacts.
- **Filtering order.** Band-pass and notch filters applied to data that still contain large, sharp artefacts (the pulse, the muscle burst) cause ringing that spreads the artefact in time. Round 1 runs on unfiltered data, after the pulse window is removed and before the filters. Filtering is then safer, and round 2 runs on the filtered data.
- **Different selection criteria.** The two artefact types are identified differently. Round 1 components show a very large, time-locked response in the first ~10–50 ms, a focal topography near the coil, and a broadband or high-frequency spectrum. Round 2 components are found with classic EEG criteria: frontal topography with blink time course, horizontal-EOG dipolar pattern, flat-high-frequency EMG spectrum, a single-channel topography, and so on.

## A typical two-round sequence (TESA-style)

Assumptions: standard EEGLAB/TESA-type workflow, single-pulse paradigm, data recorded with a TMS-compatible amplifier.

1. Epoch around the pulse (e.g., −1 to +1 s) and baseline-correct.
2. Remove the pulse/ringing window (e.g., −2 to ~6–15 ms depending on your system). Replace it with zeros or a constant, or interpolate it.
3. Downsample (e.g., to 1000 Hz). Remove bad channels and grossly bad trials.
4. **ICA round 1** (e.g., FastICA). Remove only the components with a clear TMS-evoked muscle profile. Be conservative here.
5. Remove the pulse window again, then interpolate it (e.g., cubic). Apply band-pass filtering (e.g., 1–100 Hz) and notch filtering (or another line-noise approach).
6. Remove remaining bad trials.
7. **ICA round 2.** Remove ocular, persistent EMG, electrode-noise, residual decay and cardiac components.
8. Interpolate the removed channels, re-reference (e.g., average), and baseline-correct.

## Practical cautions (apply to either choice)

- **ICA cannot cleanly separate time-locked brain signal from time-locked artefact.** The TMS-evoked muscle response and early TEPs (e.g., N15, P30) share timing with the pulse, which violates independence and stationarity. Removing round-1 components can therefore also remove or distort early neural activity. Treat peaks under ~30 ms with caution if strong muscle components were removed.
- **Sensory-evoked potentials (auditory click, somatosensory) are not artefacts that ICA removes reliably.** They are real brain responses that overlap with TMS-evoked activity, mainly around N100/P200. Deal with them at acquisition (noise masking, foam spacer) and with a realistic sham or control condition, not with ICA.
- **Rank.** Removing or interpolating channels and using an average reference reduce data rank. Remove interpolated channels before ICA and add them back afterwards, or reduce dimensionality (PCA) to the true rank. Otherwise you can get unstable or duplicated components.
- **Enough data.** ICA needs sufficient samples relative to the number of channels. A rough rule of thumb is ≥ 20–30 × (n channels)² samples. With few trials or many channels, consider PCA reduction.
- **Stationarity of the pulse window.** A long flat or zeroed segment in every epoch is a non-stationarity. Keep it as short as your hardware allows, and handle it the same way across conditions.
- **Consistency across conditions.** If you compare conditions or sessions, run ICA on concatenated data, or at least apply identical selection criteria, so artefact removal does not differ systematically between conditions.
- **Transparency.** Report the ICA algorithm, the number of components removed in each round (mean and range per participant), the selection criteria (manual, semi-automatic or automatic), and whether the selection was blind to condition. Show TEPs before and after removal, at least in supplementary material.
- **Sensitivity check.** Pipeline choice affects TEP amplitude and shape (Bertazzoli et al., 2021, among others). For key results, consider checking robustness with another pipeline, such as a single-ICA version or SOUND + SSP-SIR (Mutanen et al.), followed by ICA for ocular artefacts only.

## Alternatives to the two-ICA approach

- **SOUND + SSP-SIR (+ ICA for eye artefacts).** SOUND suppresses channel-specific noise. SSP-SIR suppresses the TMS-evoked muscle artefact using a spatial projection with source-informed reconstruction. A single ICA is then used mainly for ocular components. This is a good alternative when muscle artefacts are large and you want a method that does not rely on ICA independence assumptions for the muscle artefact.
- **Fully automated pipelines** (e.g., ARTIST, or automated component classification on top of TESA). These are useful for large datasets and reproducibility, but check the automated decisions on a subset of data.
- **Decay-artefact fitting** (e.g., exponential fit and subtraction). This is an option if a strong decay artefact persists after ICA.

## Recommendation

- **Large TMS-evoked muscle artefact present:** use two rounds. Use round 1 conservatively, for the muscle artefact only. Use round 2 for ocular, EMG, electrode and other residual artefacts.
- **Minimal muscle artefact:** a single post-filtering ICA is adequate and simpler.
- **In either case:** interpret early TEP components carefully, control sensory confounds experimentally rather than with ICA, and report component removal in full.
