# Auditory masking in TMS-EEG: setup and verification

## Why it matters

Every TMS pulse makes a loud, broadband click. It can exceed 100 dB SPL near the coil and reaches the cochlea by two routes: through the air and through the skull (bone conduction). The click evokes an auditory evoked potential (AEP) with a fronto-central/vertex N1 at about 100 ms and P2 at about 180–200 ms. This overlaps the late TEP components (N100, P180) and can be mistaken for them. Masking aims to stop the click from being perceived, so that no AEP is evoked. Removing the AEP afterwards with ICA, regression or similar methods is much less reliable than preventing it.

**Assumptions:** standard figure-of-eight coil, single pulses over a cortical target, 64-channel or similar EEG system, healthy adult participants with normal hearing.

---

## Part 1 – Setting it up

### 1. Choose the masking signal
- **Use noise shaped to the click, not plain white noise.** White noise spreads energy across frequencies the click does not use. You then need a louder overall level to mask it.
- **Good options:**
  - Noise built from the recorded click of *your* coil at *your* intensity, for example by scrambling the click's phase while keeping its spectrum, then mixing in white noise.
  - The open-source TAAC approach (TMS Adaptable Auditory Control). It builds noise from the click's spectrum and adds time-varying features so the click does not "pop out."
- **Make a new mask whenever the click changes.** Click spectrum and loudness depend on the coil model, pulse waveform (mono- vs biphasic), intensity and coil–head contact. A mask tuned for one coil or intensity may fail with another.

### 2. Deliver it with the right hardware
- **Use in-ear insert earphones** with foam tips (pneumatic/tube type). They keep the transducer away from the coil, which avoids TMS-induced artifacts in the earphone and magnetic interference with the EEG. The foam tips also work as earplugs.
- **Optionally add earmuffs** or passive ear defenders over the inserts. They cut air-conducted click further, if they fit with the coil and cap.
- **Avoid large dynamic headphones near the coil.** They can pick up the TMS pulse, the coil may displace them, and they add pressure on the electrodes.

### 3. Reduce the bone-conducted click at its source
- **Put a thin foam layer (about 0.5 cm) between coil and cap.** It damps the vibration transmitted to the skull, and it also reduces scalp pressure and somatosensory input.
- **Know the cost.** The foam increases coil-to-cortex distance. Determine the motor threshold or TEP-based intensity *with the foam in place*, and keep its thickness constant across sessions.
- **Masking noise also covers bone conduction.** Both routes end at the cochlea, so the noise masks bone-conducted sound too. Earplugs only block the air route.

### 4. Set the level per participant
1. **Start the noise early.** Turn it on before the stimulation blocks begin and give the participant a minute or two to adapt. Keep it playing *continuously* through each block, not gated per trial, so that noise onsets do not create their own ERPs.
2. **Stimulate at the real setup.** Coil over the actual target, at the actual experimental intensity and orientation, with the foam spacer in place.
3. **Raise the noise in steps** while delivering pulses at irregular intervals. Each time, ask the participant whether they can still hear the click, for example with a yes/no answer or a 0–10 loudness rating.
4. **Stop at the lowest level where the click is no longer perceived**, or at your safety ceiling, whichever comes first.
5. **Respect a safety ceiling.** Calibrate the earphone output with a sound level meter and ear coupler. Many labs keep the masking noise at or below about 90 dB(A) and limit total exposure time. Follow local occupational-noise and ethics rules. Take a hearing/tinnitus history and exclude or adapt for participants with hearing problems.
6. **If the click is still audible at the ceiling**, do not just turn the noise up. Improve the earplug fit, add earmuffs, check the foam, or reconsider intensity. Record that participant as "incompletely masked" and report it.
7. **Repeat the check** whenever the target site, coil, or intensity changes, and briefly at the start of each block, because earplugs loosen over time.

### 5. Minimise expectation effects
- **Jitter the inter-stimulus interval** (for example 2–3 s randomised) so the participant cannot anticipate pulses. Anticipation produces its own slow potentials and attention effects.
- **Keep the participant's state stable:** eyes open, fixation, low arousal changes. Loud noise is fatiguing, so plan breaks.

## Part 2 – Checking that it worked

Use several converging checks. No single check is sufficient on its own.

### A. Perceptual checks (every participant)
- **Ratings during setup:** document the final noise level and the participant's click-audibility rating at that level.
- **Per-block ratings:** short ratings of click loudness, *and separately* of scalp sensation (tapping, muscle twitch). Somatosensory input also evokes vertex N100/P200-like potentials, and auditory masking does not touch it.
- **End-of-session questionnaire:** did they hear the click, how loud, how often, discomfort, and fatigue.
- **Use ratings as covariates** or as an exclusion criterion, and pre-register the cutoff, for example excluding participants who rated the click above a set value.

### B. Online EEG monitoring during acquisition
- **Look at a running average** (for example 20–40 trials) in real time while you adjust coil and masking.
- **What a good local TEP looks like:** clear early (<50 ms) responses near the stimulated site, with an asymmetric topography that follows the target.
- **Warning signs of residual sensory responses:** a large, symmetric, vertex-centred negativity at about 100 ms followed by a positivity at about 180–200 ms, especially with weak early components. Typical locations are Cz/FCz.
- **If you see these signs,** recheck the earplug fit, raise the noise if you are under the ceiling, and re-check perception before continuing.

### C. Control conditions (strongly recommended)
1. **Click-only / sham control.** Fire the coil at the same intensity with the masking noise on, but without effective brain stimulation:
   - Option 1: a sham coil.
   - Option 2: the active coil tilted 90° or lifted off the head with a thick spacer. This produces the click but also changes the click's bone conduction and loudness, so it is an imperfect match.

   Little or no AEP in this condition is direct evidence that masking works. A realistic sham, meaning a sham coil plus weak electrical scalp stimulation to mimic the somatosensory input, controls both sensory confounds at once.
2. **Masked vs unmasked comparison (pilot or a short block).** Record a few dozen click-only trials *without* noise to confirm you can detect the AEP. Then record with noise, and show the AEP drops to baseline.
3. **Multiple stimulation sites or an active control site.** Responses that are identical across sites (same latency, same vertex topography) are likely sensory. Genuine TEPs should vary with the site.

### D. Offline quantitative checks
- **Compare N100/P200 amplitude** at Cz/FCz in an a priori window (for example 80–130 ms and 160–240 ms) between real TMS and the click-only/sham condition, and between masked and unmasked conditions. Use paired tests or equivalence tests if you want to claim the absence of an AEP.
- **Spatial correlation:** correlate the real-TMS topography and time course with the sham-evoked potential. High correlation in late windows suggests contamination.
- **Check single subjects, not only the group average.** One poorly masked participant can produce a group N100 that looks plausible.
- **Report everything:** noise type and how it was generated, delivery hardware, calibrated level in dB, foam thickness, earplugs/earmuffs, perceptual ratings, how many participants still heard the click, and the control-condition results.

## Common pitfalls
- **Reusing a white-noise level** from another study or lab without per-participant titration.
- **Titrating with the coil away from the head** or at a lower intensity than used in the experiment.
- **Assuming masking also removes somatosensory potentials.** It does not, so control them separately.
- **Noise switched on only at trial onset,** which creates onset ERPs.
- **Loose insert earphones** later in the session.
- **Relying on ICA or other post-hoc cleaning** to remove an AEP that masking should have prevented.

## Minimal workflow
1. Generate a click-matched mask for the coil and intensity.
2. Fit insert earphones and the foam spacer, and add earmuffs if feasible.
3. Turn on continuous noise, place the coil on the target, and titrate the noise until the click is inaudible or the safety ceiling is reached.
4. Check the online average for early local TEPs and no dominant vertex N100/P200.
5. Collect auditory and somatosensory ratings per block.
6. Include a sham or click-only control, and ideally a second site.
7. Analyse and report the vertex N100/P200 against the controls, per participant.
