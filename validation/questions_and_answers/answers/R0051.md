# Fixing coil pose over an MRI-defined DLPFC target: is it the right approach?

## Short answer

**Not quite.** Holding the pose constant once you have chosen it is good practice. Neuronavigation should keep the coil there and correct any drift. The problem is *which* pose you lock, and when you lock it:

1. **The 45° handle angle is a motor-cortex habit and is not an optimum for DLPFC.** A common best coil orientation across subjects is well supported only for M1 and S1. In frontal cortex, gyral folding differs enough between people that the best orientation varies by subject (Gomez-Tames et al. 2018). Using 45° at DLPFC is a default, not an anatomically optimal choice.
2. **Being on target anatomically does not mean the EEG will be usable.** DLPFC is the most artifact-prone of the commonly studied non-motor sites. If the coil sits on the right spot but produces a large early craniofacial muscle-artifact peak, loud clicks or strong scalp sensation, the early TEP may be uninterpretable. Preprocessing cannot reliably rescue that.
3. **Lock the pose only after online QC.** The recommended order is: set an initial pose from anatomy or an E-field model, check it with short online TEP averages, make small adjustments, then lock it. After that, keep it fixed and track deviations with neuronavigation.

So: **fix the coil for the main acquisition, yes. Skip the online optimisation step before it, no.**

---

## Why "anatomically optimal" is not the whole story

### The coordinate fixes position only, not orientation
An MNI coordinate such as (-38, 44, 26) sets 3 of the coil's 6 degrees of freedom (x, y, z). Roll and pitch usually follow from keeping the coil tangential to the scalp. **Yaw (handle angle) is still open**, and that is where 45° was filled in by convention. Effective stimulation depends on the induced current running roughly perpendicular to the local sulcal wall or gyral crown. At DLPFC, that direction differs from person to person.

### A group coordinate is not an individual optimum
- (-38, 44, 26) is a group-level coordinate transformed into each participant's MRI. The transform may land on a gyral crown in one person and a sulcal bank in another.
- The best coil centre is often not the scalp point nearest the cortical target. The offset is several mm (about 5.5 mm on average), and larger for deeper targets.
- If the coordinate is meant to stand for a functional region, an individual functional localiser restricted to an a priori ROI mask, combined with E-field modelling, is more defensible than a bare coordinate.

### DLPFC's expected artifact and sensory burden
In a large three-site comparison (prefrontal vs premotor vs parietal; Biabani et al. 2024), prefrontal stimulation had:
- residual muscle artifact surviving ICA in about **62% of individuals** (parietal: 0%)
- the **loudest perceived click**, despite the lowest stimulation intensity
- the highest twitch, discomfort and pain ratings, and the least effective white-noise masking
- the largest N100/P200, which partly reflects sensation rather than cortical response
- early-TEP topography that was unreliable in most individuals

Expect about two in three participants to have problematic early-window muscle contamination at this site. The main remedy is at acquisition: small coil rotations and position shifts, and intensity. Adjusting these before the main block is how you manage it.

## Recommended procedure

### Step 1: Choose the initial pose with a model, not a fixed angle
- Build an individual head model from T1 (+T2) and run E-field simulations at the target.
- Sweep coil yaw (for example 24 orientations in 15° steps) and pick the angle that maximises induced field in the target ROI, not just at a single voxel. If you use E-field-navigated hardware, the on-board real-time E-field display can do the same job.
- Set roll and pitch tangential to the scalp and allow for a few mm of offset from the nearest scalp point.
- Save the initial pose, the predicted E-field, and the 45° pose as a reference.
- If modelling is not available, say in the methods that 45° was a heuristic. Then rely more heavily on the online search in Step 2.

### Step 2: Online TEP/artifact check before the main acquisition
- Run short online averages (about 10–30 pulses) with real-time TEP display and masking already on.
- **Signal to watch:** the early muscle-artifact peak. This is a bipolar, MEP-like deflection after the pulse artifact, at latencies later than about 4 ms, focal over frontal, temporal, jaw or facial channels. At DLPFC it is often the only time-locked activity visible in a short average, and it can hide the smaller early cortical TEP. **It is separate from any twitch the participant feels.** "No twitch, comfortable participant" does not mean the early window is clean.
- **Search:** rotate the coil in small steps (for example ±10–20° around the model-derived yaw). Try small position shifts (a few mm) within the target region, and adjust intensity within your dose rule. At each setting, compare:
  - size of the early muscle-artifact peak
  - early local TEP near the target, roughly 8–50 ms (a working setup aim is about 8–10 µV peak-to-peak; this is a practical guide, not a hard threshold)
  - topography (local and plausible vs focal over facial or temporal muscle)
  - pulse, decay or recharge artifacts
  - late N100/P200 size relative to the early response
  - participant discomfort, click and sensation ratings
- **Warning sign:** if late N100/P200 is large but the early local response is weak, suspect sensory contamination, a target mismatch, or too little effective stimulation. This is not a sign of good engagement.
- Don't overfit to noisy previews. Prefer settings that are consistently better over a couple of short blocks.

### Step 3: Stopping rule and lock
- **Go:** the early window is interpretable for your endpoint (muscle peak small enough, or limited to a pre-specified, short, excludable interval), with no dominant decay or pulse artifact. Masking must also be acceptable, verified by a small or absent N100/P200 in the EEG and not just by the participant's report.
- **Then lock** position, orientation, tilt and intensity. Record the final pose and how far it is from the planned pose (mm, degrees).
- **Retune auditory masking** after any change in intensity, orientation or position. Titrate it with real TMS, not sham.
- **No-go fallback:** if no pose within the target region gives an interpretable early window, use a pre-specified backup (a more medial or posterior DLPFC site, or a lower intensity). Otherwise, limit the endpoint to measures that do not rely on the contaminated early window, and state this.

### Step 4: During the session, keep the pose fixed but actively held
"Fixed" should mean the neuronavigated pose is actively maintained, not that the coil is left alone:
- Monitor navigated error continuously. Set tolerances in advance (for example ≤2–3 mm displacement and ≤5° angular deviation). Re-align when they are exceeded, between pulses or between blocks.
- Log deviations per block or per pulse if the system supports it, so they can be reported or used as covariates.
- Check head registration between blocks and re-register if the tracker or headband has moved.
- Watch the online average throughout. If artifacts change (for example electrode drift or cap movement under the coil), fix the cause before continuing.
- For multi-session or between-subject comparisons, reuse the same individually optimised pose. Do not re-derive it from the group coordinate each time.

## Controls you still need, whatever the coil pose
- **Auditory:** a masking noise tuned to each participant's click, plus ear defenders or a foam spacer as appropriate. Verify masking in the EEG.
- **Somatosensory:** a realistic sham or sensory-control condition. Collect click-loudness, scalp-sensation, twitch and discomfort ratings, especially if you will compare TEP amplitudes across sites, groups or sessions.
- **Muscle:** pre-register how the earliest interval will be handled (for example a muscle vs no-muscle subgroup check on uncleaned epochs). Do not build a primary endpoint on samples you expect to interpolate or remove.

---

## Reporting checklist
- Target definition: MNI coordinate, how it was transformed to individual space, and the final individual coordinate
- Coil model, **current direction**, handle angle, tilt, and how orientation was chosen (E-field sweep, online search, or the 45° heuristic)
- Planned vs final pose and the reason for any change
- Intensity decision chain: starting reference (RMT and/or E-field), effect of online TEP and artifact checks, final value
- Navigation tolerances, registration quality, and recorded deviations during acquisition
- Online QC procedure and go/no-go criteria
- Masking, sham or sensory controls, and sensory ratings

---

## Assumptions
- Single-pulse TMS-EEG with TEPs as the endpoint, healthy adults, a figure-of-eight coil, and a navigation system that can track pose in real time.
- If this is an rTMS intervention where the EEG is secondary, the case for an individualised orientation and continuous navigated holding still applies. The early-TEP optimisation step matters less, but artifact and sensory checks are still needed if any EEG outcome will be interpreted.

**Bottom line:** keep the coil fixed during acquisition, but only after optimising the pose for this participant and this signal. For DLPFC, a group-level coordinate with a 45° handle is a reasonable starting point, not a validated end point. Pick the orientation from individual anatomy or the E-field model, refine it with short online TEP checks aimed at minimising the early muscle-artifact peak, then lock it and hold it actively with neuronavigation.
