# Is the 5–10 ms biphasic deflection after DLPFC TMS an early cortical response?

## Short answer

**Probably not, and I would not report it as P5/N10 components.** A large biphasic deflection at about 5–10 ms that is strongest over frontotemporal channels after DLPFC stimulation is the classic pattern of a **TMS-evoked cranial muscle artifact**. The pulse depolarises the temporalis and frontalis muscles (and possibly branches of the facial and trigeminal nerves) under and next to the coil. Until you have positive evidence that it is neural, call it an early artifact, not a TEP component.

**Assumptions:** a figure-of-eight coil over left DLPFC (around F3/F5/AF3), single biphasic pulses, and an online average with little processing (no ICA or SSP; maybe baseline correction or a display filter).

---

## Why this looks like muscle and not cortex

| Feature you describe | Muscle artifact | Genuine early TEP |
|---|---|---|
| Latency ~5–10 ms, biphasic | Typical: compound muscle action potentials peak at roughly 5–7 ms and 10–12 ms with opposite polarities | Cortical deflections this early are hard to measure reliably. At DLPFC the first well-described peaks are usually N15–P30, then N40/N45, P60, N100, P180–P200 |
| "Large" | Often tens to hundreds of µV, sometimes mV, and can be many times larger than the later TEP | Early TEPs are usually a few µV, up to roughly 10–20 µV |
| Strongest over **frontotemporal** channels | These channels sit over the temporalis and frontalis muscles. DLPFC targets are close to temporalis, which makes this a known problem site | An early local response should peak near the target, with a smooth dipolar topography. It should not be confined to the lateral edge of the montage |
| Shape | Sharp, high-frequency, steep slopes. Often stereotyped across trials | Smoother and lower frequency |

Other non-neural sources can add to or mimic the deflection:
- **The tail of the pulse artifact or amplifier recovery.** This happens especially if the online display uses high-pass or other filters, which can turn a step or decay into a spurious biphasic transient.
- **Electrode movement or the coil pressing on nearby electrodes.**
- **Decay artifacts** from electrode–gel polarisation. These are usually slower and more monophasic, but they can overlap.

None of these is "the early local cortical response."

## How to check it (ideally during the session)

1. **Move or rotate the coil.** Change the handle angle (for example, step through orientations) and/or shift the target slightly medially or dorsally, away from temporalis. A muscle artifact changes amplitude dramatically with small changes in position and orientation. A cortical response changes more gradually.
2. **Change intensity.** Muscle artifacts often grow steeply above a threshold and may be absent at lower intensity.
3. **Look at single trials and the raw, unfiltered signal.** Muscle artifacts are huge and consistent trial by trial. Make sure the online display filters are not creating the biphasic shape.
4. **Inspect the topography and the time course in channels near the coil versus channels farther away.** A focal maximum on the lateral frontotemporal electrodes, with a steep spatial falloff and polarity reversal around the muscle, points to muscle.
5. **Ask the participant and watch the face.** A jaw clench, eyebrow twitch or eyelid twitch with each pulse is a strong hint. Optional extra check: record temporalis or frontalis EMG with a bipolar pair.
6. **Check whether the later TEP is visible and plausible** in channels near the target, for example in the first ~50 ms. Real-time TEP monitoring practice focuses on maximising a clear early response near the target while keeping muscle artifact minimal. It does not treat the artifact as the response.

## What to do about it

**At acquisition (best option):** adjust coil orientation, position or tilt, and if needed intensity, until the 5–10 ms deflection is small and an early cortical response is visible near the target. Document the final parameters and how large the remaining artifact is.

**Offline (if it stays):**
- Use methods designed for TMS-evoked muscle artifacts:
  - ICA, often in two rounds: one for the large early artifact, then one for the remaining artifacts.
  - SSP-SIR, which was built specifically for muscle artifacts.
  - SOUND, for noise and bad channels.
  - Enhanced deflation or beamforming-type approaches.
- Often you will also need to cut and interpolate a window, for example the first ~10–15 ms after the pulse.
- Be explicit that this **removes or distorts any genuine signal in that window**. Any cortical signal that was hidden under the artifact cannot be recovered with confidence.
- Report:
  - the removal window
  - the number of components rejected
  - how sensitive the later peaks (N15–P30, N45, P60, N100) are to cleaning choices.

## On the naming "P5/N10"

- There is no established P5/N10 TEP convention for DLPFC (or for M1). Polarity-plus-latency labels imply a physiological component, and reviewers will read them that way.
- If after cleaning and the checks above you still see a reproducible early deflection that is:
  - dipolar,
  - centred near the target, and
  - insensitive to coil-orientation tweaks that clearly change the muscle artifact,
  
  then you can describe it descriptively (polarity, latency, channels, reference). Present it as a tentative early response with explicit caveats about residual artifact.
- As it stands, it is more accurate to describe it as an **"early TMS-evoked muscle artifact (~5–10 ms) over frontotemporal channels"** and to report the cortical TEP from the first credible components onward.

## Bottom line

A large biphasic frontotemporal deflection at 5–10 ms after DLPFC TMS is almost certainly mainly a cranial muscle artifact, possibly mixed with pulse or recovery artifacts. Do not label it P5/N10. Reduce it during acquisition by adjusting the coil, remove it offline with documented artifact-specific methods, and base your interpretation on later, physiologically plausible TEP components.
