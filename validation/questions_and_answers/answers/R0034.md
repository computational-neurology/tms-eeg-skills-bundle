# Comparing occipital vs premotor P200: test choice and the bigger problem

## Short answer

Because the same participants received both stimulations, the comparison is **within-subject**. Mechanically, that means a **paired test**: a paired t-test (or Wilcoxon signed-rank if the paired differences are clearly non-normal or N is small) on per-participant P200 amplitudes. A better option is a **paired cluster-based permutation test** across channels × time, or a **linear mixed model** with site as a fixed effect and participant as a random effect.

**But no statistical test will show that "occipital cortex is more responsive" from these data.** A larger P200 after occipital stimulation fits several explanations that have nothing to do with cortical excitability. The test can only show that the *recorded signals differ*. It cannot tell you *why*. Below I explain why the claim doesn't hold as stated, and what you could test and claim instead.

---

## 1. Why the P200 is the worst component for this claim

- **The P200 is mostly sensory, not TMS-specific.** In TMS-EEG, the late N100–P200 complex is strongly driven by the auditory response to the coil click and the somatosensory response to scalp stimulation. These peripheral evoked potentials have a vertex-maximal (central) topography regardless of where you stimulate. Without good masking and a site-matched sham, much of your P200 may not come from the stimulated cortex at all.
- **Sensory input differs between sites.** Occipital and premotor stimulation differ in scalp sensation, muscle recruitment (neck/occipital muscles vs temporal/frontal muscles), discomfort, coil–ear distance and how loud the click is through bone conduction. Any of these can change the size of a sensory-driven P200, independently of cortical responsiveness.
- **Topography is not comparable.** The "P200" after occipital and premotor stimulation may come from different generators and peak at different electrodes. Comparing amplitude at one electrode (or at each site's "local" electrode) mixes up *where* the signal is with *how big* it is.

## 2. Confounds between sites (beyond the sensory issue)

| Confound | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Dose / intensity** | How was intensity set? %MSO, % of RMT (an M1 measure, not valid for occipital or premotor), phosphene threshold, or E-field-based dosing? Equal %MSO does **not** give equal cortical stimulation. |
| **Induced E-field** | Coil–cortex distance, skull thickness, CSF and gyral geometry differ between occipital and premotor regions. Without E-field modelling (e.g., SimNIBS) you cannot tell whether occipital cortex got more effective stimulation. |
| **Coil orientation** | Current direction relative to sulcal geometry strongly changes response size. Optimal orientation differs by region. |
| **Electrode proximity and reference** | The distance between the generator and the electrodes, plus the reference choice (average reference vs mastoid/linked), changes recorded amplitude differently for posterior and frontal sources. |
| **Artifact handling** | ICA/SSP-SIR/SOUND or decay removal may have removed different amounts of signal per site, e.g., more muscle components with premotor/temporal-adjacent stimulation. Report the number of rejected components, trials and channels per site and check that they don't differ systematically. |
| **Order/time effects** | Session order, fatigue, earplug/noise-masking fit and arousal can differ between blocks. Counterbalancing is required. |

## 3. Statistical pitfalls to avoid

- **Double dipping.** Don't pick the electrode or time window where the occipital–premotor difference looks largest and then test it. Define the ROI/window in advance, define it from an orthogonal contrast (e.g., the grand average of both conditions pooled), or use a cluster-based permutation test over the full channel × time space.
- **Raw amplitude vs normalised measures.** If you compare amplitudes, consider global mean field power (GMFP), which doesn't depend on a single electrode or the reference. For responsiveness questions, also consider early local measures such as local mean field power at the stimulated site in an early window (~15–80 ms).
- **Report effect sizes and CIs** (e.g., paired Cohen's d_z with 95% CI), not just p-values.

## 4. What I would actually do

1. **Recast the question.** Instead of "is occipital cortex more responsive?", ask "do TEPs differ between sites after controlling for sensory input and stimulation dose?"
2. **Use site-specific sham/sensory control conditions** (realistic sham with matched click and scalp stimulation, or electrical scalp stimulation plus masked click) at each site. The main comparison then becomes the **site × condition (real vs sham) interaction**: is the real-minus-sham difference larger for occipital than for premotor? A 2 × 2 repeated-measures ANOVA, a mixed model, or a cluster-based permutation test on the difference-of-differences tests this.
3. **Focus on early components** (roughly < 60–80 ms), which reflect local cortical activation better than the P200, and are less contaminated by peripheral evoked potentials. Report the P200 too, but interpret it cautiously.
4. **Control for dose.** Model the E-field for each site and participant. Either dose by E-field from the start, or include E-field magnitude at the target as a covariate in a mixed model, e.g.:
   `amplitude ~ site + Efield + sensory_rating + (1 | participant)`
   Collect perceived loudness, scalp sensation and discomfort ratings per site and include them as covariates.
5. **Check masking.** Confirm that noise masking and foam/thin-layer padding were applied equally at both sites. A clear N100/P200 at central electrodes in both conditions is a warning sign.
6. **Show the data.** Plot butterfly plots, GMFP and topographies for both sites, with sham traces overlaid if you have them. Readers and reviewers will want to see whether the "P200" has a central (sensory) or site-local topography.

## 5. If you only have the current data (no sham, no E-field)

- Run a paired cluster-based permutation test (or paired t-test on a pre-defined ROI/window) and report it as **"TEP amplitude in the P200 window differed between stimulation sites"**, not as greater cortical responsiveness.
- Explicitly discuss the sensory-confound, dose and E-field limitations.
- If you can, compare topographies (e.g., a topographic dissimilarity test / TANOVA) to show whether the two P200s even share a generator configuration.

## Assumptions

- Both sites were recorded with the same EEG setup, reference and preprocessing pipeline, with comparable trial counts.
- "Twice as large" refers to the group-average peak amplitude at some chosen electrode(s). I assumed no site-specific sham or E-field modelling was done, because the question doesn't mention them.
- Sample size is typical for TMS-EEG (about 15–30 participants). With very small N, use a non-parametric or permutation approach.
