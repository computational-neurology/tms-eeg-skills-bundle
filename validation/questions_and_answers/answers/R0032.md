# Large biphasic deflection at ~5-10 ms after DLPFC TMS: cortical response or artifact?

## Short answer

**Probably not, and I would not report it as P5/N10.** A large biphasic deflection at about 5-10 ms that is **strongest over frontotemporal channels** after DLPFC stimulation fits the typical picture of the **early craniofacial muscle-artifact peak**, not the early local cortical TEP. In a low-trial online average at a lateral frontal target, this peak is often the *only* time-locked activity you can see in the early window. The real early local response is usually smaller and hidden underneath it.

Calling it "P5/N10" would label it as a neural component. Your data do not support that yet, and the description points the other way.

## Why this looks like muscle and not cortex

| Feature you describe | Muscle-artifact peak | Early local cortical TEP at DLPFC |
|---|---|---|
| Latency ~5-10 ms, just after the pulse artifact | Typical: it appears after the pulse artifact, later than ~4 ms | Reported DLPFC early peaks come later (e.g., an N40 at prefrontal sites; P20/N45 premotor, P20/N35 parietal) |
| **Large** amplitude | Typical: often much larger than cortical TEPs | Early local TEP at DLPFC tends to be **weak** |
| **Biphasic**, MEP-like shape | Typical: brief biphasic EMG-like burst | Not the expected shape |
| Strongest over **frontotemporal** channels | Typical: focal and dipolar near temporalis, frontalis, jaw and facial muscles | Expected near the target channels (e.g., F3/AF3/F5 for left DLPFC) with a broader field |
| Clean-looking in the average | Time-locked muscle activity looks *more* convincing after averaging | — |

Some context on the size of the problem: in the largest sensory-confound dataset to date (n = 135; prefrontal, premotor and parietal sites), **about 62% of individuals stimulated over prefrontal cortex still had muscle artifact after ICA**, compared with 34.5% at premotor and 0% at parietal sites. Prefrontal was the worst site on every artifact and sensory measure. A frontotemporal biphasic peak at 5-10 ms is the expected finding at this site, not an unusual one.

One common trap: **the participant not reporting or showing a twitch is not evidence that the peak is cortical.** A felt facial or scalp twitch is a comfort issue. The early EEG muscle peak can be large even when the participant feels nothing.

## Other possibilities to rule out

- **Decay artifact.** This is an exponential-like return to baseline after the pulse artifact, caused by charge at the electrode-skin-gel interface or by lead and cable effects. It is usually smooth and sloped rather than sharply biphasic, and it is often confined to channels with poor contact or near the coil or cables. It can still overlap the first 10 ms.
- **Residual pulse artifact or amplifier recovery.** This is less likely at 5-10 ms with a good setup. But if the pulse artifact looks stretched well beyond the first few ms, check sampling rate, filters, synchronization and recharge timing.
- **Genuine early cortical activity.** It could be mixed in, but you cannot separate it from the peak by looking at the average alone.

## How to tell them apart

Do these checks now, during setup, while you can still change the setup:

1. **Single trials.** Muscle shows up as high-frequency EMG-like bursts or sharp biphasic transients in individual trials, usually in the same frontotemporal channels. Do not judge from the average alone.
2. **Topography at the peak.** A very focal dipole at the edge of the montage (lateral frontal/temporal, above temporalis or near the orbit or jaw) points to muscle. A field centred near the stimulated channels with a plausible spread fits cortex better.
3. **Small coil rotations and position shifts** (a few degrees, a few mm), with intensity held constant. A muscle peak usually changes a lot in size, and often in shape, with small moves that bring the coil closer to or further from temporalis/frontalis. A cortical response should change much more gradually.
4. **Intensity series.** Muscle artifact often grows steeply and nonlinearly once a muscle is recruited.
5. **Current direction** (if your stimulator allows it). A deflection that flips polarity exactly with pulse direction is suspect for pulse or decay residue.
6. **Extra EMG or EOG channels** over temporalis, frontalis or orbicularis oculi, if available, so you can confirm that the peak is time-locked muscle activity.
7. **Contact and leads.** If the deflection is limited to one or two channels and looks smooth and exponential, re-gel, check impedance, and reroute the cables roughly perpendicular to the coil handle.

## What to do with the session

- **Use the peak as your tuning signal.** Rotate and shift the coil (and lower intensity if your dosing allows) to **minimise this early biphasic peak**. Don't judge by whether the participant is comfortable. A more medial or posterior DLPFC position, away from temporalis, often helps. Set orientation from individual anatomy or an E-field model rather than a default 45°.
- **Go/no-go rule.** Continue only once the early window you plan to analyse is not dominated by this peak. If it can't be reduced and your endpoint depends on early local responses, think about a backup target (e.g., a more medial frontal site). Do not plan to rescue it with ICA afterwards. At prefrontal sites ICA often leaves residual muscle in, and an aggressive ICA can also remove real early signal.
- **Check the late window too.** A weak early local response alongside a large N100/P200 at DLPFC points to sensory (auditory/somatosensory) contamination. Collect separate 0-10 ratings of click loudness, scalp twitch, discomfort and pain after each block. Also tune masking again after any change in coil position or intensity.
- **Log everything.** Keep screenshots or notes of the online average and topography before and after each adjustment, with coil coordinates, orientation, intensity and masking settings. These belong in your methods.

## How to report it

- **Do not name it P5/N10** or describe it as an early local cortical response. If you report it, describe it neutrally as an "early (≈5-10 ms) biphasic deflection with frontotemporal maximum, consistent with craniofacial muscle artifact".
- If early TEPs are an endpoint, state in advance:
  - how you identify muscle-contaminated participants on **uncleaned** epochs
  - a check of the muscle vs no-muscle subgroup (to show the early effect survives without muscle)
  - which samples are removed or interpolated, and that no endpoint depends on those samples
- Name early components only for peaks that hold up after this check, using the latencies expected for prefrontal stimulation (e.g., around 40 ms). Even then, do not present early TEP amplitude as a pure measure of cortical excitability.

## Assumptions

I assumed single-pulse TMS over left or right DLPFC with a figure-of-eight coil, standard sampling (≥ 5 kHz), a pulse artifact that ends within the first few ms, and a 10-30 trial online average. If your pulse or decay artifact lasts well past 5 ms, deal with the hardware, contact and synchronization issues first, because those would take priority over the muscle explanation.
