# Short answer: no, not as stated

The 0.31 cut-off does not apply to PCIst. It belongs to a different metric with a different scale. So comparing a PCIst of 0.25 against 0.31 does not tell you whether this patient is below the consciousness threshold.

## 1. 0.31 is the cut-off for PCI (Lempel–Ziv), not PCIst

- **0.31** is the empirical cut-off (PCI\*) from the large benchmark study by Casarotto et al. (2016). That study used the **original PCI**, which Casarotto et al. call **PCI^LZ**. This is the Lempel–Ziv complexity of the binarised, source-modelled, statistically thresholded TMS-evoked activity, normalised by source entropy. That PCI is bounded roughly between 0 and 1.
- **PCIst** (Comolatti et al., 2019) is a different algorithm. It works in sensor space and uses dimensionality reduction (PCA/SVD) and state-transition recurrence analysis. It sums the change in the number of state transitions (ΔNST) across the significant principal components. Its values are **not normalised to 0–1** and sit on a completely different numerical scale. Typical values in awake subjects are in the tens.
- PCIst correlates strongly with PCI^LZ and separates conscious from unconscious conditions about as well. But the numbers cannot be swapped, and the 0.31 threshold cannot be carried over. Any PCIst cut-off has to come from a PCIst-specific calibration on a benchmark population. Use one recorded with the same pipeline and parameters as yours.

## 2. A PCIst of 0.25 is itself a warning sign

On the standard PCIst scale, 0.25 is extremely low. It usually means one of these:
- Almost no components passed the signal-to-noise criterion. This can happen with a low-amplitude or absent early response, ineffective stimulation, or a strict SNR/variance threshold.
- The response was dominated by a stereotyped, low-complexity wave, as in deep NREM sleep or propofol anaesthesia.
- A **parameter, scaling or implementation issue**. Examples: a different baseline/response window, wrong units, a rescaled or "normalised" variant, or too few trials.

Before you interpret the value, check:
1. Exactly which PCIst implementation and parameters you used (baseline window, response window, e.g. 0–300 ms, max variance retained, minimum SNR, number of trials averaged).
2. That the TEP has a clear early (<50 ms) local response with adequate amplitude near the target. In the original benchmark practice, this was roughly ≥10 µV peak-to-peak.
3. That artefacts are adequately removed and not driving or suppressing the result. Check TMS pulse and decay artefacts, muscle, and auditory/somatosensory evoked components.

## 3. Even with the right metric, a single value needs context

Assume you had the correct PCI^LZ value or a properly calibrated PCIst cut-off. A few more points still apply:
- **Benchmark conditions.** The 0.31 threshold was validated under specific acquisition conditions. These were high-density EEG (~60 channels, so your montage is fine on that front), effective stimulation (estimated cortical E-field above about 120 V/m), noise masking, artefact rejection, and source modelling for PCI^LZ.
- **Multiple stimulation sites.** Patient-level classification uses the **maximum PCI across several cortical targets** (typically 2–4 sites, e.g. frontal and parietal, in both hemispheres). One low value from one site can underestimate the patient's capacity for complexity, for example if that site is lesioned, or if stimulation was ineffective or poorly oriented.
- **Type of sedation matters.** "Sedated" is not the same as "unconscious":
  - Propofol, midazolam and xenon at doses that cause unresponsiveness typically give low complexity.
  - **Ketamine** anaesthesia gives high, wake-like PCI despite unresponsiveness. This fits with vivid dream-like experiences reported afterwards.
  - Light or conscious sedation, where the patient is still responsive or can be roused, may leave PCI in the conscious range.
  
  Interpret the value against the agent, the dose or effect-site concentration, and the clinical depth of sedation at the time of recording.
- **What the cut-off measures.** A low value under sedation reflects reduced capacity for integrated, differentiated responses *in that state*. It says nothing about the patient's capacity for consciousness off sedation. In a disorders-of-consciousness context, PCI should be measured without sedative confounds, or at least with them documented.
- **Uncertainty.** Values near any threshold should be read with some uncertainty, for example from repeat sessions or bootstrap over trials. They should not be treated as a hard binary.

## Assumptions

- "PCIst" here means the state-transition PCI from Comolatti et al. (2019), computed on sensor-level TMS-evoked potentials.
- The 0.31 value you quoted is the PCI\* cut-off from Casarotto et al. (2016) for the original Lempel–Ziv PCI.

## Recommendation

- **To use the 0.31 cut-off:** compute **PCI^LZ** with source modelling and the standard statistical thresholding, from at least two effective stimulation sites, and take the maximum.
- **To keep using PCIst:** compare your value to a PCIst cut-off calibrated on a benchmark dataset of conscious and unconscious conditions, processed with your exact pipeline and parameters.
- **Either way:** first check that the 0.25 value is not caused by a weak response or a parameter or implementation problem. Record the sedative agent and depth before drawing any conclusion about this patient's level of consciousness.
