# Fixing coil position at an MRI-defined DLPFC target: is it the right approach?

## Short answer

Only partly. You should keep the coil stable during the session. But fixing it at the MRI coordinate with a 45° handle **before checking the EEG response** is not recommended for TMS-EEG. A target that is right anatomically can still give a poor TMS-evoked potential (TEP). It may produce a small or missing early response, large cranial-muscle artefacts, or strong sensory contamination. Prefrontal targets are especially prone to this.

The approach now widely recommended has two parts:

1. **Before the session:** use real-time TEP readouts to tune position, orientation and intensity around the anatomical target.
2. **During the session:** lock in that tuned setup and keep it stable with neuronavigation.

## Why the anatomical target alone is not enough

- **Coordinate ≠ effective stimulation.** MNI (-38, 44, 26) is a group-level coordinate (the commonly used "Fox" DLPFC coordinate). Once it is warped into the individual brain, it marks a scalp/cortex location only. It does not tell you whether the induced field is well aligned with the local gyral geometry, or how deep the cortex lies under the coil there.
- **Orientation matters as much as position.** "Handle 45° posterior to the midline" is a convention taken from motor cortex. At the DLPFC, the field that actually reaches cortex depends on the individual sulcal anatomy. Rotating the coil by 30–45° can change early TEP amplitude a lot. The best orientation differs between people.
- **Muscle artefacts are the main practical problem at the DLPFC.** Lateral and anterior prefrontal sites lie over the temporalis and frontalis muscles. A setup that stimulates these muscles produces large artefacts in the first ~5–20 ms that can saturate or swamp the early TEP. Often a small shift (a few mm medially/superiorly) or a rotation removes the artefact while still reaching the same cortical region.
- **Sensory confounds.** Coil position and angle change how much the scalp is stimulated, and so how large the somatosensory evoked components are. The click must also be masked (noise masking plus a thin foam layer). Without this, late components (around N100/P200) can be mostly sensory rather than cortical.
- **Response size.** Prefrontal TEPs are smaller than motor ones. Without online checking, you may record a full session and only find out afterwards that the early response could not be told apart from noise.

## Recommended procedure

### 1. Setup / optimisation phase (about 10–20 min)

- Start at the neuronavigated target with your default orientation and intensity.
- Average a short block of pulses (about 20) and look at the early response (roughly 15–50 ms) in channels under and near the coil, with a fast real-time display (rt-TEP approach).
- Aim for:
  - a clear early peak-to-peak response, with roughly ≥6–10 µV in the 20-trial average as a practical benchmark;
  - minimal or no muscle artefact;
  - no obvious sensory or auditory contamination. Check that the subject cannot hear the click under masking, and ask about scalp sensation.
- If the criteria are not met, change one thing at a time:
  - rotate the coil (for example in 15–45° steps);
  - make small displacements (≤1 cm, preferably towards medial or superior positions to avoid temporalis);
  - adjust intensity. An E-field-based dose (for example targeting ~100 V/m at the cortical target) is easier to defend across subjects than %RMT alone.
- Set in advance how far you may move from the anatomical target (for example ≤10 mm) so that you remain within DLPFC. Write this into your protocol or pre-registration.

### 2. Fix and record

- Save the optimised position, orientation and tilt in the neuronavigation system as the session target.
- Record the final coordinates, angle, intensity (stimulator output and, if available, estimated E-field) and the reason for any deviation from the planned target.

### 3. Keep it stable during the session

- Monitor deviation continuously. Typical tolerances are about ≤2–3 mm and ≤3–5°. Reposition when these are exceeded, ideally between blocks or using an automatic pause.
- Check regularly that the head tracker has not slipped and that coil pressure on the EEG cap is constant. Electrode thickness changes coil–cortex distance.
- Use a coil holder or robotic arm if available. Hand-holding is acceptable only with strict navigation feedback.
- Check TEP stability across blocks. A large drop in amplitude or a new artefact usually means the coil, cap or subject has moved.

## When a strictly fixed anatomical target can be justified

If the scientific question requires the exact coordinate, such as testing a specific connectivity-defined target or matching a therapeutic protocol, you may keep it. In that case, still:

- check the EEG response online;
- adjust orientation only, where possible;
- report the TEP quality obtained.

Data from a target that produces mainly artefacts or sensory responses cannot be interpreted as cortical TEPs, however accurate the anatomy.

## Assumptions

- This is a TEP study (single pulses or paired pulses with EEG readout), not an rTMS treatment session where EEG is secondary.
- A figure-of-eight coil and a navigation system with real-time deviation display are used.
- The MNI target is transformed into each participant's native MRI space.
