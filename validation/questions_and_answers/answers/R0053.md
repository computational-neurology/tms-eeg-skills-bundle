# Choosing stimulation intensity for DLPFC TMS-EEG: a brief guide

**Assumptions:** single-pulse (or paired-pulse) TMS-EEG over the left DLPFC in healthy adults or patients, with a figure-of-eight coil and a biphasic pulse. The outcome is TMS-evoked potentials (TEPs) and/or oscillatory responses. If you are delivering rTMS and using EEG as a readout, the logic below still applies to the probe pulses.

---

## 1. The core problem

DLPFC stimulation produces no MEP, so you have nothing at the target to tell you that the pulse is effective. Your dose should give a **genuine, reliable cortical response** while keeping **muscle artifacts and sensory (auditory/somatosensory) confounds** low. A higher intensity is not automatically better.

## 2. Options, from most common to most principled

| Approach | How | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|---|
| **% Resting motor threshold (RMT)** | Measure RMT at M1 (FDI/APB), then stimulate DLPFC at about 90–120% RMT (100–120% is most common in TEP work) | Standard, easy to compare with the literature, gives a safety anchor | M1 excitability does not transfer well to DLPFC. Scalp-to-cortex distance and gyral geometry differ between sites |
| **Distance-adjusted %RMT** | Correct the RMT for the difference in scalp-to-cortex distance between M1 and DLPFC (from MRI). A Stokes-type adjustment is roughly 3% MSO per mm | Cheap improvement on raw %RMT | Still assumes M1 and DLPFC have the same excitability |
| **E-field-based dosing** | Use neuronavigation with E-field modelling (e.g., SimNIBS, or the Nexstim built-in estimate) to deliver a fixed induced field (V/m) at the DLPFC target across participants | Equalises the physical dose at the target | Needs individual MRI. The "right" V/m value for DLPFC is not firmly established |
| **TEP-response-based dosing (recommended where feasible)** | Adjust intensity, coil position and orientation online while watching the averaged EEG response in real time, until there is a clear early TEP | Ensures an effective cortical response at the target in every participant | Needs real-time EEG display and some operator experience. Intensity then differs between participants and must be reported |

## 3. Practical recommended workflow

1. **Measure RMT** at M1 anyway. You need it for safety, for reporting and for comparison with other studies.
2. **Pick and fix the target** with neuronavigation, using an individual MRI if possible. Common choices are MNI/Beam F3 or an fMRI-/connectivity-based target. Prefer a slightly **more medial/anterior** spot when possible, because lateral DLPFC sites strongly activate the temporalis and frontalis muscles.
3. **Start around 90–100% RMT**, or at your E-field target if you have one, and record about 20–30 test pulses.
4. **Check the averaged response online:**
   - Look for a clear early TEP (about 15–50 ms) in electrodes under and near the coil. As a rule of thumb from real-time TEP approaches, aim for an early peak-to-peak response of roughly **≥ 6–10 µV** in the averaged trials.
   - Check the first ~15 ms for **large muscle artifacts**. If they appear, rotate the coil (often toward a more anterior-posterior or medial-lateral angle, whichever reduces the artifact), shift the coil slightly medially, or reduce intensity before increasing it again.
5. **Adjust in small steps** (about 2–5% MSO) until you get a clear early response without a dominating artifact. Stay within a sensible ceiling (typically ≤ ~120–130% RMT for single pulses) and within safety guidelines.
6. **Fix the final intensity** for the session and record it as %MSO, %RMT and, if available, the estimated E-field in V/m.

## 4. Control the sensory confound, especially at higher intensities

- TEP components later than about 60 ms, particularly the N100/P200 complex, scale with **click loudness and scalp sensation**. They partly reflect auditory and somatosensory evoked responses, not only direct cortical activation.
- Use **noise masking** matched to the coil click (ideally with the TMS click's own spectral profile) together with earplugs, and a **foam spacer** or thin layer under the coil to reduce bone conduction.
- Ask participants after the test pulses whether they can still hear the click and rate how strong the scalp sensation is. Raise the masking level, or accept a lower intensity, if needed.
- Include a **sensory/sham control condition**, such as a realistic sham, a control site, or sensory-matched stimulation. Without it, high-intensity DLPFC TEPs are hard to interpret.

## 5. Between participants and between sessions

- **Between participants:** individualising the dose (by E-field or TEP response) reduces variance more than a fixed %MSO. Report the method and the resulting range of intensities.
- **Between sessions (longitudinal or pre/post designs):** keep the dosing rule constant. For pre/post comparisons within a session, keep the **intensity and coil position fixed** using navigation. Re-dosing between pre and post would confound the effect you are trying to measure.
- If there are several arms, such as different targets or active vs. sham, decide in advance whether intensity is matched by %RMT, by E-field or by response, and justify that choice.

## 6. Safety and reporting checklist

- Follow current international TMS safety guidelines (screening, intensity limits, number of pulses). Single-pulse DLPFC TMS-EEG at these intensities is low risk.
- Report: device, coil type, pulse waveform and current direction, coil orientation, how the target was defined, %MSO, %RMT (and how RMT was measured), E-field estimate if available, inter-pulse interval and jitter, number of trials, masking procedure, and perceived sensation/loudness ratings.

---

**Bottom line:** use RMT as a safety and reporting anchor, not as your dosing principle. Where possible, dose to an **effective early cortical response at the DLPFC target** (real-time TEP monitoring, ideally supported by E-field modelling). Then minimise muscle artifacts through coil placement and orientation, control sensory confounds with masking and a proper control condition, and keep the dose fixed within each session.
