# Stimulation intensity for left-DLPFC TMS-EEG: is a fixed 110% RMT appropriate?

## Short answer

**Partly.** 110% RMT is safe for single-pulse TMS. It is common in DLPFC TMS-EEG papers and works as a **starting point**. As the **only** dosing rule, it is weaker than current TMS-EEG practice for three reasons:

1. **Motor threshold transfers poorly to prefrontal cortex.** RMT measures how excitable the corticospinal system is at the motor hotspot. It does not account for differences in coil-to-cortex distance, gyral geometry, or local excitability at the DLPFC. A given %RMT can therefore produce quite different cortical E-fields and TEP amplitudes over DLPFC from one participant to the next. Some participants will get weak or absent early TEPs, while others get large responses mixed with artefact.
2. **In TMS-EEG, the outcome is the EEG response, not a clinical effect.** The relevant question is whether the pulse produces a clear, reproducible early cortical response (roughly the first 50 ms) with little contamination. RMT does not tell you that. Current recommendations favour adjusting intensity to the EEG readout. Two routes are real-time TEP monitoring (e.g., the rt-TEP approach) and E-field-based dosing, with RMT reported for reference.
3. **Over DLPFC, artefacts and sensory confounds increase with intensity.** Lateral prefrontal sites lie near the temporalis and frontalis muscles. Stronger pulses produce larger cranial-muscle artefacts, more scalp (somatosensory) stimulation and a louder coil click. These can swamp or mimic early TEP components and add non-specific late (N100/P200-like) sensory potentials. Reusing the rTMS intensity does not guarantee a clean or interpretable TEP.

Your rTMS protocol has a different aim (a therapeutic or modulatory dose, conventionally indexed to RMT, often 110–120%). Copying that convention into TMS-EEG is understandable, but it does not optimise the measurement.

## Recommended approach

Keep RMT, but treat it as a reference and starting point, not the final dose:

1. **Measure RMT the standard way.** Use the hand-muscle hotspot (e.g., FDI) with the same coil, stimulator and pulse waveform as the TMS-EEG session. RMT is the minimum intensity that gives MEPs ≥ 50 µV in ≥ 5 of 10 trials in the relaxed muscle, or use an adaptive threshold-hunting method.
2. **Start at about 100–110% RMT over the neuronavigated left-DLPFC target.** Optimise coil position and orientation first, to reduce muscle artefact while keeping the target.
3. **Adjust intensity (and, if needed, position or orientation) using real-time inspection of the averaged EEG response** over about 20–30 pulses. Aim for a clear early TEP near the target (e.g., a peak-to-peak amplitude of about ≥ 6–10 µV in the first ~50 ms in electrodes under the coil), with no large muscle or decay artefacts. Stay below a pre-set ceiling, e.g., ≤ 120% RMT or a fixed % maximum stimulator output.
4. **Optionally, check or set dose by modelled E-field** (individual MRI plus field modelling). This lets you report the estimated cortical field at the target, which compares better across participants and studies than %RMT. At a minimum, consider a scalp-to-cortex distance adjustment of the motor-derived intensity.
5. **Use auditory masking (noise matched to the coil click) and a thin foam layer or similar to reduce bone conduction and scalp sensation.** Consider a sensory or sham control condition, because sensory contributions grow with intensity.
6. **Report everything:** RMT (%MSO), final intensity (%MSO and %RMT), the criterion used to adjust it, coil position and orientation, and E-field estimates if available.

If you must keep a fixed 110% RMT (e.g., to match the rTMS arm exactly, or because it is pre-registered), that is defensible. Justify it explicitly. Still log the EEG quality checks and report which participants had weak or contaminated early TEPs. Expect more variability between participants and possibly more exclusions.

## Assumptions

- Single-pulse TMS over left DLPFC, with a figure-of-eight coil and neuronavigation (MRI-guided or by coordinates/landmarks).
- The same stimulator and pulse waveform (e.g., biphasic) for RMT and for the TMS-EEG session.
- Healthy adults screened under standard TMS safety guidelines.

## Methods text

**Option A – recommended (RMT-referenced, adjusted to the EEG response):**

> Resting motor threshold (RMT) was determined over the left motor cortex hotspot of the right first dorsal interosseous muscle as the minimum stimulator output eliciting motor-evoked potentials ≥ 50 µV in at least 5 of 10 consecutive trials in the relaxed muscle, using the same coil and biphasic pulse waveform as in the TMS-EEG session. For TMS-EEG, the left DLPFC target was stimulated under neuronavigation, starting at 110% RMT; intensity and coil orientation were then individually adjusted, based on real-time inspection of the averaged EEG response, to obtain a reliable early TMS-evoked potential (peak-to-peak amplitude ≥ [X] µV within 50 ms post-stimulus at electrodes near the target) while minimising muscle and decay artefacts, without exceeding [120% RMT / Y% maximum stimulator output]. Final intensities (mean ± SD: [ ]% MSO, corresponding to [ ]% RMT) [and individual E-field estimates at the target, [ ] V/m] are reported.

**Option B – if a fixed 110% RMT is retained:**

> Stimulation intensity was set to 110% of each participant's resting motor threshold (RMT), defined as the minimum stimulator output eliciting motor-evoked potentials ≥ 50 µV in at least 5 of 10 consecutive trials in the relaxed right first dorsal interosseous muscle, determined with the same coil and pulse waveform used for the TMS-EEG session (mean ± SD: [ ]% maximum stimulator output). This intensity was chosen to match the study's rTMS protocol; because motor threshold may not reflect prefrontal excitability, the presence and quality of early TMS-evoked potentials were verified online for each participant, and [scalp-to-cortex distance / modelled E-field at the target] is reported as a covariate.
