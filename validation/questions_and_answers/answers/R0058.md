# Large 5–10 ms deflection after DLPFC TMS: is it a cortical response, and can you call it P5/N10?

## Short answer

**Probably not, and no.** A large biphasic deflection at about 5–10 ms that is strongest over **frontotemporal** channels after DLPFC stimulation looks like a **TMS-evoked cranial muscle artifact**. The pulse directly activates the temporalis and frontalis muscles (and sometimes other facial or scalp muscles) under or near the coil. Treat it as an artifact until you have shown it is not one. Don't report it as "P5/N10" components:

- "P5/N10" is not an established TEP nomenclature for DLPFC (or for any other site).
- The signal's features (latency, size, topography) match muscle activity, not an early local cortical response.

**Assumptions:** standard TMS-compatible EEG amplifier, biphasic pulse, a typical lateral DLPFC target (around F3/AF3), and an average-referenced online display with little or no artifact processing.

## Why it is most likely muscle and not cortex

| Feature | TMS-evoked muscle artifact | Early local cortical TEP |
|---|---|---|
| Latency | Starts a few ms after the pulse, peaks about 5–15 ms, can last to about 20–40 ms | Early cortical activity exists, but at this latency it is small and hard to separate from artifact |
| Amplitude | Large: tens to hundreds of µV, sometimes mV | Usually a few µV to about 10–15 µV peak-to-peak at the closest channels |
| Waveform | Sharp, biphasic or polyphasic, high-frequency content | Smoother, lower-frequency deflections |
| Topography | Maximal at lateral frontotemporal electrodes over the muscle (e.g., F7/FT7/AF7/T7 on the stimulated side), steep focal gradient | Maximal near the stimulated cortex, consistent with a cortical source |
| Dependence on coil | Changes strongly with small shifts in coil position, orientation or intensity | Changes more gradually |

Lateral DLPFC sites sit right over or next to the temporalis and frontalis muscles. That is why DLPFC TEPs are known to be badly contaminated by muscle artifacts in the first tens of milliseconds.

**Average-reference caveat:** with an average reference, a large artifact on a few lateral channels is spread to every channel. It can then look like a widespread "component."

## Other non-cortical causes to rule out

- **Pulse artifact or edge effects.** If the online pipeline blanks or interpolates only up to about 5 ms, the edge of that window can make a sharp deflection.
- **Amplifier recovery or ringing**, or a **recharge artifact**, depending on the stimulator settings.
- **Electrode movement or decay artifacts.** These are usually slower, exponential-looking drifts, often at single channels with poor contact or under the coil.
- **Peripheral nerve stimulation (trigeminal/facial).** Its sensory-evoked brain responses come later (roughly >20 ms) and do not explain a large 5–10 ms spike. The direct muscle twitch it causes does contribute at this latency.

## How to check (ideally now, while you are still acquiring)

1. **Look at single trials.** A muscle artifact is large and present in nearly every trial, with high-frequency content. It is not a small signal that only appears after averaging.
2. **Check the topography at the peak.** If the maximum sits over the temporalis or frontalis rather than over the target, that points to muscle.
3. **Move the coil.** Shift the coil slightly medially or posteriorly, rotate its orientation, or lower the intensity a little. If the deflection shrinks a lot or disappears while the later response stays similar, it was muscle.
4. **Watch and ask the participant.** Look for a visible jaw, brow or temple twitch, and ask what they feel. Ask them to relax the jaw: teeth apart, mouth slightly open, no clenching.
5. **Compare with controls**, such as a more medial target or a sham/realistic-sham condition, if your design has them.

Real-time TEP monitoring is meant for exactly this. Adjust coil position, orientation and intensity until:
- the early response (first ~50 ms) at channels near the target is free of large muscle or decay artifacts;
- there is a clear, physiologically sized (several µV) early response.

Fixing it during acquisition works much better than trying to remove it offline.

## If you cannot avoid it

- **Remove the artifact offline with a documented method.** Options include ICA (e.g., a two-step approach that first targets the large muscle component), SOUND plus SSP-SIR, or PCA-based suppression. Report exactly what you did.
- **Be aware that these methods can distort or remove genuine early cortical signal.** Claims about the earliest latencies need extra support, such as consistency across cleaning pipelines or sensitivity analyses.
- **Start your component analysis after the contaminated window.** For DLPFC this usually means about 15–25 ms onward; commonly reported peaks are around N40, P60, N100 and P180–200. Say clearly that earlier latencies were not interpreted because of artifact.
- **Report the artifact openly:** amplitude, channels, how many participants were affected, and the cleaning steps.

## On naming

Even if a small residual deflection survives proper artifact control, do not borrow labels like "P5/N10" as if they were recognised components. If you report it, describe it by:

- polarity and peak latency in your own data;
- the channels or region of interest where it was measured;
- the evidence that it is cortical: topography, robustness to cleaning, behaviour across coil positions or intensities, and comparison with sham.

**Bottom line:** at present this deflection is best described as a TMS-evoked muscle artifact over frontotemporal sites. It is not an early local cortical response, and it should not be reported as P5/N10 components.
