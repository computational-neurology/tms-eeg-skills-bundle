# Large biphasic deflection at about 5–10 ms after DLPFC TMS: is it a cortical P5/N10?

## Short answer

**Probably not, and you shouldn't report it as P5/N10 components.** Your description matches the **early TMS-evoked scalp muscle artifact** very closely. That is a different thing from the early local cortical TEP:

- the deflection is **large**
- it is **biphasic** and looks like an MEP
- it peaks a few ms after the pulse, in the **5–10 ms** range
- it is **strongest over frontotemporal channels**
- the target is **lateral prefrontal**

At DLPFC this peak is often the **only** time-locked activity you can see in a short online average. The real early local response is smaller and is usually buried under it. Treat the deflection as an artifact until you have shown otherwise. Use it as an **acquisition tuning signal**, not a result.

## Why it looks like muscle and not cortex

| Feature you report | Muscle artifact | Early local cortical TEP |
|---|---|---|
| Latency ~5–10 ms, starting just after the pulse artifact (later than ~4 ms) | Typical | Possible, but it would be expected to be small |
| Large amplitude | Typical; often much larger than the TEP | Early DLPFC TEPs are characteristically weak |
| Sharp biphasic, MEP-like shape | Typical (the EMG of temporalis/frontalis and other cranial muscles) | Cortical early peaks are usually smoother and lower in frequency |
| Maximum over frontotemporal, lateral, inferior channels | Typical; a focal, dipolar map over muscle-rich scalp | Would be expected near the target and to spread over the following tens of ms |
| Clean-looking in the average | Time-locked muscle activity becomes *more* convincing after averaging | — |

Three more points matter here:

1. **Averaging does not remove it.** The muscle response is locked to the pulse, so it survives averaging and can look like a neat component. Single trials usually show high-frequency EMG-like bursts. An average of 10–30 trials hides this.
2. **No visible twitch doesn't mean no muscle artifact.** The twitch the participant feels is a comfort issue. The EEG muscle peak is a separate contaminant and can be large even when the participant reports no twitch.
3. **DLPFC is the worst common site for this.** In a large three-site dataset (Biabani et al., 2024, n = 135), about **62% of individuals** still had residual muscle artifact after ICA when prefrontal cortex was stimulated. The figure was about 35% for premotor and 0% for parietal. Early-TEP topography at prefrontal sites was unreliable in most individuals. The prefrontal early peak that survives careful handling is usually around **N40**. It is not a large 5–10 ms biphasic wave.

There are also other possible sources in this window:
- **residual pulse artifact or decay**, especially if channels near the coil or along the cable path show a smooth exponential-like slope
- **ocular or blink responses**, if frontopolar channels are involved

Rule these out as well, but the shape and topography you describe point most strongly to muscle.

## Why "P5/N10" is not a good label

- **No such components are established** for DLPFC TMS-EEG. If you name peaks with component labels, readers will read them as neural.
- **Labels should follow evidence, not latency.** Even if part of the signal is cortical, you can't name it until the muscle part has been separated out.
- If you do find cortical activity this early, describe it neutrally (for example, "early response within 5–15 ms"). Report its topography, the single-trial evidence and the controls below, rather than introducing new component names.

## What to do now, during acquisition

Your online average is most useful as a guide for **reducing** this peak before you commit to full data collection:

1. **Inspect single trials and the topography** in the 0–20 ms window. Look for a focal high-frequency burst over frontotemporal channels, possibly reaching jaw or facial channels.
2. **Adjust the coil a little at a time.** Small changes in rotation or handle angle, and a small move of the target point, often change the muscle peak a lot. A more medial or posterior DLPFC spot, or a different orientation, often helps. Choose the orientation from the individual anatomy and E-field, not a fixed 45° rule.
3. **Re-check intensity.** A lower intensity that still gives an early local response can greatly reduce muscle recruitment. Base the dose on online TEP quality, not on %RMT alone.
4. **Run diagnostic tests where you can:**
   - Does the peak change sharply with small coil moves while the later TEP stays stable? That points to muscle.
   - Does it invert exactly when you reverse current direction? That points to pulse artifact.
   - Is it smooth, exponential and limited to particular channels or cable paths? That points to decay. Check contact, gel and lead routing.
   - If available, add bipolar EMG over temporalis/frontalis and check whether the timing matches.
5. **Set a go/no-go rule before the main blocks**, for example: *"the early-window muscle peak is small enough that the planned endpoint does not depend on samples we would have to remove or interpolate."* If you can't reach that, consider a lower-muscle backup target.
6. **Log the final setup:** coordinates, orientation, current direction, intensity and how it was set, and screenshots of the online average and topography.

## What to plan for offline and in reporting

- **Don't expect ICA to rescue it.** At prefrontal sites muscle artifact often survives ICA, and aggressive removal can also distort real early activity. Decide in advance how the earliest interval will be handled: removal or interpolation, or a projection approach such as SSP-SIR. Don't define an endpoint that depends on samples you expect to remove.
- **If an early-window analysis is planned**, pre-register a **muscle vs no-muscle subgroup check** on uncleaned epochs. This shows that any early effect is not carried by participants with residual muscle activity.
- **Collect sensory ratings** after each block on four separate 0–10 scales: click loudness, scalp twitch, discomfort, pain. DLPFC also has the strongest auditory and somatosensory contribution to N100/P200, and masking works least well at prefrontal sites. Late components need the same caution as the early ones.
- **In the methods, report:**
  - how the early window was inspected and handled
  - the size of the cut or interpolation window
  - the coil orientation and current direction
  - how intensity was chosen
  - how many participants showed early muscle activity

## Assumptions

- "Online average" means a short average of roughly 10–30 trials shown during setup, before full offline preprocessing.
- Standard biphasic single-pulse TMS with a figure-of-eight coil over lateral DLPFC, a conventional scalp EEG cap, and no dedicated facial EMG channels.
- If the deflection is instead smooth, exponential and limited to channels under or next to the coil, re-check decay or lead artifact before considering muscle. In either case it should not be labelled as a cortical P5/N10.

## Bottom line

A large biphasic deflection at about 5–10 ms with a frontotemporal maximum after DLPFC stimulation is, until proven otherwise, a **scalp muscle artifact**, not the early local cortical response. Don't report it as P5/N10. Use it online to tune coil position, orientation and intensity until it is minimal. Then judge whether a genuine early response remains, and plan explicit controls (single-trial inspection, EMG, a muscle-free subgroup check) before attributing any early activity to cortex.
