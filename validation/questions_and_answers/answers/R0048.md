# Comparing occipital vs premotor P200: test choice and what the test can (and cannot) show

## Short answer

Because the same participants received both stimulation sites, the comparison is **within-subject (paired)**:

- **Single pre-defined measure** (e.g., P200 amplitude in an a-priori ROI/time window, or late-window GMFP): **paired t-test**, or **Wilcoxon signed-rank** if the paired differences are clearly non-normal or n is small. Report the paired effect size (d_z) with a CI.
- **Full spatiotemporal data** (no single channel/window chosen a priori): **paired cluster-based permutation test** over channels × time. This handles multiple comparisons and avoids picking a window after looking at the data.
- **If you have covariates** (sensory ratings, intensity, E-field estimates): use a **linear mixed-effects model**, e.g. `P200 ~ site + click_loudness + scalp_twitch + (1 | subject)`. In practice this is the model you need (see below).

**The main point:** none of these tests will show that occipital cortex is "more responsive". A significant result only shows that the P200 differs between the two stimulation conditions. With what you currently have, the P200 difference is most likely to come from **differences in sensory input and stimulation dose between sites**, not from cortical responsiveness. This is an inference problem, not a test-choice problem. No statistical test can fix it unless the confounds are measured.

## Why a P200 difference is not evidence of greater cortical responsiveness

1. **The P200 is mostly a sensory-evoked component.** In the largest sensory-confound study to date (Biabani et al., 2024, n = 135, prefrontal/premotor/parietal sites), late TEPs (N100/P200, ~60–300 ms) tracked participants' sensory reports. Perceived **click loudness** was the dominant contributor. **Scalp muscle twitch** had a weaker but real effect. Pain had no effect. Across sites, N100/P200 differences mirrored the sensory ratings. Over 88% of late-window variance was a single frontocentral component in every condition, *including shoulder (non-cortical) stimulation*. The authors explicitly do not claim N100/P200 is *purely* sensory, but cortical and sensory contributions cannot be separated without controls.
2. **Sensation differs a lot between occipital and premotor stimulation.** The coil sits over very different scalp: occipital/neck musculature versus vertex-adjacent scalp. It also sits at different distances from the ears, the bone-conducted click differs, and masking efficacy is known to vary by site. Occipital TMS can also evoke **phosphenes**, which add a genuine visual evoked response that is not a TMS-evoked "responsiveness" measure. A twofold P200 difference is quite consistent with sensory differences alone.
3. **The dose is not equated.** Coil-to-cortex distance, gyral geometry and induced E-field differ between occipital and premotor cortex. Even standard distance-adjusted intensity does not equalise the modelled cortical E-field across sites. If both sites used the same %MSO or the same %RMT, the dose delivered to each cortex differs by an unknown amount.
4. **Artifact burden differs by site.** Residual TMS-evoked scalp muscle activity survives ICA in a substantial fraction of individuals at some sites (34.5% at premotor in Biabani et al., 2024). Occipital stimulation near neck muscles carries its own muscle risk, and its residual can extend into later windows. If the two sites were cleaned to different degrees (different numbers of ICA components removed, different rejected trials), the amplitudes are not comparable.
5. **The measurement is not symmetric.** If "P200" was measured at electrodes near each stimulation site, or at each site's own peak latency, then the ROIs, reference effects and peak-picking differ between conditions. That biases the comparison, and picking each condition's maximum inflates differences.

## What to do instead (in order of priority)

1. **Check whether you collected sensory ratings.** Ideally these are separate 0–10 ratings of click loudness, scalp twitch, discomfort and pain per site (plus phosphene reports for occipital). If yes, enter them as covariates in the mixed model, or test whether the site effect survives in participants with matched or low ratings. If no ratings exist, you cannot attribute the P200 difference to cortex, and the claim should be dropped or heavily qualified.
2. **Use control conditions if available.** Site-matched sham (coil tilted/spaced to keep the click, plus electrical scalp stimulation) or a peripheral control such as shoulder stimulation. Compare *TEP minus matched-sham PEP* across sites, or show that the site difference is larger for active than for sham (site × condition interaction in a repeated-measures ANOVA or mixed model). Subtraction is only justified if the sham is well matched in perceived sensation.
3. **Shift the main comparison to early, local components (< ~60 ms).** These overlap little with peripherally evoked potentials and show site-specific morphology. Compare early local amplitude near the stimulated site, e.g. a local mean field power (LMFP) or ROI measure in an a-priori window, using the same paired or cluster approach. Be clear that early windows are not automatically clean. Check for residual muscle per participant, for example by splitting participants by visible muscle activity in the uncleaned 5–15 ms window and confirming that the result holds in the no-muscle subgroup. Also report the interpolated/removed pulse window, which is not physiological data.
4. **Address dose.** Report intensities and, if possible, E-field modelling at each target. Ideally use E-field-matched intensities or include the estimated cortical E-field as a covariate. Without this, "more responsive" is confounded with "more stimulated".
5. **Equalise preprocessing and measurement.** Use the same pipeline, the same rules for rejecting components and trials, and comparable trial counts per site. Report retained trials and ICA components per site. Define ROI and time windows a priori (or use the cluster test), and use a global measure (GMFP) as a reference-free complement to channel-level amplitudes.

## Suggested analysis plan

- **Primary:** linear mixed model on early local response (LMFP or ROI amplitude, a-priori window) with `site` as a fixed effect, sensory ratings and E-field (or intensity) as covariates, and a random intercept for subject.
- **Secondary:** paired cluster-based permutation test (channels × time, e.g. 15–300 ms) on TEPs for occipital vs premotor. Interpret late clusters (> ~60 ms) as *sensory-confounded* unless sham/rating controls account for them.
- **If sham data exist:** a site × condition (active vs sham) interaction. This is the strongest test available to you of a site-specific cortical difference.
- **Report:** effect sizes with CIs, per-site trial and component counts, sensory ratings per site, and a residual-muscle check.

## How to phrase the result

Even with good controls, avoid "occipital cortex is more responsive/excitable". TEP amplitude is not a pure measure of cortical excitability. It depends on dose, orientation, local geometry, the recorded projection to the scalp, and sensory inputs. A defensible wording would be:

> "Stimulation of occipital cortex produced a larger P200 than premotor stimulation (paired test/cluster statistics). Because late TEP components are strongly influenced by auditory and somatosensory input, which differed between sites [ratings], this difference cannot be attributed to differences in cortical reactivity. Early local responses [did/did not] differ after accounting for sensory ratings and estimated E-field."

## Assumptions

- Both sites were recorded in the same session(s), with the same montage, reference and preprocessing pipeline.
- The P200 was quantified as a peak or mean amplitude at some electrode set. If it was measured at different electrodes per site, re-derive it using a common ROI or a global measure before testing.
- Sensory ratings, sham conditions and E-field estimates may or may not exist. The recommendations above are ordered so you can use whatever you have. Without at least sensory ratings, the occipital-vs-premotor P200 difference should be reported descriptively, not as evidence for differential cortical responsiveness.
