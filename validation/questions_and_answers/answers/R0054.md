# ICA settings for F3 TMS-EEG data with decay artifacts and saturated channels

## Short answer

No ICA settings will reliably clean data that look like your pilot. Fix the setup before you record 30 participants. You can do this in a few minutes per session, which costs far less lab time than a dataset whose first 20 ms and target-adjacent channels can't be used.

- **Saturated channels cannot be recovered.** A clipped channel no longer holds the signal, only the amplifier's limit. ICA assumes the scalp signals are linear mixtures of their sources, and clipping breaks that assumption. Saturated channels have to be removed before ICA and interpolated afterwards. Over F3, the channels most likely to saturate (F1, F3, F5, AF3, FC3, FC1, FC5) are the ones nearest your target, so you would be estimating your main signal from neighbouring channels.
- **A large decay over the first ~20 ms is mainly a setup problem, not a cleaning problem.** It comes from charge building up and draining in the electrode–gel–skin contact, and from induction in the lead loops. Its size follows pulse size and intensity, and its polarity depends on how the leads are routed. ICA, detrending or fitting, SOUND and interpolation can reduce it. Each needs explicit modelling choices, and each can remove or distort real early responses. The decay is also time-locked to the pulse, like the TEP itself, so components that carry decay often carry real early signal too.
- **F3/DLPFC is the hardest common site even with good acquisition.** In a large three-site comparison (n = 135), TMS-evoked scalp muscle activity was still visible in the 5–15 ms window after two-pass FastICA in about 62% of individuals stimulated over prefrontal cortex. The figures were about 35% for premotor and 0% for parietal. Adding a large decay and saturation on top of that makes it much more likely that the early window can't be interpreted.
- **ICA-based pipelines also reduce TEP amplitude overall.** This affects early (N15/P30) and late (N100/P180) peaks, not only the windows next to the artifact. The more ICA has to remove, the more your amplitudes reflect the cleaning rather than the brain.

## Assumptions

- About 60-channel EEG with passive or active electrodes and a standard TMS-compatible amplifier.
- Single-pulse TMS over left DLPFC at F3, with a figure-of-eight coil.
- The endpoint includes TEP components within roughly the first 100 ms (for example N15, P30, N45). If you only care about N100/P200, the decay matters less. But at this site N100/P200 is largely driven by sound and touch, so it needs sensory controls anyway (see the last section).
- The decay lasts much longer than the 2–3 ms recovery that well-optimised setups can reach. A full 20 ms of dominant decay plus saturation points to a fixable cause, not a property of the hardware.

## What to do instead: a short setup protocol for every participant

### 1. Channel and lead preparation (before the cap goes under the coil)

- Check impedance and contact quality, especially at F1/F3/F5/AF3/FC1/FC3/FC5. Look for gel bridges and loose or unstable electrodes.
- Run the electrode wires roughly perpendicular (about 90°) to the coil handle or the direction of the induced current, where you can.
- Remove wire loops and slack near the coil, and keep cable tension stable. Write down or photograph the cable routing so it stays the same across participants. Lead configuration changes both the size and the polarity of early artifacts.
- Check the amplifier: input range, hardware filters, sampling rate and trigger synchronisation. Saturation means the signal is going beyond the input range. Confirm the amplifier is set for TMS (wide dynamic range, DC or very low high-pass, high sampling rate). A higher sampling rate helps with the pulse artifact itself, but it does not remove decay. Decay depends mainly on intensity and setup.

### 2. Online check with 10–30 trials before the main block

Look at single trials as well as the online average, and check:

- whether any channel saturates, and which ones;
- how long the decay lasts and which channels it affects. If it follows particular electrodes or wires, fix the contact or routing there;
- whether small changes to electrode contact, lead routing, coil position or rotation, or intensity shorten the decay;
- the early muscle-artifact peak: a two-sided, MEP-like deflection later than about 4 ms over frontal, temporal or jaw channels. At F3 this is often the only time-locked activity visible online. Don't read it as a cortical response, and don't assume the window is clean just because the participant reports no twitch. Reduce it with small changes in coil rotation and position and, if needed, intensity;
- whether the early response looks local to the target and plausible, rather than following the cable layout.

### 3. Go/no-go rule (write it down before the study starts)

For example, start the main block only when:

- no channel saturates outside the pulse-artifact window;
- the decay has returned to baseline well before the earliest component in your endpoint (set this in ms from your pilot after optimising, for example within ~10 ms if N15/P30 matter);
- the early muscle-artifact peak does not dominate the analysis window;
- masking and comfort are acceptable.

If a participant fails, adjust and re-check before recording. Save screenshots or notes of the setup (coordinates, coil angle, intensity, lead routing, online average) for every session.

### 4. Re-pilot once

Run one pilot session with this protocol before starting the 30 participants. If the decay still dominates 20 ms after careful preparation, look at hardware recovery, recharge timing and synchronisation. Also consider whether the intensity is higher than needed: decay duration depends strongly on intensity, and for a non-motor target the intensity should be set from the online TEP, not from a percentage of RMT. Do this before you commit to collection.

## The ICA plan to preregister (for the residual artifacts left after the fixes above)

ICA is a reasonable tool for what remains after acquisition is under control. A defensible TESA-style setup:

1. **Epoch** around the pulse with a long baseline (for example −1 to +1 s).
2. **Remove and interpolate the pulse window** before ICA, for example [−2, ~10–12] ms with cubic interpolation. Set the end point from your optimised data, not from the pilot. Report exactly which window is treated as lost.
3. **Mark bad channels before ICA** (any channel that saturated or has poor contact) and leave them out of the decomposition. Keep a copy with all channel locations so you can repair them afterwards (interpolation or SOUND). Don't interpolate before ICA; that creates dependent channels and rank problems.
4. **Reject bad trials** before ICA so a few extreme epochs don't drive the decomposition.
5. **Make a separate ICA training copy if needed.** If you need high-pass filtering or detrending to stabilise ICA, apply it to that copy and then apply the component rejection to your analysis data.
6. **PCA reduction and FastICA.** Reducing to about 25–30 components is typical for about 60 channels. Report the retained rank.
7. **State what each ICA pass is for.**
   - *Option A, classic two-pass FastICA:* the first pass targets the large TMS-evoked decay/muscle components. After filtering, the second pass targets blinks, eye movements and ongoing muscle activity. This is where most of the overcleaning risk sits.
   - *Option B, ICA → SOUND → SSP-SIR (preferred for F3):* use ICA only for blinks, eye movements and muscle activity that is not locked to the stimulus. Then use SOUND for channel noise and SSP-SIR for the remaining TMS-evoked muscle activity (for example over −2 to ~35 ms). Don't reject components with clear stimulus-locked activity by ordinary visual review.
   - Record about 1 minute of blinks or eye movements before each session so ocular components are easy to identify.
8. **Reject components on topography, time course, spectrum and condition-aware QC, not on automatic labels alone.** Don't remove auditory or somatosensory components by default; deal with them through design and contrasts.
9. **Filter after** the pulse and gross artifacts are handled (for example low-pass around 80 Hz, plus a notch filter). Avoid strong high-pass filtering on short epochs.

**Required sensitivity checks:**

- Vary the number of rejected components (and SSP-SIR projections) by ±2 and show that the TEPs stay stable.
- Compare cleaned data with minimally preprocessed data, looking at topographies as well as amplitudes.
- Split participants by whether muscle activity is visible in the *uncleaned* 5–15 ms epochs. Compare cleaned group TEPs and spatial correlations between the two groups. If they diverge, the cleaning isn't doing what it claims, and early-window topographies or source estimates shouldn't be interpreted.
- Report every excluded or interpolated window, every removed channel and component, and retained trials per participant.

## Sensory confounds at F3 (plan for these now as well)

- Prefrontal stimulation gives the loudest perceived click (even at relatively low intensity), the highest twitch and discomfort ratings, and the weakest benefit from white-noise masking. It also gives the largest N100/P200, which partly reflects sensory input.
- Use tuned auditory masking (for example adaptive noise) plus foam or a spacer under the coil. Re-tune masking whenever you change intensity or coil position.
- Collect loudness, scalp-sensation, twitch and discomfort ratings from every participant, and include a realistic sham or sensory control condition. Without these, late TEP effects at F3 can't be attributed to cortical activation, and amplitude comparisons with other sites or groups aren't justified.

## Bottom line

Spend a few minutes per session on lead routing, contact quality, amplifier settings, coil and intensity adjustment, and an online go/no-go check. Then use ICA conservatively on what is left, with a preregistered pulse window, bad-channel exclusion before ICA, a clearly stated purpose for each ICA pass, and the sensitivity checks above. Recording all 30 participants with the pilot setup and relying on ICA afterwards would most likely leave you with:

- channels next to F3 that cannot be recovered;
- an early window that cannot be interpreted;
- amplitudes that reflect the cleaning rather than the cortex.
