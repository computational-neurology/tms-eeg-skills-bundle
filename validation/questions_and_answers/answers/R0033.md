# Preprocessing active and sham TMS-EEG without biasing the comparison

## Assumptions

- Within-subject design: active and sham were recorded in the same session(s), with the same cap and amplifier settings, at the same target.
- The sham is meant to reproduce the sensory side effects of TMS (click and/or scalp sensation). It might be a placebo coil, a tilted coil, or a realistic sham with electrical scalp stimulation. Your main contrast is active vs sham TEPs (amplitude, GMFP, topography). It may also include time-frequency measures.
- "Different artifact profiles" here means that active has a larger pulse artifact, decay/polarisation artifact and cranial-muscle artifact than sham. Sham may also have its own artifacts, such as from electrical stimulation or a different click.

## The core problem

Most TMS-EEG cleaning steps adapt to the data. ICA component selection, SSP/SSP-SIR, SOUND, decay fitting, trial and channel rejection, and interpolation windows all respond to what is in each dataset. If you clean each condition separately until it "looks clean", you remove more from active than from sham. You may also remove different signal subspaces in each condition. Part of the active–sham difference then comes from the pipeline rather than the brain. This can go either way:

- **Over-cleaning active** can remove real early TEP components that share a topography with muscle or decay artifacts. This reduces the true effect.
- **Under-cleaning active** leaves residual artifact. This inflates early peaks and GMFP, and filtering can smear it into later latencies.
- **Unequal trial counts or SNR** bias peak amplitudes, GMFP and power estimates, even when the underlying signals are identical.

The goal is a **symmetric procedure, not symmetric-looking data**. Both conditions should go through the same transformations with the same parameters, decided before you look at condition differences.

## Guiding principles

1. **One pipeline, fixed in advance.** Pre-specify every parameter: windows, filters, thresholds, component criteria and number of components. Ideally preregister them, or at least lock them in a dated document before running the contrast.
2. **Same operations, not "same outcome".** Where a step must be tuned to the worst artifact, which is usually active, apply that same setting to sham as well.
3. **Shared spatial filters.** Estimate artifact subspaces once, from pooled data or from active. Then project them out of both conditions with the identical operator.
4. **Blind the decisions that need judgment.** Rename the files so the person classifying components or rejecting trials does not know which condition they are looking at. For pooled decompositions, hide the condition labels on the epochs.
5. **Equalise SNR before comparing.** Match trial counts across conditions within each participant.
6. **Quantify what each step did to each condition.** Then check that your conclusions survive reasonable alternative pipelines.

## Recommended pipeline, step by step

### 1. Before preprocessing: fix what you can at acquisition (future sessions)
- Use the same recharge delay in both conditions, and push recharge artifacts outside the analysis window.
- Use the same noise masking and the same foam/spacer. Give the same attention to electrode impedance under the coil.
- If the sham uses electrical stimulation, record its timing and keep its artifact window known.

### 2. Epoching and pulse-artifact removal
- Use **identical epoch limits and an identical removal/interpolation window** in both conditions, for example −2 to +X ms.
- Choose X from the active condition, where the artifact is longest, and apply it to sham as well. Removing a short clean segment from sham costs little. Using a shorter window in sham, though, makes the two conditions' early-latency data differ in how it was constructed.
- Use the same interpolation method in both (e.g., cubic or none/zero-padding before ICA).
- Make the earliest latency you interpret ≥ X ms plus a margin. State that the comparison is undefined before that.

### 3. Downsampling and filtering
- Apply the same anti-alias filter, high-pass, low-pass and notch to both, at the same stage of the pipeline.
- Filter only after large artifacts have been removed or replaced, so ringing from the pulse artifact is not spread asymmetrically. Use zero-phase filters with modest orders. Consider a gentle high-pass (e.g., ≤1 Hz), or detrending instead.

### 4. Bad channels
- Use the **union** of bad channels across conditions per participant. Remove and interpolate the same channel set in both, so that spatial sampling and interpolation smoothing are matched.

### 5. Trial rejection
- Use the same automated criteria (amplitude, kurtosis, joint probability) with fixed thresholds, or do visual rejection blind to condition.
- Expect active to lose more trials because of muscle artifacts. After rejection, **subsample to equal trial counts** per participant and condition. Use random subsampling repeated many times, then average the metric, or keep one pre-registered random draw.
- Report the number of trials retained per condition. Test whether rejection rates differ between conditions.

### 6. Artifact subspace removal (ICA, SSP-SIR, SOUND): the key step
Never run separate decompositions per condition and then pick components independently. The options below are listed from best to least preferred.

**Option A: joint decomposition on pooled data (preferred default).**
- Concatenate active and sham epochs (after trial-count matching, or at least balanced) and run one ICA.
- Classify components blind to condition, using fixed, pre-specified criteria such as topography, time course, spectrum and early-latency variance. Use the same automated classifier settings if you use one.
- Remove the same components from both conditions. Because the unmixing and the removed subspace are identical, any brain signal lost in that subspace is lost from both conditions equally.
- Caveat: the large active artifacts can dominate the pooled decomposition. A **two-round approach** works well. Round 1 removes only the very large, fast decay/muscle components. Round 2 runs after filtering and targets ocular, other muscle and line-noise components. Both rounds use the pooled data.

**Option B: estimate artifact topographies from active and project them out of both.**
- Identify the artifact components or SSP vectors from active data. Build the projector P = I − U Uᵀ, where U is an orthonormal basis of the artifact topographies.
- Apply the **same P to both conditions**. Projection is well defined for any data, even when the artifact is absent in sham.
- For SSP-SIR, apply both the SSP projection and the SIR reconstruction identically to both conditions. That way the projection's distortion of brain topographies is matched.

**Option C: SOUND or other noise-adaptive methods.**
- Use the same regularisation and iteration settings in both. Ideally, estimate the channel noise model from pooled data. This prevents channel weighting from differing systematically between conditions.

In every case:
- **Fix the number of removed components, or the removal criterion, in advance.** Report how many were removed and how much variance they explained in each condition.
- Do **not** remove the sensory-evoked components (N100/P200-like auditory or somatosensory responses) from one condition only. If you treat them as a confound, deal with them identically in both conditions, or leave them in and let the sham subtraction handle them. Do not mix these strategies.
- If you fit and subtract a decay artifact (e.g., exponential fits), run the same fitting routine with the same settings on sham. It will usually fit near-zero, which is fine and shows the procedure was symmetric.

### 7. Re-referencing and baseline
- Use the same reference (e.g., average reference after interpolation) and the same baseline window. Make sure the baseline does not overlap with residual pre-pulse artifacts in either condition.

### 8. Electrical-stimulation artifacts in a realistic sham
- If the sham has its own artifact, for example from scalp electrical stimulation, handle it with the same logic in reverse. Apply the sham-defined removal window or projection to active as well.

## Bias diagnostics (do these and report them)

1. **Variance-removed table.** For each step, report the per-condition proportion of variance removed and the number of components, trials and channels rejected. Large asymmetries need justification.
2. **"What was removed" plots.** Plot the removed signal (raw minus cleaned) per condition, as butterfly plots and GMFP. If the removed signal from active contains structured activity at latencies or topographies typical of genuine TEPs, you may be over-cleaning.
3. **Residual-artifact check.** Compare early-latency high-frequency power (muscle band, e.g., 30–100 Hz in the first 30–50 ms) and lateral/temporal channels between conditions after cleaning. Residual differences there suggest the active–sham effect may be artifact.
4. **Pipeline sensitivity (multiverse-lite).** Rerun the contrast with 2–4 defensible pipeline variants, for example:
   - different interpolation windows;
   - ICA vs SSP-SIR;
   - a different number of removed components;
   - a different high-pass.

   Report whether the effect's sign, latency and significance hold up. Effects that appear in only one pipeline are fragile.
5. **Ground-truth injection (optional, strong).** Add a known synthetic TEP-like signal, or a scaled real response, to the sham data. Run the full pipeline and check whether it is recovered without distortion. This tells you whether the pipeline itself would create or erase a difference.
6. **Null check.** Split the sham data into two random halves and run the full pipeline and contrast on them. This gives the false-positive rate of your pipeline-plus-statistics combination.

## Analysis-level considerations

- **Restrict inference to latencies where both conditions are interpretable.** Usually this means after the interpolation window and after residual muscle artifacts have decayed. Say explicitly that the earliest components (<~15–20 ms) are not compared if the cleaning there is unreliable.
- **Interpret active − sham with care.** Subtraction assumes additivity, i.e., that sensory responses are the same size in both conditions. In practice, perceived intensity often differs between active and sham. Collect intensity and click-loudness ratings and use them as covariates or in sensitivity analyses.
- Use whole-epoch, whole-scalp **cluster-based permutation tests**, or pre-registered windows/ROIs, to avoid choosing windows after seeing the cleaned data.
- Prefer measures that do not depend strongly on SNR, or matched-SNR comparisons. GMFP and peak amplitudes rise with noise, so trial-count matching is essential.
- For time-frequency analyses, use the same wavelet or taper settings in both conditions. Make sure the interpolation window and edge effects are matched, since early-latency power is very sensitive to them.

## What to report

- The full pipeline with every parameter, stating that it was applied identically to both conditions, and whether it was fixed before the contrast.
- The interpolation window and its rationale; the filters and the order of operations.
- The decomposition strategy (pooled, or projected from active) and how components were classified (criteria, blinding).
- Per-condition counts of rejected trials, channels and components, plus variance removed. Trials retained after matching.
- The results of the sensitivity analyses.
- The latency range over which the comparison is considered valid.

## Short version

Apply one pre-specified pipeline to both conditions. Use the worst-case (active) artifact settings for both. Use shared spatial filters, either from a pooled decomposition or one projector applied to both. Use the union of bad channels and equal trial counts. Make judgment calls blind to condition. Then show, with variance-removed tables, "removed signal" plots and a few alternative pipelines, that your active–sham difference does not depend on how the artifacts were cleaned.

Relevant methodological work includes the TESA toolbox and pipeline papers (Rogasch and colleagues), SSP-SIR and SOUND (Mutanen and colleagues), work on peripheral and sensory confounds and realistic sham (Conde et al., 2019; Gordon et al., 2021), comparisons showing that different pipelines yield different TEPs (Bertazzoli et al., 2021), and the recent TMS-EEG methods consensus (Hernandez-Pavon et al., 2023).
