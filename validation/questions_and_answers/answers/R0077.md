# Should the coil stay fixed at the MRI-defined DLPFC target for the whole session?

**Short answer:** Only partly. Keeping coil position and orientation stable *during data collection* is correct, and you should actively enforce it. But fixing the coil at the anatomical target **before checking the EEG response** is not the right approach for TMS-EEG. An anatomically "optimal" target does not guarantee a usable TMS-evoked potential (TEP). At lateral prefrontal sites in particular, the default placement often gives large muscle artifacts, a weak early response, or both. The recommended workflow is: start from the MRI target, spend a short calibration phase adjusting the coil based on the real-time EEG response, then freeze that final position for the session.

## Why the anatomical target alone is not enough

1. **The coordinate is a group-level target.** MNI (-38, 44, 26) is the well-known group-average DLPFC site defined by anticorrelation with the subgenual cingulate. Once warped into an individual brain it can land on a gyral crown, in a sulcus, or at different depths. "Anatomically optimal" here means "optimal on average across a group", not "optimal for this person's cortex".
2. **A 45° handle angle is a convention borrowed from M1.** For M1, 45° (posterior-lateral) places the induced current roughly perpendicular to the central sulcus. At DLPFC, gyral geometry varies a lot between people, so 45° has no special physiological meaning there. How strongly the cortex is activated depends on the angle between the E-field and the local sulcal walls.
3. **Lateral DLPFC sits under or near the temporalis and frontalis muscles.** Stimulating them causes large biphasic artifacts in the first ~5-30 ms that can hide the early TEP components. Small changes, such as moving the coil a few mm medially or rotating it, often reduce these artifacts substantially.
4. **The early TEP is the readout that matters.** Without a clear, artifact-free early response (roughly the first 50 ms), later components (e.g., around 100-200 ms) are hard to interpret. Auditory and somatosensory evoked potentials, which are not specific to the target, can dominate them.

## Recommended procedure

### 1. Planning (before the session)
- Transform the MNI coordinate into each participant's native MRI space and inspect where it falls. Snap it to the nearest gyral crown or sulcal wall if needed.
- If possible, run E-field modelling (e.g., SimNIBS) to choose the orientation that maximises the field component normal to the target sulcal wall, or the field magnitude at the target. Treat 45° as a starting value to be tested, not a fixed rule.
- Set intensity from the estimated E-field at the target, or from TEP-based titration, rather than only as a percentage of resting motor threshold. Scalp-to-cortex distance at DLPFC differs from M1.

### 2. Calibration with real-time TEP monitoring (start of session)
- Place the coil at the planned target and orientation. Deliver a short block of about 20-30 pulses and view the average response live, in the channels under and around the coil and as a butterfly plot.
- Check:
  - **Muscle artifact:** large, sharp deflections within ~0-15 ms. If present, move the coil a few mm medially or anteriorly and/or rotate it in steps of about 15-30°, then re-test.
  - **Decay or polarisation artifacts:** slow drifts after the pulse. Check electrode impedances and the contact between coil and electrodes.
  - **Early TEP amplitude:** aim for a clear early response. As a working criterion, use a peak-to-peak amplitude of roughly ≥10 µV within the first ~50 ms in channels near the target, with a topography that is maximal near the stimulated site.
  - **Sensory confounds:** make sure noise masking makes the click inaudible, and use a thin spacer or foam layer to reduce bone conduction and scalp sensation.
- Limit adjustments to a small, pre-defined neighbourhood. As an assumption, stay within about 1-1.5 cm of the planned target and any orientation, so you remain in DLPFC.

### 3. Freeze and maintain (data collection)
- Record the final position and orientation as the new navigation target. Keep it constant for all experimental blocks.
- Monitor deviations with neuronavigation throughout. Set tolerance limits (e.g., ≤2-3 mm displacement, ≤3-5° angular deviation) and reposition when they are exceeded. Head movement, cap or tracker slippage, and the coil drifting under its own weight are common causes.
- Check that the head tracker has not moved, and re-register if it has.
- Log coil-to-target error for each pulse or block, so you can treat it as a covariate or exclusion criterion later.
- For multi-session designs, reuse the same optimised individual coordinates in later sessions rather than redoing the full optimisation. Re-check the response briefly with real-time monitoring.

## Methodological safeguards
- **Pre-specify the adjustment rules** (which artifacts, which amplitude criterion, maximum displacement and rotation). Apply them only during calibration and blind to experimental condition. This avoids flexibility that could bias condition comparisons.
- **Report the details:** planned vs. final coordinates, orientation, the E-field or intensity used, navigation error, and the masking procedure.
- If the design really does require an unmodified anatomical target (e.g., to match a treatment protocol), say so explicitly. In that case, still use real-time monitoring to document data quality, and use stronger artifact-handling strategies during preprocessing (e.g., ICA or SOUND/SSP-SIR for muscle artifacts). Be aware that this cannot fully recover a response obscured by large muscle artifacts.

## Bottom line
Use the MRI target as the **starting point**, not the endpoint. Optimise coil position and orientation briefly at the start of the session using the real-time EEG response: minimise muscle artifacts and get a clear early TEP. Then keep that optimised configuration rigidly fixed, monitored by neuronavigation, for the rest of the session.
