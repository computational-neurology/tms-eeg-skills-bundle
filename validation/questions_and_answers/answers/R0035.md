# Is a PCIst of 0.25 "below the 0.31 threshold"?

**Short answer: No, that comparison isn't valid.** The 0.31 cutoff (PCI\*) belongs to a different metric, the original source-level, Lempel-Ziv-based PCI (PCI-LZ; Casali et al., 2013). PCIst (Comolatti et al., 2019) uses a different algorithm on a different scale, and 0.31 means nothing on that scale. A PCIst of 0.25 can't be judged against 0.31 at all. And if 0.25 really is a PCIst value, it is low enough to point to a computational or acquisition problem before it says anything about the patient.

---

## 1. PCI-LZ and PCIst are two different metrics

| | PCI-LZ (Casali 2013) | PCIst (Comolatti 2019) |
|---|---|---|
| Computed on | Estimated cortical sources (needs forward/inverse modelling) | Sensor-space averaged TEP (SVD + state transitions) |
| Range | Normalised, bounded at ≤ 1 | **Not upper-bounded** |
| Typical values | Around 0.3–0.4 near the boundary; e.g. PCImax of 0.34 and 0.40 in MCS patients | Benchmark: **47.89 ± 12.65** awake vs **14.19 ± 5.26** in NREM sleep/anaesthesia |
| Cutoff | PCI\* = 0.31 | Derived empirically with a linear classifier, **separately for each montage**. There is no single universal value, and none of them is 0.31 |

The two metrics correlate well (r = 0.82) and classify similarly (AUC 0.995 vs 0.998), but their values can't be swapped for each other. Applying 0.31 to PCIst is the most common mistake made with this metric.

## 2. On the PCIst scale, 0.25 is suspiciously low

In the benchmark (382 sessions), even unconscious states (NREM sleep, propofol, midazolam, xenon) averaged about 14. A value of 0.25 is roughly 50 times smaller than that mean. You get it when almost nothing survives the dimensionality reduction or almost no transitions exceed baseline. Before reading anything physiological into it, check the following:

1. **Which metric was actually computed?** If your pipeline did source modelling, binarisation and Lempel-Ziv compression, you have PCI-LZ, and 0.31 is the relevant comparison (see section 4). If it ran SVD and state transitions on sensor data (e.g. `calc_PCIst`), you have PCIst.
2. **Input format.** PCIst expects a 2-D array of shape (channels × time) holding the **trial-averaged** TEP, not epochs. The time vector must be in **milliseconds** with a negative baseline. Typical TMS-EEG parameters are baseline −400 to −50 ms, response 0 to 300 ms, k = 1.2, min_snr = 1.1, max_var = 99. A time vector in seconds, transposed data, or a mis-specified window can collapse the result to near zero.
3. **NaNs.** The reference implementation returns exactly 0 when the input contains NaN. 0.25 isn't exactly zero, but check for NaN and flat channels anyway.
4. **Decompose the value.** PCIst = N_C × mean(ΔNST): the number of surviving components times the mean number of significant state transitions per component. Run the calculation with the full return option and look at how many components survived the SNR filter, their SNRs, the variance explained and the per-component ΔNST. A near-zero value usually means about one weak component with almost no transitions above baseline. That points to a response barely distinguishable from baseline, not to a measured "low complexity".

## 3. Even a correctly computed low PCIst is weak evidence here

**Technical causes look the same as low complexity.** Before giving a physiological reading, rule out:
- **Stimulation failure.** Was there a local response with peak-to-peak amplitude above 10 µV in the first 50 ms at the channel nearest the target (averaged over about 20 trials), verified online?
- **Target over or next to a lesion.** Such sites are not expected to respond.
- **Too few trials.** The clinical standard is 120–150 per site. This matters most when large slow waves dominate, which is likely under sedation.
- **Over-cleaning.** Aggressive ICA can suppress the TEP across the whole scalp. Compare the response before and after cleaning.
- **Arousal drop** during the session.

**Sedation is a confound in its own right.** Propofol, midazolam and xenon all reliably lower perturbational complexity through cortical bistability: after a brief activation, cortical neurons fall into a silent OFF-period, and the response collapses into a stereotyped slow wave. A low value recorded under sedation therefore can't tell the drug's effect apart from the patient's underlying capacity for consciousness. If the clinical question is the patient's capacity, test off sedation, or at the lowest clinically acceptable level, and document the drug, dose and timing. Ketamine is the exception: complexity stays high under ketamine.

**One site is not enough.** The per-patient statistic is **PCImax**, the maximum across at least two stimulation sites. Typical sites are superior frontal (BA6/BA8) and superior parietal (BA7), about 1 cm lateral to the midline to avoid scalp muscle. A single low value at one site doesn't establish a low PCImax.

## 4. What would make a threshold comparison legitimate

- **If you computed PCIst on 60 channels:** compare it only against a cutoff derived for a comparable 60-channel montage **and** a comparable pipeline. The benchmark pipeline was: about 200 pulses per session, ICA, spherical interpolation, 0.1–45 Hz band-pass, downsampling to 725 Hz, average reference, baseline −400 to −5 ms. Absolute PCIst values carry every upstream preprocessing choice with them, so a published cutoff can't simply be borrowed across montages, amplifiers or pipelines. Re-derive or validate it on reference data processed the same way. With 60 channels the benchmark sensitivity for MCS was 91.8%, so the method is good, but only within its calibrated setup.
- **If you actually computed PCI-LZ:** then 0.25 is a legitimate value to set against 0.31. Even so, it would mean "no evidence of high complexity under these conditions", not "unconscious". The sedation and technical caveats above still apply.

## 5. How to report it

Report: the metric (PCIst), the full parameter set, the montage and channel count, trials per site, the number of sites, any sedative agent and dose, the source of the cutoff, and the N_C / mean(ΔNST) decomposition, not the bare scalar.

Keep in mind what PCI does **not** do. It detects **capacity** for consciousness; it does not grade it. It doesn't track behavioural severity (no correlation with CRS-R), can't separate MCS+ from MCS−, and isn't a prognostic index on its own.

---

### Assumptions
- I've taken "PCIst" to mean the Comolatti et al. (2019) state-transition index computed on sensor data. If it was actually a source-level, Lempel-Ziv computation, read section 4, second bullet.
- I've assumed "sedated" means pharmacological sedation during the recording. The specific agent matters for interpretation, ketamine in particular.
- Stimulation sites, trial counts and online response checks weren't specified, so I've listed them as things to verify, not as known failures.

### Bottom line
You can't call the patient "below the threshold" on this basis. The 0.31 cutoff doesn't apply to PCIst. A PCIst of 0.25 is far below even the values typical of unconscious states, which points to a computation or acquisition problem first. And recording under sedation makes any low value hard to attribute to the patient rather than the drug.
