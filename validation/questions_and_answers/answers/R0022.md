# Is a PCIst of 0.25 "below the 0.31 threshold"?

**Short answer: No, not as stated.** The 0.31 cutoff (PCI\*) belongs to a different metric, the original **PCI-LZ** (Casali et al., 2013). It cannot be applied to a **PCIst** value. And a PCIst of 0.25 is far outside the range PCIst normally takes, so before you interpret it at all, check how it was computed.

---

## 1. PCI-LZ and PCIst are two different metrics on different scales

"PCI" covers two measures with different algorithms, scales and cutoffs:

| | **PCI-LZ** (Casali 2013) | **PCIst** (Comolatti 2019) |
|---|---|---|
| Computed in | Estimated cortical sources (needs forward/inverse modelling) | Sensor space (no source modelling) |
| Core operation | Binarise against baseline statistics, then Lempel-Ziv compression | SVD, then recurrence-based state-transition counting |
| Range | Normalised, bounded at 1 | **No upper bound** |
| Typical values | 0–1; values near the cutoff are common in disorders of consciousness (e.g., 0.34 and 0.40 in MCS patients) | Benchmark means **47.89 ± 12.65** awake vs **14.19 ± 5.26** in NREM sleep or anaesthesia |
| Cutoff | **PCI\* = 0.31** | Derived from data **separately for each montage**. There is no universal value, and none of them is 0.31 |

The two metrics correlate (r = 0.82) and classify the benchmark data about equally well (AUC 0.998 vs 0.995). **Their numbers are not interchangeable.** On the PCIst scale, 0.31 has no meaning.

## 2. A PCIst of 0.25 is itself a red flag

With the published TMS-EEG settings on a 60-channel montage, PCIst is **two orders of magnitude** above 0.25, even in unconscious states (deep sedation is around 14 ± 5). So 0.25 is very unlikely to be a real PCIst result. The most likely explanations:

1. **It is actually a PCI-LZ value.** If you ran a source-level pipeline (current density estimation, bootstrap statistics, normalised Lempel-Ziv complexity), then 0.25 is on the scale where PCI\* = 0.31 applies. If so, please confirm it and report it as PCI-LZ, not PCIst.
2. **A normalisation or scaling step changed the output.** Examples: a wrapper that rescales the result, dividing by the number of channels or samples, or a non-standard `times` axis (seconds instead of milliseconds, which shifts the baseline and response windows).
3. **The computation partly failed.** Possible causes:
   - Epochs were passed instead of the trial-averaged evoked response.
   - Almost no components survived the SNR filter.
   - The baseline window doesn't match the data.
   - NaNs in the input. These make the reference implementation return exactly 0. Treat any near-zero or exactly zero value as a possible bug, not a finding.
4. **Wrong parameters.** The published TMS-EEG settings are: baseline −400 to −50 ms, response 0 to 300 ms, k = 1.2, min_snr = 1.1, max_var = 99, no embedding, n_steps = 100.

**Assumption:** I'm assuming you used the standard PCIst implementation on a trial-averaged, average-referenced 60-channel TEP. If so, recheck the input and parameters before reading anything physiological into 0.25.

## 3. If it really is PCIst, how should it be judged?

- **Use a montage-specific cutoff.** PCIst thresholds were derived from the benchmark with a linear classifier, separately for each montage (hd-EEG 60 ch, 10-20 19 ch, 8 ch). Sensitivity drops as channel count falls. For MCS patients it was 91.8% at 60 ch, 89.8% at 19 ch and 83.7% at 8 ch.
- **Your pipeline must be comparable to the benchmark's.** The benchmark used:
  - a 60-channel TMS-compatible amplifier and MRI-guided TMS
  - about 200 pulses per session
  - ICA and spherical interpolation of bad channels
  - 0.1–45 Hz band-pass and downsampling to 725 Hz
  - epochs of −400 to 400 ms, average reference, and baseline correction from −400 to −5 ms

  Absolute PCIst values, and so the cutoff, only carry over if your acquisition and cleaning are comparable. Otherwise the threshold needs to be re-derived for your own setup, ideally with conscious and unconscious reference recordings.
- **Look at the decomposition, not only the single number.** PCIst = N_C × mean(ΔNST), where N_C is the number of surviving principal components (spatial differentiation) and ΔNST is the per-component state transitions (temporal complexity). Run the calculation with full output and report:
  - the number of surviving components
  - the per-component ΔNST values
  - the component SNRs
  - the variance explained

  A low value from one component with few transitions points to something different than many components that each show little activity.

## 4. Even if the value is genuinely low, it's weak evidence on its own

A low PCI (either metric) looks exactly the same whether complexity was truly lost or the acquisition failed. Before calling a sedated patient's response "below threshold" in a physiological sense, rule out:

| Possible technical cause | Check |
|---|---|
| Stimulation didn't engage cortex | Was there an early local TEP above **10 µV peak-to-peak in the first 50 ms** at the electrode nearest the target, after averaging about 20 trials? Ideally checked online, adjusting site, orientation and intensity. |
| Target over or near a lesion (if any) | Check structural imaging. Such sites are not expected to respond. |
| Too few trials | Aim for **120–150 artifact-free trials per site**. This matters most under sedation, where high-amplitude slow waves dominate the background. |
| Over-cleaning | Aggressive ICA can globally suppress the TEP. Compare the response before and after cleaning. |
| Arousal or sedation level drifting during the session | Record sedation depth, drug and dose, and timing relative to each block. |
| Only one stimulation site | The per-patient statistic is **PCImax**, the maximum across sites. Use **at least two sites**, e.g., superior frontal (BA6/BA8) and superior parietal (BA7), about 1 cm lateral to midline to avoid scalp muscle. |

The opposite direction matters too: residual muscle artefact and auditory or somatosensory co-stimulation **inflate** PCIst. So a value that comes out "high enough" after you fix the computation also needs checking for residual muscle and masking quality.

## 5. Interpreting a low value in a sedated patient

If the value is valid and below a montage-appropriate cutoff, it is consistent with the expected effect of sedation. Propofol, midazolam and xenon all reduce perturbational complexity through cortical bistability: the TMS-evoked activity collapses into a stereotyped slow wave. Keep in mind:

- PCI is a **detection measure for the capacity for consciousness at that moment**. It doesn't grade *how* conscious someone is and isn't validated as prognostic. In MCS it did not correlate with CRS-R scores and could not separate MCS+ from MCS−.
- In a sedated patient, a low value mainly reflects the drug state. It says little about underlying capacity once sedation is lifted. Repeating the measurement off sedation (or at a defined lower dose) is the clinically informative comparison.

## 6. What to report

- Which metric you used (PCIst or PCI-LZ), and the full parameter set
- Montage, number of trials, and number of stimulation sites
- PCImax across sites
- Sedative agent, dose and timing
- The N_C / ΔNST decomposition
- Where your cutoff comes from: for PCIst, a threshold derived for your montage and pipeline; for PCI-LZ, PCI\* = 0.31

**Bottom line:** Don't compare 0.25 with 0.31 until you know which metric you have. If it is truly PCIst from a standard 60-channel pipeline, 0.25 points to a computation problem rather than a low-complexity brain state. If it is actually PCI-LZ, then 0.25 < 0.31 is a legitimate comparison. Even then, it only means "below threshold under these acquisition conditions and this sedation state", after the technical causes in section 4 have been ruled out.
