# How long a window to remove and interpolate around the TMS pulse

## Short answer

With a modern TMS-compatible, DC-coupled EEG amplifier sampling at 5 kHz or more, a typical removal window is about **−2 ms (or −1 ms) to +5–10 ms** around the pulse. Do not pick the number from the literature, though. Measure it in your own data. Find the latest time point at which the pulse artefact and the amplifier's recovery from it are still visible on any channel or trial. Add a margin of 1–2 ms. Then use that one fixed window for every participant and condition.

If you would need much more than about 10–15 ms, the extra length is usually not the pulse artefact. It is decay, muscle or movement artefact. Handle those with dedicated methods (ICA, SSP–SIR, SOUND and similar) or by improving acquisition. Don't simply lengthen the interpolation window.

**Assumptions:** single-pulse TMS; a cortical target such as M1 or DLPFC; a DC-coupled, high-slew-rate amplifier without hardware sample-and-hold; sampling at 5–25 kHz; a standard biphasic or monophasic pulse lasting about 0.1–0.4 ms. Different hardware changes the numbers but not the logic.

## 1. What the window should cover, and what it shouldn't

| Artefact | Typical duration | Removed by the cut-and-interpolate window? |
|---|---|---|
| The pulse itself (the induced voltage during the magnetic pulse) | < 0.5 ms | **Yes** |
| Amplifier saturation and recovery, ringing, anti-aliasing filter smear (this smear also reaches slightly *before* the pulse) | ~1–5 ms on good systems; up to ~10 ms on electrodes under the coil or with weaker hardware | **Yes**. This is usually what sets the window's end. |
| Trigger-to-pulse jitter | sub-ms to ~1 ms | **Yes**. This is why the window starts at about −1 to −2 ms rather than at 0. |
| Electrode / electrode–gel decay artefact (slow exponential) | tens to hundreds of ms | **No**. Use ICA, detrending or exponential fitting, and better skin preparation and impedance. |
| Cranial muscle artefact (temporalis, frontalis) | ~5–30 ms, very large | **No**. Use ICA, SSP–SIR or similar. Cutting it out would remove the early TEP. |
| Capacitor recharge artefact | a brief transient at the stimulator's recharge delay | Remove a **second**, short window there, or set the recharge delay outside your analysis epoch. |

The main principle is that interpolation replaces data with a smooth curve that carries no information. Use it only for the period when the recording is physically meaningless: saturated, ringing, or dominated by the induced voltage. If a period still holds brain signal mixed with artefact, separate the two instead of cutting.

## 2. How to choose the length from your own data

1. **Look at the raw data first.** Use unfiltered, non-downsampled data that is epoched but not baseline-corrected or re-referenced, or only minimally so. Filtering and downsampling before removal smear the pulse across many milliseconds and make it look longer. They also contaminate the samples around it.
2. **Plot at a fine time scale.** Make single-trial and averaged butterfly plots over about −5 to +30 ms, on a symmetric log or clipped amplitude scale so that both the saturated part and the recovery are visible. Pay special attention to the channels under and next to the coil. These are always the worst.
3. **Find the onset.** It is the first sample that departs from the pre-stimulus trace. Because of anti-aliasing filters and jitter, this is often a fraction of a millisecond to about 1 ms *before* the nominal trigger. Start the window at least 1 ms earlier than this onset. In practice that means −1 to −2 ms.
4. **Find the offset with an objective criterion.** Use something like one of these:
   - the first time after which the absolute amplitude, or the first derivative, stays within e.g. baseline mean ± 5 SD, or within a fixed limit such as a few hundred µV for the derivative-based check;
   - the end of saturation or flat-lining plus the end of high-frequency ringing, judged by the slope returning to physiological values;
   - the point where global mean field power (GMFP) of the single-trial data drops back toward plausible amplitudes. Recovering muscle or decay can keep it high for longer, which is why you should combine this with the derivative check.

   Compute the offset per channel and per trial. Take a high percentile, such as the 95th–99th, across trials and the maximum across retained channels. Add a 1–2 ms safety margin.
5. **Fix one window for the whole study.** Derive it from pilot data or from all participants pooled. Then apply the same window to every participant, condition, site and intensity. Artefact duration grows with stimulation intensity and depends on coil orientation and position. Windows that differ between conditions would create differences between conditions. Pre-register the window and its criterion if you can.
6. **Check the result.**
   - After interpolation, look for steps or discontinuities at the window edges. A step at the end edge means the window is too short. Check the start edge for the same problem.
   - Run a sensitivity analysis by repeating the main analysis with, for example, +6, +10 and +15 ms ends. Components you interpret, such as N15/P30, N45 and P60, should not change in any meaningful way. If they do, the result depends on artefact handling.

## 3. Interpolation and pipeline details that affect the choice

- **Method:** Cubic interpolation, fitted on a few ms of clean data on each side of the gap, is the common default (for example in TESA). Linear interpolation is acceptable for short gaps. Avoid long gaps. Any interpolation longer than about 10–15 ms produces a smooth artificial waveform that can look like an early component, and it distorts the spectrum and time–frequency estimates near time zero.
- **Order of operations:** Remove the window, then interpolate *before* any filtering or downsampling. Many pipelines, TESA among them, remove the window again (setting it to constant or zero) before ICA so that the interpolated segment does not drive components. They then interpolate again before the final filtering.
- **Don't interpret anything inside the window.** Treat the first few ms after it with caution as well, because later filtering spreads the interpolated segment into neighbouring samples. Report the earliest interpretable latency explicitly.
- **Report it.** State the exact window (e.g. "−2 to +8 ms, cubic interpolation using 1 ms of data on each side"), the criterion you used to choose it, the amplifier, the sampling rate and the recharge delay.

## 4. Hardware and paradigm notes

- **Sampling rate:** At 5 kHz one sample is 0.2 ms. At 20–25 kHz the artefact's time course is resolved much better, and the artefact often turns out to be shorter than lower sampling rates suggest.
- **Sample-and-hold or gated amplifiers** blank the input for roughly the first ~2 ms in hardware. The window you remove in software can then be short, but you still need to cover the gate and any residual transient.
- **Acquisition is the best way to shorten the window.** Keep impedance low (below ~5 kΩ). Route lead wires perpendicular to the coil's induced current and keep them away from the coil. Use thin, TMS-compatible (e.g. Ag/AgCl, low-profile) electrodes. Keep the coil from touching the electrodes or pressing on them, which causes movement artefact. Avoid high-pass coupling with long recovery.
- **Paired-pulse / rTMS:** Remove a window around *every* pulse. When the interstimulus interval is shorter than the window (e.g. 2–3 ms SICI), the two windows merge into one longer gap, so interpret early components with even more caution. Make sure the control and single-pulse conditions get matched windows.

## 5. Rule of thumb

- Start at **−2 to +10 ms**.
- Shorten it, for example to −1 to +5/6 ms, if your data show clean recovery earlier.
- Lengthen it, up to about +15 ms, only if the pulse or ringing itself demonstrably lasts longer.
- If you seem to need more than that, find the source (decay, muscle or movement) and use a targeted correction rather than a longer window.
