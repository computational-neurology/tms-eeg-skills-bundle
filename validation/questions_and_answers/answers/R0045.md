# Occipital vs premotor P200: what a test can and cannot show

## Short answer

The choice of test is not the main problem. A paired comparison will tell you whether the occipital P200 is reliably larger than the premotor P200 in the same people. It **cannot** tell you that occipital cortex is "more responsive." The P200 is the TEP component most dominated by peripheral sensory input: the coil click, the scalp and muscle twitch, and related sensations. These differ between stimulation sites. A twofold site difference in P200 is the kind of result you would expect from sensory differences alone, with no difference in cortical responsiveness at all.

So I would split this into (1) the correct statistic for the effect you actually measured, and (2) what you would need before the "more responsive cortex" claim is defensible.

---

## 1. The statistic for the effect you measured

Assumptions: the same participants had both sites, P200 was taken from a predefined window and ROI (or from GMFA/GMFP), and you have one value per participant per site.

**Primary (single a priori measure):**
- **Paired t-test** on the per-participant occipital minus premotor P200 difference, if the differences are roughly normal. Check with a Q-Q plot or Shapiro-Wilk on the *differences*, not on the raw values.
- **Wilcoxon signed-rank test** if the differences are skewed or have outliers, or N is small (below about 15).
- Report the mean (or median) difference with a 95% CI and a paired effect size (d_z). A ratio such as "twice as large" should come with its CI, ideally estimated from per-participant log-ratios.

**If you did not fix the window and electrodes in advance:**
- Use a **cluster-based permutation test** (paired, sign-flip permutations) across channels × time, roughly 150–250 ms. This controls family-wise error without choosing a window after seeing the data.
- Report it as "a spatiotemporal cluster in which responses differed between sites." Do not say the effect has a precise latency or location; cluster tests do not support that.

**Mixed-model alternative (recommended if you can add covariates):**
- A linear mixed model such as `P200 ~ site + sound_rating + twitch_rating + (1 | participant)`, or trial-level or block-level data with random slopes where they are supported. This form lets you test whether the site effect survives once the sensory ratings are accounted for, which is the question that matters (see below).

Whichever test you use, the honest conclusion is: *"Late TEP amplitude (P200) differed between occipital and premotor stimulation."* It is **not** *"occipital cortex is more excitable or responsive."*

---

## 2. Why the P200 difference does not mean greater cortical responsiveness

### The P200 is largely a sensory-evoked potential
- In the largest sensory-confound study to date (n = 135; prefrontal, premotor, parietal), components from about 60 ms onward, including N100/P200, were dominated by **one frontocentral component (≥88% of variance) at every site**. That held even for shoulder stimulation, which does not stimulate cortex.
- **Perceived click loudness was the strongest predictor** of late TEP size. People who heard louder clicks had larger N100/P200 responses. Removing masking increased late responses at premotor and parietal sites.
- **Scalp muscle twitch** had a smaller but real effect, with the most robust part beyond about 250 ms. Discomfort had a marginal effect. **Pain had none**, so a pain rating is not a proxy for contamination.
- **Site differences in N100/P200 tracked the sensory ratings across sites.** That is the confound you face: occipital and premotor stimulation will almost certainly differ in perceived click, in which muscles twitch (occipital and neck muscles vs scalp over the vertex), and in discomfort.
- Even this work does not show that the P200 is *purely* sensory: a cortical contribution cannot be ruled out. You therefore cannot call it artifact, and you also cannot treat it as a clean index of local cortical responsiveness.

### Occipital-specific confounds
- **Phosphenes and visual input.** Occipital TMS can produce phosphenes, which are conscious visual percepts. These can evoke visual and attentional potentials in the P200 range that premotor stimulation does not. Ask about phosphenes on every block.
- **Neck and posterior muscle activation.** This differs from the premotor site in both size and topography.
- **Coil placement and click transmission.** Occipital placement changes coil-to-head contact and how the click is conducted through bone, so masking efficacy may differ between sites. A masking effect of a given size at one site says how well masking worked there. It does not say how much auditory input that site generates.

### Dose is not equated between sites
- If both sites were stimulated at the same % output or % rMT, the induced cortical E-field almost certainly differed. Scalp-to-cortex distance, gyral geometry and coil orientation relative to the local sulci all differ between occipital and premotor cortex.
- Standard coil-to-cortex distance correction does **not** equalise modelled cortical E-field across sites. Without E-field modelling, a larger response could reflect a larger dose rather than greater responsiveness.

### Topography check
- If the P200 is maximal over **frontocentral** electrodes for both sites, it is not a local occipital response. It is the generic late sensory-dominated complex, and its amplitude difference is weak evidence about occipital cortex.
- A claim about local responsiveness needs effects **near the stimulated region**.

## 3. What would support a "more responsive" claim

In order of importance:

1. **Separate sensory ratings per site and block** for click loudness, scalp twitch, discomfort and pain on a 0–10 scale, plus a phosphene report for occipital blocks. Rate them separately: they affect different TEP components.
   - Enter the ratings as covariates in the mixed model, or test whether the site effect holds in a subgroup with matched ratings.
   - If you did not collect ratings, collecting them retrospectively is unreliable. State the limitation explicitly rather than drawing the cortical conclusion.
2. **Site-matched sensory controls.** Use a realistic sham per site: a click preserved by tilting the coil, plus electrical scalp stimulation matched to perceived intensity.
   - Compare **TEP minus sham** between sites, or test a site × (active vs sham) interaction.
   - A matched sham can reasonably be subtracted, because sensory input appears to add to the early cortical response rather than interact with it. That evidence is limited to roughly the first 110 ms at the targets tested, so do not extend it automatically to the P200 window or to occipital cortex.
3. **Shift the inference to early, local components (under about 60 ms).** These show site-specific peaks and little overlap with sensory-evoked potentials, so they are a better basis for site-specific claims. Analyse them in an ROI near each stimulation site, or with LMFP.
   - Early components are **not automatically clean**. In the same dataset, residual scalp-muscle artifact survived ICA in about 35% of participants after premotor stimulation (and about 62% after prefrontal).
   - Inspect uncleaned epochs at 5–15 ms for muscle activity at both sites. Then check that the site effect holds in the subgroup without visible residual muscle.
4. **E-field modelling**, so you can show the doses were comparable or normalise the response to the estimated cortical E-field. Alternatively, set intensity per site by a matched E-field or by an individually titrated early-TEP criterion, rather than one motor-threshold-based intensity for both sites.
5. **Equal trial counts and matched preprocessing** at both sites. Different numbers of rejected trials or ICA components between sites can create amplitude differences on their own. Report the rejected trials and components per site.

---

## 4. Suggested wording if you cannot add controls

> "P200 amplitude was larger after occipital than after premotor stimulation (paired t-test / Wilcoxon, mean difference = X µV, 95% CI [..], d_z = ..). Because late TEP components are strongly shaped by site-dependent auditory and somatosensory input, and stimulation dose was not equated across sites, this difference cannot be attributed to differences in cortical responsiveness."

## Bottom line

- Use a paired t-test or Wilcoxon signed-rank test for one predefined measure, or a paired cluster-based permutation test if the window was not fixed in advance.
- Better, use a mixed model with the sensory ratings as covariates.
- None of these tests turns a P200 difference into evidence that occipital cortex is more responsive. That claim needs:
  - sensory ratings (with phosphene reports for occipital blocks)
  - site-matched sham controls
  - early local components checked for residual muscle artifact
  - comparable doses, shown by E-field modelling
