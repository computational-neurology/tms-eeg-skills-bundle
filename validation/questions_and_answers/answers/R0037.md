# One or two rounds of ICA in TMS-EEG preprocessing?

## Short answer

Neither one nor two rounds is correct in every case. Two-pass ICA is a well-established option, best known from TESA and also used in ARTIST and TMSEEG, but it is **not automatically better** than one pass. What matters is that you can say **which artifact each pass is meant to remove**, and that you never use either pass to quietly remove real stimulus-locked cortical signal.

A defensible default:

- **One main ICA round** for stereotyped physiological artifacts that are *not* time-locked to the pulse: blinks, eye movements, ongoing/background scalp muscle, and other persistent noise.
- **Handle the large, early, TMS-specific artifacts with dedicated methods**, not ordinary ICA rejection. That means the pulse, amplifier decay/recharge, and TMS-evoked muscle. The methods are pulse-window removal and cubic interpolation, a second interpolation window, SSP-SIR (or SOUND followed by SSP-SIR), trial rejection, or adjusting the coil or target at acquisition.
- **Add a second ICA round only when you have a concrete reason.** Typical reasons are a large high-amplitude early artifact that would dominate the decomposition, or residual non-stimulus-locked artifacts after filtering, SOUND or SSP-SIR. Treat it as a documented, QC-checked step, not a ritual.

If you are reproducing a published TESA, ARTIST or TMSEEG protocol, follow its two-pass structure, but report what each pass removed and run the sensitivity checks below.

## What each round is classically meant to remove

### Canonical TESA-style order (Rogasch et al. 2017)

Channel rejection → epoching → baseline → pulse removal/interpolation (e.g., about −2 to 10–12 ms) → downsample → bad-epoch rejection → **ICA 1** → band-pass/notch filter → **ICA 2** → bad-channel interpolation → average reference → final baseline.

| | **Round 1 (before filtering)** | **Round 2 (after band-pass/notch)** |
|---|---|---|
| **Classic target** | Very large early artifacts that remain after pulse interpolation: TMS-evoked scalp/facial muscle (biphasic, peaks around 4–5 and 7–10 ms, can exceed 1 mV, decays over >50 ms) and amplifier/electrode decay. In ARTIST and TMSEEG, the first ICA explicitly targets **decay**. | The "ordinary" EEG artifacts: blinks, eye movements, ongoing/persistent muscle, electrode noise, and residual decay or other leftovers from pass 1. |
| **Why it sits there** | These artifacts are orders of magnitude larger than the TEP. Filtering across them causes ringing and smearing, and when they are present they dominate any decomposition. Removing them first allows clean filtering and a better-conditioned second ICA. | Once the huge transients are gone and the data are filtered, ICA can separate the smaller stereotyped artifacts more reliably. |
| **Main risk** | **Highest risk of overcleaning.** These components are time-locked to the pulse, just like the TEP, which breaks ICA's independence assumption. Rejecting them can remove genuine early cortical signal. | Removing auditory/somatosensory-related components by default, and overcleaning in general. |

### How the approach is moving

The first pass is the controversial one. ICA assumes artifact and brain sources are independent and separable in space. Large TMS-evoked muscle and decay artifacts are time-locked to the pulse, as is the TEP itself, so they violate that assumption. As a result, ICA can fail in both directions:

- **Overcleaning:** ICA-based pipelines tend to give smaller TEP amplitudes across early (N15/P30) and late (N100/P180) peaks, not only near the artifact window. Changing only the decay-suppression method (FastICA vs. SOUND vs. model-based detrending) changes early TEP amplitude *and* topography.
- **Undercleaning:** even after a two-pass FastICA pipeline, TMS-evoked muscle was still visible in the 5–15 ms window in about 62% of participants after prefrontal stimulation, about 35% after premotor and 0% after parietal stimulation (Biabani et al. 2024).

For that reason, many current workflows, especially those based on SOUND/SSP-SIR, move the TMS-specific job **out of ICA**:

1. Pulse removal/interpolation (and a second interpolation window for residual decay if needed).
2. Bad channels/trials marked; **ICA for ocular, blink, eye-movement and non-stimulus-locked/background muscle**. This is the main physiological ICA slot, placed *before* SOUND.
3. SOUND (noise/bad-channel correction), then **SSP-SIR for the remaining early TMS-evoked muscle**.
4. **Optional** second ICA only for *residual non-stimulus-locked* stereotyped artifacts, with before/after QC.

Under this framing, the second ICA pass is optional residual cleanup, not a required step.

## What ICA should *not* be used to remove (in either round)

- **Components with clear TMS-stimulus-locked activity or TMS-evoked muscle morphology.** These can contain or overlap with the genuine TEP. Use interpolation, SSP-SIR/projection, trial rejection or acquisition changes instead. If you do reject such a component (for example to reproduce a TESA protocol), document the artifact model and run a sensitivity analysis.
- **Auditory/somatosensory components** (e.g., N100/P200-like activity from the coil click and scalp sensation). Control these by design: masking noise, foam spacer, and sham or sensory-control conditions with contrasts or justified ERP subtraction. Do not reject them by default. They are real brain responses, just not specific to the TMS target.

## Decision guide

| Situation | Suggestion |
|---|---|
| Midline/parietal or posterior target, little muscle, good decay recovery | One ICA round (ocular/background) after pulse interpolation is usually enough. |
| Large residual decay after interpolation that would dominate ICA | Try a second interpolation window or detrending first. If you use an early ICA pass for decay, report it and check sensitivity. |
| Lateral/frontal target (e.g., DLPFC) with strong TMS-evoked muscle | Prefer SSP-SIR (± SOUND), or remove/interpolate about −2 to 10–12 ms and analyze later windows (e.g., 20–60 ms). Don't expect ICA to fully clean it. Run the muscle vs. no-muscle QC below. |
| Reproducing TESA/ARTIST/TMSEEG for comparability | Two passes as published, with the target of each pass stated explicitly in the methods. |
| Few trials / low channel count | Be cautious with any ICA. Keep the PCA-compressed rank below the number of trials. Two passes increase instability. |

## Practical settings and QC (apply to each round)

- **Before ICA:** handle the pulse (and decay) first. Mark bad channels and exclude them from the decomposition, then repair them afterwards (interpolation or SOUND). Reject gross bad trials.
- **Rank:** PCA compression to roughly 25–30 components for a ~60-channel montage is common. Fewer channels need a deliberate rank choice.
- **ICA-training data:** if you high-pass (e.g., 1 Hz) only to stabilize ICA, do it on a copy and apply the unmixing to your analysis data. Don't let the training filter become your TEP waveform. Avoid aggressive high-pass filtering on short epochs.
- **Concatenating conditions** for one ICA is valid only if they share the same non-neural noise structure. Check that conditions are cleaned equally, because condition-specific cleaning creates spurious differences.
- **Component decisions:** judge each component on topography, time course (trial-locked vs. not), spectrum and the TEP before vs. after. Never decide on an automatic label alone. Log every rejected component per participant.
- **Sensitivity check:** vary the number of rejected components by ±2 and confirm the TEP (amplitude *and* topography) is stable.
- **Compare against minimally preprocessed data** in artifact-free windows. Large changes in morphology there suggest overcleaning.
- **Residual-muscle check for frontal/lateral targets:** split participants by visible muscle in the *uncleaned* 5–15 ms window. Compare their cleaned TEPs and spatial correlations. If the subgroups diverge, the cleaning is not working.
- **Report:** windows interpolated, decay method, ICA algorithm, rank, number and type of components removed per pass, and retained trials.

## Assumptions and caveats

- I assumed a standard TEP study (around 60 channels, epoched data, a single-pulse paradigm) and no specific software constraint. The logic applies equally to TESA/EEGLAB (FastICA) and MNE-Python, but exact function calls should be checked against the current documentation of the toolbox you use.
- The best choice depends on target (muscle burden), amplifier (decay profile), trial count, and whether you need the earliest latencies (<20 ms). If early latencies matter, avoid relying on ICA for TMS-evoked muscle.
- Whatever you choose, do not read TEP amplitudes as a pure measure of cortical excitability. Amplitudes from ICA-heavy and ICA-light pipelines are not directly comparable across studies.
