# Conclusion sentence for the N45 finding after 20 Hz rTMS

## Short answer

I don't recommend writing that cortical excitability increased. The data don't support it, and the most common reading of the N45 points the other way. Here is a sentence the result can support:

> "Following 20 Hz rTMS, the amplitude of the M1 TMS-evoked N45 increased by 30% relative to baseline, indicating a modulation of early cortical TMS-evoked activity that, given the established pharmacological link between the N45 and GABA-A receptor-mediated neurotransmission, may reflect enhanced GABA-A-mediated inhibition rather than a net increase in corticospinal excitability."

If you want a shorter, more neutral version:

> "20 Hz rTMS increased the amplitude of the M1 N45 component by 30% compared with baseline, suggesting a change in early, putatively GABA-A-mediated cortical reactivity."

## Why "increased cortical excitability" is not a safe conclusion

1. **The N45 is mostly read as an inhibitory marker.** In pharmaco-TMS-EEG studies (e.g., Premoli et al., 2014, J Neurosci), positive allosteric modulators of GABA-A receptors, such as alprazolam, diazepam and zolpidem, made the N45 larger. Antagonists or reduced GABA-A tone have been linked to a smaller N45 (e.g., S-ketamine; Darmani & Ziemann's work on NMDA/GABA balance). A larger N45 is therefore more naturally read as **more GABA-A-mediated inhibition**, the opposite of increased excitability. This reading is not certain, since the pharmacology of TEP peaks is still debated. But that uncertainty makes the claim harder to defend, not easier.

2. **A bigger TEP peak does not mean "more excitable."** TEP amplitude reflects the summed balance of excitatory and inhibitory postsynaptic activity in the generating network, along with how synchronous it is. A single peak getting larger cannot be mapped onto a one-dimensional "excitability" scale. Excitability claims at M1 are usually supported by MEP amplitude, the MEP input-output curve or the resting motor threshold, ideally alongside early TEP components such as P30 or the early TMS-evoked local response.

3. **Peripheral and sensory signals contaminate the N45 latency window.** Around 40–60 ms, TEPs over M1 overlap with somatosensory input from scalp and muscle stimulation and with feedback from the evoked movement (re-afferent signals). Some auditory-evoked activity can also show up there. An rTMS session could change these through altered MEP size, habituation, a different noise-masking level or changes in scalp sensation, without any change in the cortical response to TMS itself.

4. **No control condition is mentioned.** A pre/post change with no sham rTMS session (or an active control site) can't be separated from time-of-day effects, habituation, changes in electrode impedance or gel, attention or arousal drift, and regression to the mean.

## Assumptions behind this answer

- "Increased by 30%" means a larger absolute (more negative) N45 deflection, measured with the same peak-picking window and method before and after.
- Coil position (neuronavigated), orientation and single-pulse TMS intensity were the same pre and post, and intensity was not re-adjusted to a post-rTMS motor threshold.
- The change is statistically reliable at the group level. If it was only tested descriptively, or in a single participant, the conclusion should be weaker still.
- No MEP or other independent excitability measure was reported alongside it.

## What would make a stronger conclusion possible

- **Sham-controlled design.** Compare the active 20 Hz rTMS effect with sham (or an active control site) in a crossover, and report the time × condition interaction rather than a pre/post change in the active session alone.
- **Converging excitability measures.** Report MEP amplitude or an MEP input-output curve, plus early TEP components (P30, early local response). If MEPs increase while the N45 also increases, you can describe "increased corticospinal output accompanied by enhanced N45, possibly reflecting a compensatory increase in GABA-A-mediated inhibition."
- **Sensory confound control.** Use noise masking and foam padding, check that peripheral-evoked potentials and sensory ratings did not change between pre and post, and run a realistic sham or ICA/SOUND/SSP checks to show the N45 change is cortical.
- **Robust peak quantification.** Use the same latency window and ROI before and after, ideally with cluster-based permutation statistics over time and channels so the result does not depend on a single picked peak.

## Suggested wording if MEPs also increased (only if this is true)

> "20 Hz rTMS increased MEP amplitudes, consistent with enhanced corticospinal excitability, and concurrently increased the M1 N45 by 30%, which may reflect a concomitant upregulation of GABA-A receptor-mediated inhibition."
