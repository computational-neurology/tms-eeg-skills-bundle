# Comparing occipital vs. premotor TEPs: test choice, and why the P200 cannot show "responsiveness"

## Short answer

You have the same participants in both conditions, so any test has to be **paired (within-subject)**. For one pre-defined amplitude value per participant and site, use a **paired t-test**, or a **Wilcoxon signed-rank test** if the paired differences are clearly non-normal. For full scalp-by-time data, use a **paired cluster-based permutation test**.

The harder problem is what the result means. A bigger P200 after occipital stimulation does **not** show that occipital cortex is "more responsive" to TMS, whatever the p-value. The P200 is the TEP component most contaminated by non-transcranial (sensory) input, and a between-site comparison is confounded in several other ways. Any test you run will measure "the scalp signal differs between the two stimulation conditions". To claim "the cortex reacts more", the design and analysis below need to change.

---

## 1. Why the P200 comparison doesn't answer the question

| Confound | Why it matters for an occipital vs. premotor comparison |
|---|---|
| **Sensory-evoked potentials** | The N100–P200 complex is largely driven by the auditory coil click and by somatosensory input from the scalp. Even with masking, these give a vertex-maximal N1–P2 that looks the same for very different stimulation sites. Click loudness through bone conduction, scalp sensation and saliency differ between occipital and frontal coil positions, so the P200 difference may be sensory rather than cortical. |
| **Stimulation dose is not matched** | The same % MSO, or even the same % RMT, gives different induced E-fields at the two targets. Scalp-to-cortex distance, gyral geometry and coil tilt over the occiput differ. A larger response may just mean a stronger effective dose. |
| **Different electrodes / topography** | The two peaks are measured at different scalp locations. Amplitude there depends on source depth and orientation, the volume conductor and the reference. A twofold difference at two electrodes does not mean a twofold difference in cortical activation. |
| **Site-specific artifacts and preprocessing** | Premotor/frontal stimulation usually produces much larger muscle artifacts (temporalis, frontalis). Removing these takes more aggressive ICA/SSP/SOUND cleaning, which also removes real signal and can shrink later components. Occipital stimulation adds its own problems: neck muscles, possible phosphenes and visual evoked activity. If preprocessing differed in effect between sites, the amplitude difference may partly come from the pipeline. |
| **Post-hoc selection** | If the P200 window or electrode was picked after you saw that it was "twice as large", a test on that same window is circular (double dipping) and will inflate the effect. |
| **Directional framing** | "Show that occipital is more responsive" is a conclusion, not a hypothesis. Unless this contrast and its direction were pre-specified, report it as exploratory. |

---

## 2. What to do instead (in order of how much it improves the inference)

### a) Add a sensory control and test an interaction
Record a **realistic sham** at each site: matched click (noise masking plus a spacer or sham coil) and matched scalp sensation (e.g., electrical scalp stimulation). Ideally also record a **low-intensity control**, or vary intensity. Then the claim becomes a **Site × Condition (real vs. sham) interaction**:

- 2 × 2 repeated-measures ANOVA, or better
- a linear mixed model: `amplitude ~ site * condition + (1 + site + condition | participant)` (trial-level data if available).

The interaction, i.e. how much more real exceeds sham at occipital than at premotor, is the closest you can get with scalp EEG to "more cortical responsiveness". If you already have the data without sham, say clearly that you cannot separate a cortical difference from a sensory one.

### b) Match or model the dose
- Calibrate intensity to a **matched induced E-field at the target** (e.g., individual SimNIBS models), or to an individual TEP-based threshold rather than motor threshold.
- If intensity was not matched, at least estimate the E-field at each target and include it as a covariate, or report the TEP amplitude per unit E-field.

### c) Use measures that are more specific to the stimulated cortex
- Focus on **early, local components** (roughly 15–80 ms), which are less sensory-contaminated than the N100/P200.
- Quantify them in a way that does not depend on one electrode:
  - **Local mean field power (LMFP)** over a pre-defined ROI under each coil, or
  - **Global mean field power (GMFP)** or source-space amplitude at the target.
  A source-space comparison is still limited by differences in sensitivity to the two regions, so interpret it with care.
- Keep the **late components (P200 and beyond) as a sensory-contamination index**, not as the main outcome.

### d) Run identical, blinded preprocessing
Run both sites through the same pipeline, with fixed parameters and component rejection blind to site where possible. Report how many trials, channels and components were removed per site, and check that these numbers don't differ systematically.

---

## 3. The statistics themselves

**Option 1: pre-specified ROI/window (confirmatory)**
- Fix the ROI and time window before testing, from the literature, a pilot, or a site-collapsed grand average that is orthogonal to the contrast.
- Compute one value per participant and site (mean amplitude in the window is more robust than peak amplitude; LMFP or GMFP are alternatives).
- Use a **paired t-test** on the occipital – premotor difference. If the differences are clearly non-normal or have outliers, use a **Wilcoxon signed-rank test**, or a permutation test on the paired differences.
- Report **Cohen's dz with a 95% CI** (and the raw mean difference in µV) as well as the p-value.
- If there are several components or windows, correct for multiple comparisons (Holm/FDR).

**Option 2: whole-scalp, data-driven**
- Use a **paired (dependent-samples) cluster-based permutation test** over channels × time, e.g. FieldTrip `ft_timelockstatistics` with `depsamplesT`, or MNE `permutation_cluster_1samp_test` on the paired differences.
- This avoids choosing a window after seeing the data. But a significant cluster does **not** localise the effect in time or space precisely. Describe it as "a difference within this broad window", not "the P200 differs".

**Option 3: trial-level mixed model**
- A linear mixed model with participant (and, if relevant, session) random effects uses all trials. It handles unequal trial counts after rejection and lets you add covariates such as E-field strength, sham response or intensity.

**Assumed design:** each participant has one session per site, both sites recorded with the same montage and reference, and data averaged per participant. If the sites were recorded in different sessions, consider session order and between-session variability (counterbalancing, order as a factor).

---

## 4. How to report what you currently have

Without sham or dose matching, a defensible statement is something like:

> "The P200 after occipital stimulation was larger than after premotor stimulation (paired test, effect size with CI). Because late TEP components are strongly influenced by auditory and somatosensory input that differs between stimulation sites, and because induced E-field strength was not matched, this difference cannot be attributed to greater cortical excitability or responsiveness of occipital cortex."

To support the stronger claim, the key additions are a realistic per-site sham with a Site × Condition interaction, matched or modelled E-field dose, and an analysis built on early, local TEP measures defined before looking at the data.
