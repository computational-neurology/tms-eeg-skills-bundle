# Short answer

Partly. You are right that the coil should stay at **one fixed position and orientation during data collection**. Changing it partway through a block mixes different stimulation conditions in the same average. The approach goes wrong in two places, though:

1. It treats the MRI or MNI coordinate, plus a 45° handle angle, as already optimal **for TMS-EEG**. It is a starting point, not a verified final target.
2. It treats "fixed" as "set once and leave alone". Keeping a coil in place takes active monitoring and correction.

A better workflow:
- **Before recording:** start from the MRI target and fine-tune it for TMS-EEG.
- **During recording:** lock that position in, and correct any drift back to it.
- **After recording:** document the coil position you actually delivered.

---

## 1. The coordinate is not "anatomically optimal" for TMS-EEG

- **What the coordinate is.** MNI (-38, 44, 26) is the group-level left DLPFC coordinate that Fox et al. (2012) linked to the strongest anticorrelation with the subgenual cingulate. It was derived from connectivity, for depression treatment. It was not chosen to produce a clean, large TMS-evoked potential (TEP), and it is not tailored to any individual.
- **Error in the individual head.** Moving a group coordinate into each person's MRI adds registration error, and individual sulcal anatomy differs. The coordinate may fall on a gyral crown, a sulcal wall or a sulcal fundus. That changes how much field reaches the cortex and in which direction.
- **Muscle artifacts.** The lateral prefrontal scalp sits over the frontalis and temporalis muscles. At this site, a coil placed for anatomy alone often triggers large muscle artifacts lasting about 5–30 ms or longer. These can hide or distort early TEP components (for example around 20–40 ms). The best anatomical spot can be a poor spot for EEG.

## 2. The 45° handle angle is borrowed from motor cortex

- **Where 45° comes from.** It is the standard motor cortex (M1) orientation, set so the induced current runs roughly perpendicular to the central sulcus. Nothing makes it optimal for the middle frontal gyrus. Local gyral orientation in DLPFC varies a lot between people.
- **What determines the effective orientation.** It is roughly the one that drives current perpendicular to the targeted gyral crown or sulcal wall. E-field modelling (for example SimNIBS) on each participant's MRI can estimate it, or you can find it empirically.
- **The trade-off.** Rotating the coil (often toward a more anterior–posterior or medial angle) and moving it slightly medially can greatly reduce muscle artifacts. The cost is a small loss of field strength at the exact coordinate. That compromise is usually worth making, and you should document it.

## 3. Fine-tune with real-time TEP feedback before recording

- **Purpose.** Without a motor output, an "anatomically correct" position can still produce little or no cortical response. Current TMS-EEG guidance (for example the rt-TEP approach of Casarotto et al., 2022, and the Hernandez-Pavon et al., 2023 recommendations) is to check and fine-tune the response before recording.
- **Procedure.**
  - Deliver short test trains of about 20–30 pulses at the planned intensity.
  - Look at the averaged response online, with the artifact window removed.
  - Aim for a clear, lateralised early response near the stimulation site within about the first 50 ms. A peak-to-peak amplitude around 10 µV is a common working target.
  - Avoid large muscle, decay or recharge artifacts.
- **If the response is weak or artifact-heavy:**
  - Adjust position by a few millimetres, rotation by about 15–45°, tilt, or intensity.
  - Stay inside a pre-defined area around the MRI target, for example within about 1 cm.
  - Re-test after each change.
- **Intensity.** DLPFC gives no motor readout, so the intensity derived from resting motor threshold (RMT) is only a rough guide. Coil-to-cortex distance differs between M1 and DLPFC. Consider adjusting the dose with an E-field estimate, using the TEP response, or both.
- **Masking.** Set up auditory masking (noise matched to the click) and a foam spacer or thin padding before you finalise the position. Masking and coil pressure both affect the response, and a thin spacer also changes coil-to-scalp distance.
- **Record the final choice.** Note the final position and orientation, how far it is from the original MRI target, and why you chose it. Fix the rule for doing this in advance (preregister it if relevant), so the fine-tuning is not an unplanned analytic choice.

## 4. "Fixed" means actively maintained, not unattended

Even with a coil holder or arm, the coil drifts relative to the head over a session. Causes include head movement, the participant relaxing, the holder slipping, the EEG cap and electrodes compressing, and gel shifting. A few millimetres or degrees of drift can change TEP amplitude and shape, especially for a focal coil.

- **Monitor continuously.** Watch the neuronavigation readout throughout the session, not only at the start.
- **Set tolerances in advance.** Typical values are no more than about 2–3 mm displacement from the target and no more than about 2–5° in rotation or tilt. Pause and re-position when a value is exceeded.
- **Log the coil position for every pulse,** if your system allows it. You can then report the delivered accuracy and remove trials outside tolerance, using a rule fixed in advance.
- **Use pauses.** Re-check position at breaks between blocks. Make sure the coil returns to the same target, not just to "roughly the same place".
- **Consider coil heating.** In long sessions with high pulse counts, the coil may need to be cooled or swapped. Any swap means re-registering and re-verifying the position.
- **Robotic coil holders,** if available, automatically compensate for head movement and greatly reduce drift.

## 5. Recommended protocol

1. Map the DLPFC coordinate into each participant's MRI and check where it lands in the anatomy.
2. Optionally, run an individual E-field model to choose an orientation suited to the local gyral geometry.
3. Set up EEG, masking and spacer. Place the coil at the target and orientation.
4. Run rt-TEP checks. Within a pre-defined limited area, fine-tune position, orientation, tilt and intensity to get a clear early response with acceptable artifacts.
5. Lock in the final parameters and record them.
6. Keep that position throughout recording, with continuous navigation monitoring, threshold-based re-positioning, and pulse-by-pulse logging.
7. Report the target-versus-delivered deviation and any trials you excluded.

## Assumptions

- This is a single-pulse or low-rate TMS-EEG study measuring TEPs from left DLPFC with a figure-of-eight coil. Repetitive TMS with only offline outcomes would change some priorities, but drift monitoring still applies.
- "45°" means the usual handle orientation, posterior and about 45° from the midline.
- The coil is held by a mechanical arm, not a robot.
