# A large 5–10 ms deflection after DLPFC TMS: early TEP or artifact?

## Short answer

**Probably not, and I would not report it as "P5/N10".** A large biphasic deflection at about 5–10 ms that is strongest over frontotemporal channels after DLPFC stimulation fits a **TMS-evoked cranial muscle artifact** (temporalis and frontalis) much better than an early local cortical response. Other possibilities are electrode or amplifier decay artifacts. Until you have ruled these out, treat it as artifact, not as a named TEP component.

## Why it looks like muscle and not cortex

| Feature | What you describe | Genuine early local TEP | TMS-evoked muscle artifact |
|---|---|---|---|
| Latency | ~5–10 ms, biphasic | Onset within the first ~10–30 ms, but the first clear peaks are usually reported around 15–30 ms | Typically peaks ~5–15 ms; often biphasic or triphasic and decays by ~20–40 ms |
| Amplitude | "Large", visible in an online average | Small: roughly a few µV up to ~10–20 µV peak-to-peak | Tens to hundreds of µV, sometimes mV; often dwarfs everything else |
| Topography | Strongest over frontotemporal channels | Maximal near the target (e.g., F3/AF3/F5/FC3 for left DLPFC), spreading later | Maximal over the muscle (lateral frontal and temporal sites: F7/F8, FT7/FT9, T7, AF7), often with sharp polarity changes between neighbouring electrodes |
| Single trials | — | Not obvious on single trials; needs averaging | Usually clearly visible on single trials, with high-frequency content |
| Dependence on coil | — | Changes gradually with target and orientation | Changes strongly with small shifts in coil position, angle or orientation, and scales steeply with intensity |

DLPFC is a known problem site because the coil sits close to the temporalis and frontalis. Coils placed more laterally or anteriorly (e.g., F3/F5 or Beam F3), or oriented to drive current towards the temporal muscle, often produce exactly this pattern.

## Other artifacts to rule out

- **Decay or electrode-movement artifact.** A slow or exponential deflection after the pulse caused by electrode–gel polarisation, poor impedance or coil pressure on electrodes. It is usually maximal at the electrodes under or near the coil.
- **Pulse artifact or ringing from the amplifier.** With a TMS-compatible amplifier this should be over within ~2–5 ms. If it is longer, check the sampling rate, the hardware filter settings and whether the amplifier is saturating.
- **Recharge artifact.** It appears at a fixed latency that depends on stimulator settings. Check whether the 5–10 ms deflection is time-locked to a recharge delay.
- **Online display issues.** Online averages are usually unfiltered or minimally processed, without ICA, interpolation or baseline correction tailored to TMS. Some online-filter settings can smear the pulse artifact into a biphasic shape in the first ~10 ms.

## Practical checks you can do now

1. **Look at single trials** at the frontotemporal channels. Large, consistent, high-frequency bursts at 5–10 ms mean muscle artifact.
2. **Check the amplitude.** Anything above ~30–50 µV in the first 15 ms is very unlikely to be cortical.
3. **Look at the topography.** Is the maximum at the target electrodes or lateral, over the temporalis? A lateral maximum with steep local gradients points to muscle.
4. **Make small coil adjustments online.** Rotate the coil (e.g., a few tens of degrees), move it slightly medially or posteriorly, or tilt it. If the deflection drops sharply while the target stays nominally the same, it is muscle. Real-time TEP monitoring was developed to support this: you adjust position and orientation until the early response is free of muscle and decay artifacts while keeping a measurable early local response (e.g., the rt-TEP approach, Casarotto et al., 2022).
5. **Run an intensity series.** Muscle artifacts usually grow steeply and nonlinearly with intensity.
6. **Run sham or control conditions.** Use a sham coil or a spacer and an off-target control. Remember that these address sensory contamination at later latencies, not muscle artifacts, but they help separate the response from generic pulse effects.

## If you cannot avoid it during acquisition

Offline methods can attenuate muscle artifacts, for example ICA with dedicated TMS-muscle components, SOUND plus SSP-SIR, or the TESA muscle-component classification. But removing a very large muscle artifact also removes or distorts overlapping early cortical signals. Even after cleaning, you **cannot confidently interpret** activity in roughly the first 15–20 ms at the affected sites. Prevention at acquisition is far better than correction.

## About the label "P5/N10"

- "P5/N10" is not standard TEP nomenclature. TEP components are named by polarity and approximate latency, but for DLPFC and other sites the usually reported components start around **N15/P20–P25** and continue with N40/N45, P60, N100 and P180 (latencies vary by site and study).
- Giving a component name to a deflection that may be muscle or decay artifact would invite justified criticism from reviewers. It could also mislead readers into thinking you measured a very early cortical response.
- If, after the checks above, a small (a few µV), focal, artifact-free deflection remains near the target, you can describe it carefully as an early local response. Use neutral labels such as "early local peak-to-peak amplitude, 10–30 ms" or a latency-based name, and justify it with topography, single-trial inspection and dependence on coil position.

## Assumptions

- You use a TMS-compatible EEG amplifier with a standard 10–10 or 10–20 cap, and the "online average" is a quick average with little or no preprocessing.
- "Frontotemporal" means lateral sites such as F7/FT7/T7 (or the right-hemisphere equivalents), not the electrodes directly under the coil.
- Stimulation is single-pulse at a typical suprathreshold intensity (e.g., ~90–120% RMT or an equivalent E-field).

## Bottom line

A large biphasic signal at 5–10 ms, maximal over frontotemporal channels, is the classic signature of a TMS-evoked cranial muscle artifact (possibly mixed with decay artifact), not the early local cortical TEP. Don't report it as P5/N10. Check it on single trials, try adjusting coil orientation and position online to remove it, and report only an early local response that is small, focal, artifact-free and centred on the target.
